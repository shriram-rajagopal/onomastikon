The Hebrew of the medieval Jewish world, no one's daily speech but everyone's written language: the medium of the geonic academies of Babylonia, the poets and philosophers of al-Andalus, the travelers, merchants, and correspondents of a diaspora that ran from Córdoba to Baghdad and beyond. Post-biblical in grammar and hospitable to loans, it functioned as the international language of a civilization without a territory, and its geographical vocabulary is correspondingly its own, a mixture of biblical names reapplied to new places, contemporary names taken in from Arabic and Greek, and coinages found nowhere else. The atlas treats it as a stage in its own right beside Biblical Hebrew, on the model of the Byzantine Greek and Medieval Latin files.

Its onomastic habits reward attention. Medieval Hebrew rarely let a biblical name die: Sefarad, a single obscure toponym in Obadiah, became and remains the name of Spain; Bavel stretched to cover the Baghdad of the geonim; Edom, by a typological transfer this atlas notes but does not catalogue, came to mean Rome and Christendom entire. Beside these revivals it wrote down names no scripture knew, none more consequential than the Khazar king it called by his people's own name in the one exchange of letters that kingdom left behind. The homeland assigned here is Córdoba, the capital of the language's golden age and the address from which its most famous geographical letter was posted.
