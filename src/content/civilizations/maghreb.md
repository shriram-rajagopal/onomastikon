The Maghreb is North Africa west of Egypt as the Arabic geographers defined it: the coastal band and its hinterland from the Gulf of Sirte to the Atlantic, conquered in the second half of the seventh century and organized around the new metropolis of Kairouan, later around Fez, Marrakesh, and Tunis. The geographers subdivided it into a near, middle, and far west, *al-Maghrib al-Aqṣā* being roughly Morocco, and at its greatest extension the term embraced al-Andalus across the strait.

The name is a compass direction: *al-Maghrib*, "the west, the place of sunset," the mirror of the Mashriq, the east. It displaced two older namings that had worked from other vantages, the Greek habit of calling the continent's northern edge Libya and the Roman province-name Africa, each of which survives in other threads; what the Arabic name did differently was orient the region relative to the new center of the world in Arabia and Iraq. To be the Maghreb is to be defined as west of somewhere, and the region has carried that eastward-facing name for thirteen centuries, into the official name of modern Morocco, *al-Mamlaka al-Maghribiyya*, the western kingdom. Rome named the region for a tribe it conquered; the Arabs named it for where the sun went down from Baghdad.
