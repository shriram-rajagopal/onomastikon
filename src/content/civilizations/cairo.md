Cairo is a stack of cities at the point where the Nile valley opens into the Delta. At the bottom lies Babylon, the Roman fortress town described by Strabo, whose Coptic churches still stand; beside it the conquerors of 641 built Fustat, the garrison capital of Islamic Egypt; and north of both, in 969, the Fatimid general Jawhar laid out a new palace city for the caliph al-Muʿizz, al-Qāhira, "the Victorious," which grew to swallow its predecessors and become the largest city of the medieval Mediterranean world, seat of the Fatimids, Ayyubids, and Mamluks.

The page's names live at three depths, one per city in the stack. The Greek and Coptic *Babylōn* preserve the oldest layer, a Mesopotamian name stranded on the Nile by a legend no one could verify even in antiquity. *Al-Qāhira* is the newest and most deliberate, a Fatimid victory name with an astrological founding story attached. And between them sits the strangest of the three: *Miṣr*, the name of Egypt itself, applied to the capital, first to Fustat and then to Cairo, so that the city and the country share one name to this day. Most capitals borrow prestige from their country; Egypt's capital simply took the country's name whole, and an Egyptian going to Cairo has been "going to Miṣr" for thirteen centuries, as if the whole land had a front door.
