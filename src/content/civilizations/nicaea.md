Nicaea sits at the eastern end of Lake Ascania in Bithynia, a Hellenistic foundation of about 310 BCE, established by Antigonus and renamed by Lysimachus for his wife Nikaia. Its fame is conciliar: the councils of 325 and 787, the first and the last of the seven ecumenical councils, both met there, making the city's name permanent in the vocabulary of Christianity as the Nicene creed. After 1204 it had an imperial career as capital of the Byzantine government-in-exile, the empire of Nicaea, from which Constantinople was retaken in 1261; the Ottomans under Orhan captured it in 1331.

The name-chain is short and its last link is the page's payoff. *Níkaia*, a queen's name built on *níkē*, victory, passed to Latin untouched and lived fifteen centuries unchanged; Turkish then made *İznik* not from the name alone but from the Greek phrase *eis Níkaian*, "to Nicaea," the preposition fused onto the front and the ending worn away, exactly the process that turned *eis tḕn pólin* into Istanbul. The pair are the atlas's clearest demonstration that the Turks learned Bithynia's geography from Greek speech rather than Greek maps: what got borrowed was not the name but the answer to "where are you going?" A city of creeds ended inside a fossilized piece of conversation; İznik is Nicaea as spoken by someone on the road to it.
