The Crusader States were the four Latin polities planted in the Levant by the First Crusade, the county of Edessa, the principality of Antioch, the county of Tripoli, and the kingdom of Jerusalem, with Cyprus as their long-lived island neighbor. From 1098 they held, lost, and partly re-held the coast for two centuries, a Latin-ruled, French-speaking layer over Arabic, Syriac, Greek, and Armenian populations, until the Mamluk capture of Acre in 1291 ended the mainland states; their naming layer is the newest this atlas records, and the shortest-lived.

Their names are an essay in perspective. The settlers and their cousins at home named the states by distance: Medieval Latin *partes transmarinae*, "the lands across the sea," and its French twin *Outremer*, "overseas," a country defined, like Transoxiana, entirely by the water between the namer and the named; the difference is that here the residents themselves used the outsiders' term, colonists who went on calling their home "overseas" for two hundred years. The other side needed no new name at all: to the Arabic chroniclers the newcomers were simply *al-Faranj*, the Franks, an ethnonym whose thread belongs to the future page for Francia, and their coastal holdings were the *sāḥil*, the coast. The page stays developing for the deferred half of its own name: Old French *Outremer* awaits the language's arrival with Phase 5. A state that called itself "overseas" and was called "the foreigners" by its neighbors had, in a sense, no name of its own at all, and after 1291 there was nothing left to need one.
