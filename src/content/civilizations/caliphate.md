The empire of the Prophet's successors, from Abu Bakr's election in 632 to the Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258. One state in name and idea, it passed through the four Rashidun caliphs, the Umayyads of Damascus, and the Abbasids of Baghdad; this page treats the whole arc as a single thread, because the name did not change when the dynasty did. What the dynasties contested was possession of the *khilāfa*, the succession itself, and even after 1258 the title survived in a shadow line at Cairo. At its height in the eighth century the caliphate ran from the Atlantic coast of Iberia to the Oxus valley, the largest state the world had yet seen, and the pages for al-Andalus, Khurasan, and its great cities each carry a piece of it.

The naming pattern around it is the atlas's clearest case of neighbors naming a people by the tribe they met first. Persian called the Arabs *Tāzīg*, from the border tribe of Ṭayyiʾ; that word travelled the Silk Road to become Chinese *Dàshí* and crossed the mountains to become Armenian *Tačik*, so that from Chang'an to Dvin the caliphate wore the name of one pre-Islamic tribe. Greek and Syriac did the same thing with different tribes: the *Sarakēnoi* of Byzantine writers and the *Ṭayyāyē* of the Syriac chronicles are ethnonyms of the same kind, kept in prose here because they name the Arabs as a people rather than the state. Only the endonym names the thing itself, and what it names is not a land or a people at all but a claim: *al-Khilāfa*, the succession. Every neighbor named the caliphate for the Arabs nearest them; the caliphate named itself for the argument that defined it.
