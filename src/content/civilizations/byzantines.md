The eastern Roman empire ruled from Constantinople between Constantine's dedication of the city in 330 and its fall to the Ottomans in 1453. In those eleven centuries it never once called itself Byzantine: that word is a scholars' coinage, spread by Hieronymus Wolf's *Corpus historiae Byzantinae* of 1557, and built on the name of a city that had been renamed a thousand years before he wrote. The state's own vocabulary was Roman throughout. Its people were *Rhōmaîoi*, its polity the *basileía tōn Rhōmaíōn*, the empire of the Romans, and its land, in everyday speech, *Rhōmanía*. When the west collapsed in 476, nothing ended in the east; the empire simply had one capital left, and it kept the name along with the crown.

What is remarkable is that the east agreed. Arabic *al-Rūm*, Classical Persian *Rūm*, Armenian *Hoṙom*, Syriac *Bēth Rhōmāyē*, and Chinese *Fúlǐn* all descend from *Roma*, and all pointed at Constantinople: every language that faced the empire across a real border accepted that it was Rome. The dissent came from the west, where after 800 the Latin world had a rival claimant to the Roman title and took to calling the easterners Greeks, a name they did not use for themselves. The Islamic languages meanwhile split the Greek inheritance in two, filing the ancients under *al-Yūnān* on the page for Greece and the living empire under *al-Rūm* here, philosophy in one drawer and the frontier war in the other. The modern habit of saying "Byzantine" quietly sides with the western denial; the names gathered on this page preserve the opposite verdict, of the neighbors who watched the empire daily and never doubted whose it was.
