The Seljuks were the Oghuz Turkish house that entered the Islamic world from the steppe, took Nishapur in 1037 and Baghdad in 1055, and ruled the caliphate's eastern half as sultans while the caliph kept the pulpit. Their victory at Manzikert in 1071 opened Anatolia to Turkish settlement, and after the Great Seljuk state fragmented, the branch line at Konya, the Sultanate of Rūm, carried the name in Anatolia until 1308, presiding over the beyliks from which the Ottomans would rise. The page sits under the name-thread rule deliberately: the thread here is the eponym itself, *Saljūq*, the ancestor whose name the whole dispersed house carried, a dynasty-name promoted to a state-name by everyone who dealt with them.

The page's naming has a hole and an anachronism, and both are the story. The hole is the endonym: the Seljuks' own chancery wrote Persian and their Oghuz Turkish stayed oral, so the house is named for us by its Arabic and Persian historians, *Āl Saljūq* and *Saljūqiyān*, and no Turkish self-designation survives from the period to host. The anachronism is Byzantine: the Greek historians, trained to see the east through classical lenses, called the Seljuks *Pérsai*, Persians, dressing the newest power on their border in the oldest name they had for an eastern empire. Their own title told the truer joke in reverse, for the Konya sultans called their realm Rūm, Rome. On the Anatolian frontier of the eleventh century, the Turks said they ruled Rome and the Romans said they were fighting Persia; neither was right, and both names stuck for centuries.
