The Khazar qaghanate rose in the mid-seventh century from the wreck of the western Türk empire and held the Volga-Caspian steppe for three hundred years. It fought the caliphate to a standstill in the Caucasus, married a princess to a Byzantine emperor, taxed the river trade of eastern Europe from its capital at Atil, and, in the act that made it forever singular, its royal house adopted Judaism, the only steppe empire and the only medieval great power to do so. Kievan Rus broke the qaganate in the 960s, and its remnants dissolved into the peoples of the steppe within a century.

Its names ring the compass of its diplomacy: Greek *Cházaroi* in the historians and the imperial handbooks, Arabic *al-Khazar* in the geographers who also named the Caspian for them, Persian *Khazar* in the same tradition, Armenian *Xazirkʿ* in the historians of the Caucasus frontier, and Hebrew *Kūzār* in the qaghanate's own most famous document, the tenth-century correspondence between Ḥasdai ibn Shapruṭ of Córdoba and King Joseph. The Caspian pages of this atlas already carry the sea named for them; this page gathers the people. Their own Khazar language survives only in scattered names and titles, so even here the endonym thread runs through borrowed pens, the king of the Khazars writing his people's name in the alphabet of the faith they chose.
