Dilmun was the trade-land of the Persian Gulf, centered on the island of Bahrain with stations on Failaka and the facing Arabian coast, the middle port of the Bronze Age sea-route that joined Mesopotamia to the copper of Magan and the far goods of Meluhha. Its name enters the record at the very beginning of writing itself, in the archaic tablets of Uruk at the end of the fourth millennium, which makes it the first foreign land the written record knows. For the next two and a half thousand years it appears wherever Mesopotamian commerce looks south: Ur-Nanshe of Lagash has the ships of Dilmun bring him timber, Sargon of Akkad boasts that they moor at his quay of Agade beside those of Magan and Meluhha, and the Old Babylonian merchants of Ur sail to Dilmun for copper and pay in textiles and silver. Sargon II of Assyria still receives tribute from Uperi, king of Dilmun, whose home the annals place far off in the sea of the rising sun, and the land is last glimpsed in cuneiform under the Neo-Babylonian kings, after which the island drops from the record until the Greeks reach it under another name.

Dilmun's own language was never written, so the land is known entirely by what others called it, and its onomastic interest lies in how long a single thread can run. The Sumerian *Dilmun*, quite possibly the local name as the earliest scribes heard it, passed to Akkadian as *Tilmun* and held for two millennia; when Alexander's surveyors reached the island in the late fourth century BCE it surfaces in Greek as *Týlos*, which scholarship generally takes as the same old name in Greek dress; and the medieval Arabic *Awāl* then names the same archipelago for most of a millennium more. Dilmun also lived a second life inside Mesopotamian literature as a holy place: the pure land of the poem *Enki and Ninhursag*, and the far country where the sun rises in which the gods settled Ziusudra, the hero of the flood, to live forever. It is the only country on the Bronze Age map that was at once a real place with invoices and a paradise with an immortal in residence, and one ordinary commercial name had to do both jobs at once.
