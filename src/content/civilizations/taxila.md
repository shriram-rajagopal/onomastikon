Taxila commanded the routes where the roads from Bactria and Kabul come down to the Indus and turn toward the Gangetic plain, and nearly every power that entered India from the northwest held it in turn: the Achaemenids took it into the satrapy of Gandhāra, its king Taxiles submitted to Alexander in 326 BCE and feasted his army, the Mauryas governed the northwest from it, and Indo-Greeks, Sakas, Parthians, and Kushans each rebuilt it in their own fashion, the city migrating between three successive sites on the plain. In the Buddhist imagination it was the university town, where the Jātakas send princes and physicians to study; the Huns broke it in the fifth century CE and it never recovered.

Its names divide by register with unusual neatness. The Sanskrit *Takṣaśilā* and its Pali form *Takkasilā* carry a folk etymology, "hewn stone" or "the rock of Takṣa," which the Buddhist tradition retold as "the cut-off head" in a tale of the Bodhisattva's self-sacrifice, an explanation the Chinese pilgrim Faxian translated straight into his account of the site. The Greek *Táxila* that Alexander's historians fixed, and Latin took over, is a clipped soldiers' rendering of the local form. A Gāndhārī spelling in Kharoṣṭhī script is attested epigraphically in the region but its language is not yet in this atlas's inventory, a gap noted rather than papered over.
