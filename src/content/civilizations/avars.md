The Avars arrived on the Pontic steppe in 558, fugitives, by Byzantine report, from the new Türk qaghanate rising in the east, and within a decade they had built a qaghanate of their own on the Hungarian plain over subject Slavs, Gepids, and Bulgars. For two generations they were the scourge of the Balkans, extracting enormous tribute from Constantinople and joining Persia in the great siege of 626, whose failure broke their prestige; a diminished but wealthy qaghanate endured until Charlemagne's armies plundered its ring-fortress in the 790s, and the last Avar polity dissolved in the 820s. Who they had been before 558 is disputed, for Byzantine writers doubted their claim to the name of the mighty Rouran and dubbed them Pseudo-Avars, an ancient controversy the entries note without adjudicating.

The name is recorded from three directions: the Greek *Ábaroi* of the historians who paid them, the Latin *Avari* of the annalists who destroyed them, and, if the standard reading holds, the *Apar* of the Orkhon inscriptions, the steppe's own memory of them in the mourner-list of Kül Tegin's funeral, a form this atlas holds as a documented gap until its runiform spelling can be verified against an edition. The endonym is otherwise silent, as with most of the steppe empires of this atlas: the Avars are named entirely by their tributaries, their conquerors, and their supplanters.
