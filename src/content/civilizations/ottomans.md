The Ottomans began around 1299 as one Turkish frontier principality among many, raiding Byzantine Bithynia under a bey named Osman. Within a century his house had taken Bursa, crossed into Europe, made Edirne its capital, and reduced the Byzantine empire to a city; the interregnum after Timur's victory at Ankara in 1402 nearly ended it, and the recovery under Mehmed I and Murad II set up the event this atlas closes on, Mehmed II's capture of Constantinople on 29 May 1453. The state continued for another four and a half centuries, but past the wall of 1453 it belongs to other books; here it is the closing bracket, the power that ended the Roman thread this atlas has followed since the Tiber.

The names divide by vantage with unusual neatness. The dynasty's own style was *Âl-i ʿOsmân*, the House of Osman, a family name where older empires put a people or a land; the Arabic chanceries agreed, corresponding with *Banū ʿUthmān*, the sons of ʿUthmān. The Byzantines, who had the most at stake, refused the family the compliment and called the power simply *hoi Toûrkoi*, the Turks; and Persian letters, with the deepest irony available, filed the Ottomans under *Rūm*, so that the sultan who conquered the Romans' city was already, in Persian, a ruler of Rome. A dynasty named for one man, an enemy named for a people, a domain named for the empire it was swallowing: the Ottomans close the atlas carrying all three, and in 1453 the third one stopped being a metaphor.
