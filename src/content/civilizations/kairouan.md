Kairouan was founded around 670 by the conqueror ʿUqba ibn Nāfiʿ as the permanent camp of the Arab armies in Ifriqiya, sited deliberately inland, away from Byzantine sea power. It became the metropolis of the Islamic west for four centuries, seat of the Aghlabid emirs, first capital of the Fatimids, and home of the Great Mosque whose form echoed across North Africa and into al-Andalus; after the Hilalian sack of 1057 its primacy passed to Tunis, but it remained, and remains, the holy city of the Maghreb.

Its name is a single word with a surprising passport. *Al-Qayrawān* is the Persian *kārwān*, "caravan," in Arabic dress, used in the conquest period for a military column and its camp; the first Islamic city of Africa, founded by Arab armies at the far western end of the caliphate, thus bears a Persian name, carried the whole width of the empire in the vocabulary of its soldiers. The word later returned to Europe twice by different doors, as "caravan" through the eastern trade and as this city's name through Africa. On an atlas where Persian names ride east along the Silk Road to China, Kairouan is the counterexample that proves the network: the same Persian lexicon rode west with the armies, and its farthest western deposit became a holy city. The east named the west's first metropolis, and neither party was Greek or Roman; a new world was naming itself.
