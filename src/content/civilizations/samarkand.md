Samarkand commands the middle Zarafshan valley from the plateau of Afrasiab, the royal city of ancient Sogdiana and the eastern anchor of Transoxiana. Alexander took it in 329 BCE and killed Cleitus there at a banquet; the Sogdian merchant princes made it the head of their trading world; the Arab conquest took it around 712, and papermaking is said to have entered the Islamic world through its Chinese prisoners after Talas. Sacked by the Mongols in 1220, it rose again as Timur's imperial capital at the very end of this atlas's span, the one city of the cluster whose fourteenth-century glory outshone its ninth.

The page traces a single local name across five scripts and seventeen centuries. Sogdian *smʾrknδh*, conventionally read *Smārkanθ* and usually explained from *asmara-* "stone" and *kanθ* "town," the stone fort, appears in the merchants' own Ancient Letters and the Mugh documents; Greek *Marákanda* is the same name as Alexander's historians heard it, with the first syllable worn off; Arabic and Persian *Samarqand* carry it entire into the medieval world; and the Tang annals, cataloguing the Sogdian states by surname, filed the city as *Kāng*, chief of the nine clans of Zhaowu. Unlike its sister Bukhara, whose name resists analysis, Samarkand's parses cleanly, and what it says is exactly what Alexander found and Timur rebuilt: the stone town, durable by name and by nature, the one label in Transoxiana that never needed replacing.
