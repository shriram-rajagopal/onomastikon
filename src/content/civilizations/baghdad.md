Baghdad was founded in 762 by the caliph al-Manṣūr as the planned round capital of the Abbasid caliphate, sited on the west bank of the Tigris where the two rivers draw closest together. Within a century it had become the largest city in the world outside China, the seat of the caliphs and of the translation movement that carried Greek philosophy into Arabic. The Mongol sack of 1258 ended the Abbasid city and the caliphate itself, though the city continued under Ilkhanid and later rule to the end of this atlas's span.

The founding created a rare thing, a city with a birth certificate in two names, and the wrong one won. Al-Manṣūr's official name was *Madīnat al-Salām*, the City of Peace, and it held the coinage and the chancery for centuries; but the site was older than the caliph, a Sasanian village whose Iranian name, *Baghdād*, "given by God," appears in the Babylonian Talmud generations before Islam, and it was that village name the world kept. The pattern runs across this page: the Jewish Aramaic *Baḡdāṯā* preserves the name's pre-Islamic career, and the Chinese *Báidá* of the Song gazetteers carries the vernacular form, not the official one, to the far end of Asia. One documented gap remains, the Syriac form used by the chronicles under Abbasid rule, whose exact orthography has not yet been verified from reachable sources. A caliph could plan the round city, wall it, and name it for paradise; he could not make anyone say the name.
