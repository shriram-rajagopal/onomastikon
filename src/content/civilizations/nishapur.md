Nishapur lies under the Binalud mountains in western Khurasan, one of the four quarter-capitals of the province with Merv, Herat, and Balkh. Founded, as its name records, by or for the Sasanian king Shapur, it outgrew its royal beginnings under the caliphate: Tahirid capital, Samanid and Ghaznavid metropolis, one of the largest cities of the eleventh-century world and among its greatest producers of scholars, Omar Khayyam and the poet ʿAṭṭār first among them, and of the glazed ceramics that carry its name through the world's museums. Earthquakes and the Mongol sack of 1221 broke it; the modern Iranian city continues the site and the name.

The name is a royal dedication that the centuries quietly democratized. Middle Persian *Nēw-Šābuhr*, "fair is Shapur" or "the good city of Shapur," belongs to the Sasanian habit of stamping the founder-king's name onto foundations, a practice this atlas meets from Alexandria to Constantinople; Arabic *Naysābūr* and Persian *Nīshāpūr* carried it on with the king's name embedded but no longer heard, a fossil syllable in an ordinary city-name. By the era of its glory nobody saying Nishapur was praising a third-century king, any more than a modern speaker praises Constantine; the entry's chain shows a proper name decomposing into pure address. Kings name cities to be remembered, and the cities comply by forgetting: Shapur's praise-name survives to this day, minus the praise, minus the king.
