The Alans emerge in the first century CE as the new masters of the Sarmatian steppe, raiders through the Caucasus into Parthia and Cappadocia, cavalry famed from Rome to China. The Hunnic storm of the 370s broke their steppe power and scattered them: some rode west with the Vandals through Gaul and Spain to Carthage, where the Vandal kings styled themselves kings of the Vandals and Alans, while others held the central Caucasus, built the medieval kingdom of Alania, took Byzantine Christianity around 900, and endured until the Mongol invasions of the 1230s. Their Iranian speech survives in the Ossetes of the Caucasus, the one living language descended from the ancient steppe of this atlas's pages.

The name is a genealogy in itself: *Alan* continues the Iranian *aryāna*, the same "Aryan" that underlies the *Ērān* on Iran's page, so that the westernmost steppe nomads and the Persian empire carried one self-designation to opposite ends of the ancient world. Around it the records gather from every direction, the Latin *Alani* of the historians who watched them cross into the empire, the Greek *Alanoí* first fixed by Josephus, the Armenian *Alankʿ* of the neighbors who fought and married them, the Arabic *al-Lān* of the geographers of the Caucasus roads, and the Chinese *Ālánliáo*, the Han registry's note that the old Yǎncài had taken a new name, the same consolidation the western sources record as the rise of the Alans. Their kingdom's fall to the Mongols closes this page's window; their descendants' republic keeps the name Alania on the modern map. One form is a documented gap rather than an entry: Šābuhr's trilingual inscription bounds the empire at the Gate of the Alans, a Middle Persian *Alānān* awaiting verification of its consonantal spelling before it can join the page.
