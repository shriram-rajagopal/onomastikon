Kush is the atlas's name for the sequence of kingdoms on the middle Nile, in what is now Sudan, that faced Egypt across the cataracts for most of recorded antiquity. The Kerma culture (c. 2500 to 1500 BCE) was the power the Middle Kingdom's fortresses were built to watch; the New Kingdom conquered it and ruled the south through a viceroy titled the King's Son of Kush; the Napatan kingdom that rose from the province in the eighth century BCE conquered Egypt in turn and ruled it as the Twenty-fifth Dynasty, Piye and Shabaka and Taharqa, until the Assyrians drove it back south; and the Meroitic kingdom, its royal center moved to Meroë above the Fifth Cataract, lasted into the fourth century CE, with its own alphabet, its iron, its ruling queens, and more pyramids than Egypt ever built. Ezana of Aksum, campaigning on the Nile around 350, recorded the ruin of its towns.

The kingdom's own name for itself is, for now, beyond this page. Meroitic, written in its own script from about 200 BCE, calls the land *qes* in Claude Rilly's reading, and that form is held here as a documented gap until an edition-verified spelling can be set in Meroitic letters. Every name on the page is therefore an outsider's, and they run in three threads. The first is Egyptian: *Kꜣš*, written by the Middle Kingdom's fortress-builders, usually with the epithet "wretched," went downstream into every Near Eastern record, Akkadian *Kūsu* in the annals of the Assyrians who fought Taharqa, Hebrew *Kûš* in Genesis and Isaiah, Old Persian *Kūšā* at the end of Darius's lists, and from the Hebrew Bible into the Peshitta and the Talmud. The second is Greek: the Greeks did not borrow the Egyptian word but described what they saw, *Aithiopía*, the land of the burnt-faced, a name Homer and Herodotus fixed on the people beyond Egypt, which the Septuagint and the Vulgate then wrote wherever the Hebrew said Kush, and which later crossed the Red Sea to Aksum, where it remains. The third is the name of the successors: the *Noûbai* whom Eratosthenes placed beside Meroë and the *Nobā* whom Ezana fought in its ruins, the Nubia that outlived Kush and replaced it on the map.

A kingdom known to the world by its northern neighbor's word, to the Greeks by their own description, and to posterity by the name of the people who took its place: the page is the record of a name that everyone used except the people it named.
