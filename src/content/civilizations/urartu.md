Urartu was the kingdom that ruled the Armenian highland from the ninth to the early sixth century BCE, centered on Tušpa, modern Van, on the eastern shore of the lake that still bears a form of its own name. At its height under kings such as Argišti I and Sarduri II it was the most powerful state of the northern mountains and Assyria's chief rival, contesting the upper Euphrates and the trade of the Iranian frontier until the Assyrians, and then the Medes, broke it. Its people were not Armenians and spoke no Indo-European tongue: Urartian was a relative of Hurrian, and the Armenians who later gave the highland its enduring name arrived only after the kingdom had fallen.

The kingdom is a study in the gap between what a state calls itself and what its neighbors call it. To the Assyrians it was *Urarṭu*, a name they had used for the highland since the thirteenth century, and it is the Assyrian form that travels: into the Achaemenid record, reused to render the new province of Armenia, and into the Hebrew Bible as *Araraṭ*, the northern land of Jeremiah's prophecy and the mountains where the ark comes to rest. To itself, in its own royal inscriptions, the kingdom was *Biainili*, "the lands of Biaina," the country around Lake Van. The endonym is barely known outside the inscriptions; the exonym became the name of a mountain, a province, and a verse of scripture.
