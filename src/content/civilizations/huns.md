The Huns crossed the Volga in the 370s, shattered the Alans and the Gothic kingdoms in a decade, and set in motion the avalanche of displaced peoples that Rome experienced as the beginning of its end. For eighty years they ruled a tribute-taking empire from the Hungarian plain, at its height under Attila the terror of both Roman capitals, and within twenty years of his death in 453 the empire had dissolved into the peoples it had swallowed. They wrote nothing; a handful of names and words survive in other languages, and their own affiliation, Turkic, Iranian, mixed, or unknowable, remains open.

The name outweighs the empire. Eight centuries before Attila, Zoroastrian scripture knew enemies called *Xiiaona*, and Sasanian Persia called its fourth-century steppe invaders *Xyōn*, Chionites; a Sogdian merchant's letter of 313 calls the sackers of Chinese capitals *xwn*; India named the invaders of the Gupta world *Hūṇa*; and the Greek and Latin of the West wrote *Oûnnoi* and *Hunni* for Attila's people. Whether all of these are one name, and whether one name means one people descended from the Xiongnu of the Chinese frontier, is the longest-running identification dispute in steppe history, and this page deliberately holds its pieces: each entry records where its form certainly points, and the connections between them are argued in the entries rather than assumed by the page. On no other entity in this atlas does so much history hang on whether similar-sounding words in five languages are in fact the same word.
