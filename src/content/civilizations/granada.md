Granada climbs the foothills where the Sierra Nevada meets the fertile plain of the Genil, the last capital of Islamic Iberia. The site is ancient: the Iberian and Roman town of Iliberri, host of the church council of Elvira around 300, stood on or beside the Albaicín hill. The city that eclipsed it took shape in 1013, when the Zirid emirs moved the population of the declining Elvira to the defensible hill-site called Gharnāṭa; from 1238 the Naṣrids ruled their reduced but brilliant emirate from it, building the Alhambra above the town, and their state alone carried al-Andalus past this atlas's closing date, falling only in 1492.

The page holds two names because the site led two lives. *Iliberri* is pre-Roman, commonly connected with an Iberian or Basque-type phrase for "new town," which would make it a distant cousin of every Neapolis on the map; the identification of its site with Granada's old quarter, long argued, is now generally accepted. *Gharnāṭa* is as disputed as *al-Andalus* itself: medieval and modern guesses run from a Romance "pomegranate" (the fruit is still the city's arms) to "hill of strangers," and none convinces. A city of two unexplained names, one from before Rome and one from after it, and between them the whole history of the peninsula; Granada begins as somebody's "new town" and ends, a millennium and a half later, as the last old city of a vanished country.
