The Caspian is the largest inland body of water on earth, shut off from every ocean, lying between the Caucasus, the Iranian plateau, and the steppe. The ancient geographers were never sure what it was: most, following Alexander's surveyors, took it for a deep gulf of the northern Ocean, and only gradually was it understood to be enclosed. Its southern shore was the rich Iranian province of Hyrcania, its western the lands of the Caucasus, and its trade and fisheries supported the peoples all around it.

Belonging to no single power, the sea was named by each shore in turn for whoever lived there. The Greeks called it the *Kaspía Thálassa* after the Caspii of its western coast, or the *Hyrkanía Thálassa* after the province to its south; the Romans took both as *Mare Caspium* and *Mare Hyrcanum*, and the Armenians, who had ruled the Caspii's old country as a province, declined the same people's name in their own grammar as *Kaspicʿ cov*. When the Turkic Khazars held its northern steppe, the medieval Arabs called it *Baḥr al-Khazar*, the "Sea of the Khazars," beside *Baḥr Jurjān*, their form of Hyrcania, and the Persians *Daryā-yi Khazarān*, a name Iran still uses; the Syriac scholars, following the geographers' newer habit of naming by city, knew it as *yammā d-Qazbīn*, the Sea of Qazvin. Only the Zoroastrian *Bundahišn* stands apart, listing the sea in its inventory of creation as the *Kamrōd*, with no eponym at all. A sea too large and too shared to have an owner thus carried a rotating series of names, each the label of whichever people stood on its rim when the namers passed.
