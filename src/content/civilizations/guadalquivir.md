The Guadalquivir drains the trough between the Sierra Morena and the Andalusian cordillera, watering the richest lowland of Iberia on its way past Córdoba and Seville to the Atlantic. Its valley carried the peninsula's wealth in every era: the silver of Tartessos, the olive oil of Roman Baetica, whose very name is the river's, and the garden landscape of al-Andalus, whose two greatest cities stand on its banks.

The river is the atlas's plainest demonstration that the modern map of Spain speaks Arabic. The ancients knew it as *Baetis*, a pre-Roman name of unknown meaning, with a memory preserved by Strabo that it had once been called Tartessos, the river of the fabled kingdom at its mouth; Rome named a province for it. The conquerors of 711 called it simply *al-Wādī al-Kabīr*, "the great river," an ordinary descriptive phrase rather than a name at all; and it is that phrase, not the ancient name, that Castilian fossilized as *Guadalquivir*. The pattern repeats across dozens of Iberian rivers whose names begin in Guad-, but nowhere with such effect: the ancient name, older than Rome, was simply let go, and what the reconquest kept forever was five Arabic syllables of plain description. Baetis had a name; the Guadalquivir has a sentence, and the sentence won.
