Seville is the river port of the lower Guadalquivir, the last point ocean shipping could reach, which made it the outlet of southern Iberia's wealth in every period. Roman Hispalis was a conventus capital and colonia beside which the emperors Trajan and Hadrian were born at neighboring Italica; the Visigoths made it briefly their capital and it was the city of Isidore, whose *Etymologies* closed antiquity's learning into one book. Under Islam Išbīliya was second only to Córdoba, capital of the greatest taifa kingdom and then of the Almohads, whose Giralda still stands; Castile took it in 1248 and made it a royal seat.

The name-chain on this page runs unbroken from a lost beginning to the modern map. *Hispalis* is pre-Roman and unexplained, possibly Phoenician; Isidore, the city's own bishop, could only offer the folk-guess that it stood on swampy pilings. Arabic reshaped the Romance pronunciation into *Išbīliya*, and Castilian *Sevilla* descends from the Arabic form, the initial consonant and the liquids showing the Arabic mediation plainly. Modern Seville is thus a Latin name that reaches the present only by having passed through Arabic, the exact westernmost counterpart of the eastern chains this atlas traces through Persian. The city's conquerors each kept the name they found; what survives today is the sum of all their accents.
