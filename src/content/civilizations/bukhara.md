Bukhara sits in the lower Zarafshan valley, the western anchor of Transoxiana, an old Sogdian oasis town that the Arab conquest took around 709. Its great age came under the Samanids, who ruled their empire from it in the ninth and tenth centuries: the Bukhara of Rudaki and the young Avicenna, of the vizier-scholars whose library dazzled its visitors, and above all of the court where, two centuries after the conquest, poets began writing Persian again, in Arabic script, and founded the literature that runs through Ferdowsi to Hafez. It remained a city of learning and trade under Qarakhanids and Khwarazmshahs, was sacked by the Mongols in 1220, and revived as a holy city of the later Turkic world.

The name's origin is disputed in a specifically Bukharan way: the old derivation from Sanskrit *vihāra*, "Buddhist monastery," would make the city's name a relic of pre-Islamic religion, but the word is more likely Sogdian, and no reading has won. The Sogdian form itself, attested on the coinage of the pre-Islamic Bukhar Khudah rulers, is the page's documented gap, held until its orthography can be verified. Meanwhile the Tang annals catalogued the city as the *An* state, one syllable in the Chinese system that filed each Sogdian city-state under a surname. The city that rescued the Persian language carries a name Persian cannot explain; the words it saved are clear, and the word it answers to is not.
