Al-Andalus is Islamic Iberia: the polity, and then the shrinking family of polities, that began with the crossing of 711 and ended with the emirate of Granada, whose final decades close this atlas's span. Under the Umayyad emirs and caliphs of Córdoba it became the most urbanized and lettered society in western Europe; after the caliphate's collapse in 1031 the name held steady over taifa kings, Almoravids, Almohads, and Naṣrids alike, denoting whatever part of the peninsula remained under Islamic rule, a referent that contracted for seven centuries without the name ever changing.

The page is deliberately endonym-led, in the manner of Minoan Crete, because the name itself is the puzzle. *Al-Andalus* appears within five years of the conquest and answers to no known language: the derivations on offer reach from the Vandals to a Gothic term for allotted land to an Atlantic word, and none has prevailed. The neighbors add little, which is itself telling; Latin Christendom went on calling the peninsula Hispania, Persian and Hebrew writers used the Arabic name or their own inherited ones, so the new thread lived almost entirely inside Arabic. Its afterlife, though, is in plain sight: when the last Muslim territory was reduced to the southern coast, the name contracted with it, and modern Andalucía preserves *al-Andalus* as a Spanish region's name, the conquest's word surviving its own reconquest. Spain kept the name of the thing it spent seven centuries removing.
