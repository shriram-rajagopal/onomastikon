The Hephthalites rose in the mid-fifth century to rule Bactria and the mountain marches between the Iranian and Indian worlds. For a century they were the terror of both: they defeated the Sasanians repeatedly, killed the king of kings Pērōz in 484 and took his son as hostage, made Persia a tribute-payer, and pushed into Gandhara and the Gupta northwest. In 560 or thereabouts the empire was broken between the two powers it had humiliated, partitioned by the new Türk qaghanate and a resurgent Ḵosrow I, and its remnants lingered as princelings in the Afghan valleys until the Arab conquests.

The record calls them, in Prokopios's famous phrase, the White Huns, Hunnic in name but unlike the Huns of Europe in every visible habit, city-keeping, law-abiding, and ruled by one king. Their name traveled into every neighboring literature: Greek *Ephthalîtai*, the Chinese court's *Yàndā*, the Armenian historians' *Hepʿtʿałkʿ*, and in the Islamic centuries the Arabic *Hayāṭila* and Persian *Hayṭāl*, all clipping the same dynastic name, plausibly that of a royal clan, to local shape. Their own coins speak Bactrian, a language not yet in this atlas's inventory, and write *ēbodalo*; the page's documented gap is thus the endonym itself, waiting on a Bactrian language file, and the Middle Persian and Sogdian forms likewise await verified spellings.
