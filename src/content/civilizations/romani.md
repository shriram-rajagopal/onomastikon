The Romani are the atlas's great migration in its final act: an Indo-Aryan people whose language left India around the turn of the millennium and crossed Persia, Armenia, and the Byzantine world, reaching Constantinople by the mid-eleventh century and Western Europe by the 1420s, when chronicle after chronicle records their companies arriving under voivodes and self-styled dukes. They carried no state, held no territory, and wrote nothing down; everything the written record preserves of them before 1453 is in other peoples' languages, and their range on the map is an envelope of attestations, not a border.

That situation makes their page an inventory of pure exonyms, each one wrong in an instructive way. Their own name, *Rom*, descends from the Sanskrit *ḍomba* of the Indian musician castes but goes unwritten until a century past this atlas's edge, so the endonym is held and the record speaks instead: Byzantium transferred the name of a dead heretical sect and produced *Atsínganoi*, the ancestor of Tsigane, Zigeuner, and the Latin *Cigani* of Sigismund's safe-conduct; Persia folded them into the legend of Bahram Gur's imported musicians as the *Lūlī*; the Arabs filed the Indian migrants of the Iraq marshes as *al-Zuṭṭ*; and Western Europe, hearing a claim of penance and pilgrimage from Little Egypt, called them Egyptians, the error that became Gypsy and Gitano. Several of these names hardened into slurs, and the people they mislabeled now go in English by the name the medieval record never once wrote down. A people who crossed more of this atlas than any other are its only entity named entirely by bystanders; every name on this page is a description of the Romani by someone watching them pass.
