Mecca is the sanctuary town of the western Arabian highlands, built around the Kaaba and its well in a dry valley off the incense road. By the sixth century it was the town of the Quraysh, a place of pilgrimage and truce months whose sanctuary predated the faith that would make it the axis of a world religion; after 630 it became the qibla and pilgrimage center of Islam, ruled in turn from Medina, Damascus, Baghdad, and Cairo while remaining the one city every Muslim faced in prayer.

Its onomastic record is strangely thin for so central a place, and the thinness is the story. The name *Makka* has no agreed etymology, and the Qur'an itself attests it in two shapes, *Makka* and *Bakka*, a doublet the commentators have worked at for centuries. Before Islam the record nearly vanishes: the one claimed ancient attestation, Ptolemy's inland town of *Makoraba*, has been read as Mecca since Renaissance geography, yet the identification now stands disputed to the point of collapse, which would leave the city with no securely attested name at all before late antiquity. At the far end of the span the Song gazetteer *Zhufanzhi* records *Májiā*, faithfully carrying the name to China inside a Buddhist frame. The page stays developing while a possible Byzantine Greek form is checked. Mecca is the rare city more securely attested in heaven than on the ground: its name enters the durable record with scripture, and everything earlier is conjecture.
