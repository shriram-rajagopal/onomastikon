Medina is the great oasis of the northern Hijaz, a well-watered cluster of palm groves and settlements on the caravan road between Yemen and Syria. Under its old name Yathrib it enters the record astonishingly early: the last Babylonian king, Nabonidus, lists it among the Arabian oases he controlled during his ten-year sojourn at Tayma in the 550s BCE. In 622 it became the destination of the hijra and the seat of the Prophet and the first caliphs, the capital of the nascent Islamic state until Ali moved to Kufa, and thereafter the second sanctuary of Islam.

The page's names divide cleanly at the hijra, and the division was deliberate. *Yathrib* is among the oldest continuously attested city-names in Arabia, running from cuneiform *Iatribu* through Ptolemy's Greek *Iathrippa* to the Qur'an's own single mention; yet the new faith retired it, preferring *al-Madīna*, "the City," clipped from "the city of the Prophet." The renaming took: the old name survives today only in recitation of the one verse that preserves it. That gives this page both of the atlas's great naming events in miniature, an ancient name with a two-thousand-year paper trail, and a young name that is barely a name at all, just the definite article and the common noun for "city," raised to a proper name by what happened there. Medina and Constantinople, the two holy capitals of this atlas's later centuries, arrived at the same solution from opposite directions: when a city becomes singular enough, its name simplifies to "the City."
