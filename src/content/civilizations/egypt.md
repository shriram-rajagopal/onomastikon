One of the longest continuously documented civilizations in human history, Egypt emerged with the political unification of Upper and Lower Egypt around 3100 BCE and persisted as a recognizable cultural and political entity through pharaonic, Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman rule before the gradual transitions of Late Antiquity. The civilization was structured along the Nile valley and the Delta, sustained by the annual inundation that deposited the fertile black silt from which the Egyptians took their self-designation *Kemet*, "the Black Land," in deliberate contrast with *Deshret*, "the Red Land" of the surrounding desert.

Egypt's three-millennium engagement with its neighbors has left it named in more ancient languages than perhaps any other entity in this atlas, and the names fall into three great traditions that never merged. At home, *Kemet* descended through the language's own stages, Demotic *Kmy* to Coptic *Kēme*, the self-name aging along with the civilization. To the east, the Semitic root *mṣr*, "border, frontier," ran from Akkadian *Miṣru* through Hebrew, Ugaritic, and Aramaic into Arabic *Miṣr*, the name Egypt bears to this day, and at its furthest reach into a Song customs ledger as *Wùsīlí*. To the north, Greek *Aígyptos*, most likely from *Hikuptah*, "the estate of the soul of Ptah," a temple name of Memphis, passed through Latin into the modern European names, so that English "Egypt" quietly preserves the name of a single temple in a city the pharaohs ruled.
