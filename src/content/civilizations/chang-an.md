Chang'an was the capital of the Han dynasty, founded by its first emperor in 202 BCE in the Wei valley, the enclosed heartland from which Qin had unified China. For two centuries it was one of the largest cities on earth, the seat of the court, the terminus of the routes west that Han envoys opened toward Central Asia, and the eastern anchor of what would be called the Silk Road; after the Han moved their capital east to Luoyang it remained the great city of the west, repeatedly a capital again under the successor dynasties. The Sui abandoned the worn Han site in 583 CE for a new city built just southeast, which as Tang Chang'an would carry the name to its medieval zenith; modern Xi'an overlies it.

The city's names split cleanly between what it called itself and what the road called it. *Cháng'ān*, "perpetual peace," is a programmatic imperial name of a familiar Chinese type, a wish inscribed on a map. The merchants who actually traveled the road used another name entirely: the Sogdian Ancient Letters, written by traders in Gansu around 313 CE, call the city *Khumdān*, a form of disputed origin that then echoes for centuries wherever the road's peoples spoke of the place, surfacing in Byzantine Greek as *Khoubdán*. Empire named the city in Chinese; the road named it in Iranian; both names meant the same walls.
