Konya commands the southern edge of the central Anatolian plateau, an old Phrygian and then Greek city that Xenophon's Ten Thousand passed as the last town of Phrygia. As Iconium it was a Roman colony and an early stage of Paul's journeys; as Qūniya it became, from the early twelfth century, the capital of the Seljuk Sultanate of Rūm, and in the thirteenth its court drew the refugees of the Mongol catastrophe, among them the Persian-speaking family from Balkh whose son became Mawlānā Rūmī, "the Roman," the Sufi poet whose shrine still centers the city. With the sultanate's end the city passed through the Karamanid beylik to the Ottomans in this atlas's final decades.

The page carries the Seljuk chapter of the atlas, and its name-chain shows what that chapter did and did not change. *Ikónion* passed to Latin unaltered, to Arabic as *Qūniya* with the familiar hardening to *qāf*, and to Turkish as *Konya*, four shapes of one pre-Greek name that no conqueror in eighteen centuries bothered to replace; the Seljuks, who renamed nothing, instead renamed themselves by their address, styling their realm Rūm. The city's greatest export makes the point better than any grammar: a poet is called Rūmī, the Roman, because he lived in Konya under Turkish sultans of Rome, three civilizations deep in borrowed names. Konya never had to change its name; the names of everyone who held it changed around it.
