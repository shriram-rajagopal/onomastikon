Toledo stands on a granite hill nearly encircled by the gorge of the Tagus, the natural fortress of central Iberia. Livy records its capture in 193 BCE with the remark that it was then "a small city, but strong by position"; the position kept mattering. The Visigoths made Toletum their royal and ecclesiastical capital, the city of the councils that governed their church and kingdom; Arabic Ṭulayṭula was the great frontier city of the Middle March; and its fall to Castile in 1085, the first major Islamic capital lost, shook al-Andalus enough to summon the Almoravids from Africa.

What gives the city its singular place in the atlas's story is what happened after that fall. Castilian Toledo inherited libraries, and through the twelfth and thirteenth centuries its translators, working Jew beside Christian, turned Arabic Aristotle, Ptolemy, and Avicenna into the Latin that fed the new universities of Europe; the name *Ṭulayṭula* stayed alive on Arabic lips describing a city now producing Latin books from Arabic ones. The name itself made the same crossing as the learning, Latin *Toletum* to Greek *Tṓlēton* to Arabic *Ṭulayṭula* and back into Romance as *Toledo*. Other cities on this atlas passed knowledge along a road; Toledo is where the whole library changed languages, in a city whose own name had already done it twice.
