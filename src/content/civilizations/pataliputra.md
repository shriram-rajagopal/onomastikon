Pataliputra was the capital of ancient India's greatest empires, set on the south bank of the Ganges at its confluence with the Son, in Magadha. The Buddhist canon watches it being born: the *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta* has the Buddha pass through the village of Pāṭaligāma while the ministers of Magadha fortify it against the Vajji confederacy, and prophesy the greatness of the city to come. Under the Mauryas it became the seat of Candragupta and Aśoka and, by the reckoning of the Greek ambassador Megasthenes, the largest city in India, a walled parallelogram along the river with five hundred and seventy towers; it served the Guptas as capital again in the fourth and fifth centuries CE before its slow decline. Modern Patna sits on the site, its name a worn-down descendant of the ancient one.

The names on this page all descend from one stem and still manage to tell the city's whole story. The Sanskrit *Pāṭaliputra* and Pali *Pāṭaliputta*, "son of the trumpet-flower tree," give the native form in court and canonical registers; the Greek *Palíbothra* is the same word as Megasthenes' embassy heard it pronounced, and Latin carried the Greek form to Pliny's geography; the Chinese *Bāliánfú* is the pilgrim Faxian's transcription, made when he reached the city around 400 CE and found the Mauryan palace still standing, work, he wrote, that no human hands could have made. One vernacular name, four scripts, and the three great routes of contact, embassy, empire, and pilgrimage, each carrying it a different way.
