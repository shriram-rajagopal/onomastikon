The Sarmatians were Iranian-speaking nomad confederations of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, first reported by Herodotus as the Sauromatians east of the Don, the Scythians' neighbors and, in his telling, the sons of Scythian fathers and Amazon mothers. From the fourth century BCE the tribes later called Sarmatian, the Iazyges, Roxolani, Aorsi, and Alans among them, moved west across the Scythian lands, and by the Roman imperial centuries they held the steppe from the Hungarian plain to the Caspian, pressing on the Danube frontier as soldiers, raiders, and uneasy federates until the Hunnic storm of the late fourth century broke the order they had built.

Their names in other people's records trace that long displacement. Greek wrote the name twice, *Sauromátai* for Herodotus's easterners and *Sarmátai* for the later lords of the whole steppe, and whether the two are one name or two peoples remains a genuinely open question; Latin fixed *Sarmatia* on the map so firmly that early modern Europe still used it; the Armenian geographers kept both Sarmatias, European and Asian, as standing regions of the world; Zoroastrian tradition remembered a *Sairima* among the ancient peoples that later Iranians read as these same westerners; and the Han court's registers carried the Aorsi, the Sarmatian east wing, as *Yǎncài*. The Sarmatians left no line of their own writing, and every entry on this page is the steppe seen from a settled window; their own voice survives only in the Iranian names they bore, and in the Ossetic speech of the Alans, the one Sarmatian people with a page and a story of its own.
