Bursa lies on the skirts of the Mysian Olympus, the great mountain of northwestern Anatolia, above a green plain famed for its springs. As Prusa it was a Bithynian royal foundation, named for a king Prusias and traditionally sited with the advice of Hannibal in exile at that court; under Rome it was a prosperous provincial city whose waterworks and finances Pliny the Younger managed, and complained of, as Trajan's governor. Orhan took it in 1326 and made it the Ottoman state's first true capital; the first sultans built their mosques and tombs there, and it remained the dynasty's necropolis and the empire's silk city after the capital moved on to Edirne.

The name's interest on this page is its perfect ordinariness, which the company it keeps makes visible. Between İznik, a fossilized phrase, and Edirne, a worn-down emperor, *Bursa* is simply *Prousa* said in Turkish, the initial cluster broken and the vowels turned, a Hellenistic king's name passing into the sultans' mouths with no story attached at all; the Ottomans kept the name of the city where they buried their founders exactly as they found it. The dynasty that Europe would call by a corrupted personal name, Ottoman from ʿOsmân, seated itself first in a city likewise named for one man, twenty-one centuries dead, of a dynasty nobody remembered. Kings vanish; the habit of naming cities for them is what survives, wearing whatever accent currently holds the walls.
