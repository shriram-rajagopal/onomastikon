Córdoba sits on the north bank of the Guadalquivir where the river ceases to be navigable, a site the Romans made the capital of their richest Iberian province. Founded as a Roman settlement in the mid-second century BCE, Corduba became the seat of Baetica and the birthplace of both Senecas and of Lucan; under the Visigoths it declined, and then in 756 the last Umayyad, escaped from the massacre of his house in Syria, made it the capital of al-Andalus. For the next two and a half centuries Qurṭuba was the largest and most learned city of western Europe, seat from 929 of a rival caliphate, city of the Great Mosque, of Averroes and of Maimonides; Castile took it in 1236.

The page's three entries are one name passed hand to hand. Latin *Corduba*, itself probably pre-Roman and of uncertain meaning, was heard by Strabo's Greek as *Kordýbē* and by the conquerors of 711, through Romance mouths, as something Arabic settled as *Qurṭuba*, the velar hardened to *qāf* and the dental picked up the emphatic *ṭ*, the same sound-substitutions that turned Makedonía into Maqadūniya on the atlas's eastern pages. Spanish *Córdoba* then descends from the Arabic form, not the Latin directly. No dynasty that held the city ever renamed it; each just respelled what it heard. The city changed language, faith, and empire three times, and the name simply changed its clothes each time and stayed.
