The Nile is the longest river in the world and the dominant geographic feature of northeastern Africa, flowing northward through more than six thousand kilometers from its sources in the East African highlands and the equatorial lakes to its delta on the Mediterranean coast. Its annual inundation, which deposited rich black silt across the Egyptian floodplain each summer until the construction of the Aswan High Dam, made pharaonic Egypt's three-millennium civilization possible and gave the river a religious and economic centrality unmatched by any other river in the ancient world. Ancient names for the Nile fall into two broad groups: the Egyptian internal designations, primarily *Ḥꜥpy* (the divinized inundation, often anglicized as Hapi) and *jtrw* or *jtrw-ꜥꜣ* ("the river" or "the great river"), and the external Greek *Neîlos* and Latin *Nīlus* of contested etymology, possibly derived from Egyptian *nꜣ-jrw* ("the rivers"), though Semitic and other derivations have also been proposed. The result is one of the few major geographic features named in nearly every ancient language whose speakers knew of it, a status the river earns through its outsized role in shaping the world that ancient writers were trying to describe.
