Aksum is the kingdom of the northern Ethiopian highlands that the Red Sea trade raised to the rank of a world power. The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* notices its port of Adulis and its metropolis around 50 CE; by 200 its king Gadarat was campaigning in Yemen and recorded there in Sabaic as king of the Aksumites and of Ḥabašat; the prophet Mani, writing in the 240s, counted it one of the four great kingdoms of the world beside Rome, Persia, and China. It minted gold on the Roman standard, carved the tallest monolithic stelae ever raised, and under Ezana, around 340, became the second state after Armenia to make Christianity its religion, the same king whose armies broke the last of Meroë on the Nile. Kaleb's invasion of Himyar in 525 made it master of both Red Sea shores for a generation. The Arab control of the sea after the seventh century cut off the trade that had made it, and the kingdom withdrew into the highlands, where its line ended in the tenth century and its successors would reign under a different name.

That name is the subject of this page, for Aksum is a kingdom that ended up called by a word that had belonged to someone else. Its own name was *ʾAksum*, the city's, extended to the realm, and the oldest written forms of it are its neighbors': the *Auxōmîtai* of the Greek sailors' handbook and the *ʾksmn* of the Sabaic inscriptions. Its Arabian neighbors called the highlanders *Ḥabašat*, a name the kings themselves carved into their titles, which Arabic took over as *al-Ḥabasha* and the European languages, through Arabic, as Abyssinia. The Greeks and the Greek Bible had a third word, *Aithiopía*, which they meant for Kush on the Nile; Ezana's Greek inscriptions applied it to his own kingdom, the Geʽez Bible rendered scripture's every Kush as *Ityoṗya*, and the kings of the highlands read the verses about Cush as verses about themselves. The Syriac churches, reading a Hebrew Bible, did the same with the other name and called the Aksumites Kushites. A Latin church historian, following the sailors' geography, called the country India.

The page is therefore the record of a transfer. The kingdom that destroyed Meroë inherited both of Meroë's biblical names, one in each church that read the Bible in Greek or in Syriac, and it kept the Greek one so firmly that the modern state on the highlands bears it still, while the name the kingdom gave itself survives as the name of a town.
