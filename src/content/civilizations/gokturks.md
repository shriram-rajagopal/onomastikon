The Türk qaghanate rose in 552 when Bumin overthrew the Rouran overlords of the Mongolian steppe, and within a generation its power ran from Manchuria to the Black Sea. It was the first empire in history ruled by a people calling themselves *Türk*, and the first steppe polity to leave substantial writing in its own language: the runiform stelae of the Orkhon valley, raised in the 730s for the princes of the second qaghanate, which look back on two centuries of conquest, collapse, Chinese subjection, and restoration before the state fell to the Uyghurs in 744. The qaghanate's earliest monument, at Bugut, is written in Sogdian, the language of its chancellery; its last monuments are in Turkic, in an alphabet of its own.

The name is the empire's most durable conquest. Every neighboring civilization wrote it down: the Chinese court as *Tūjué* through a Rouran plural, Byzantium as *Toûrkoi* when the embassy of 568 reached Constantinople, the Sogdians as *twrk* on the qaghanate's own stele, the Zoroastrian books as *Turk*, the Arabs as *al-Turk*, the Sanskrit chronicles as *Turuṣka*, and Persian epic as the *Turk* it wove into the old matter of Tūrān. Long after the qaghanate fell, the name kept its imperial career, passing to whichever Turkic-speaking power stood nearest, from the Seljuks to the Ottomans to a modern republic, so that a Bronze Age atlas's youngest self-designation is also among its most alive.
