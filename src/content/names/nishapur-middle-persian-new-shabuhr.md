*Nēw-Šābuhr* is the Middle Persian name of the city, a compound of *nēw*, "good, fair, brave," with the royal name Šābuhr, and the tradition assigns the foundation to Šābuhr I in the third century, with a refoundation credited to Šābuhr II. The Pahlavi geographical treatise *Šahrestānīhā ī Ērānšahr* lists the city among the capitals of the sunrise quarter with its founder's story attached; the attestation is Book Pahlavi, which has no Unicode encoding, so the entry carries transliteration only.

The name is a small grammatical mystery presented as a city. Read one way it is a dedication, "the good city of Shapur," on the pattern of every royal foundation from Alexandria onward; read another it is a whole sentence of praise, "fair is Shapur," frozen into a toponym, and Iranian scholarship has argued both. Either way the king is the subject, which is what the entry documents: the Sasanians named cities the way they cut rock reliefs, as statements about the monarch, and the east filled up with Šābuhr-cities, Gundēšābuhr and Bīšābuhr among them, a dynasty writing its genealogy across the map. Of them all, this one grew largest and outlived the dynasty longest, a piece of third-century royal publicity that turned into one of the great cities of Islam still saying, in a language nobody spoke anymore, how fair the king was.
