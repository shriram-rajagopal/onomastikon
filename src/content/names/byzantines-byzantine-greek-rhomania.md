The medieval life of *Rhōmanía*, the empire's everyday name for itself, continued from the late-antique coinage in the Ancient Greek entry above. It was never quite the official style, which stayed with the formal *basileía tōn Rhōmaíōn*, "empire of the Romans"; *Rhōmanía* was the ordinary word beneath it, the name of the country rather than the constitution, and it is the word that appears when Byzantine writers speak plainly. Constantine VII uses it throughout the *De Administrando Imperio*, his confidential handbook of statecraft, and it runs to the very end, into the laments composed for the fall of 1453.

Every other form on this page records how a neighbor named the empire; this one records the claim the neighbors were responding to. A *Rhōmaîos* of the tenth century spoke Greek and worshipped in Greek, but he did not call himself a *Héllēn*, a word the church had spent centuries using for pagans; his nation was Roman, whatever his language. The name also draws the page's finest boundary, against the entry for Greece: the same population that the Arabic geographers filed under *al-Yūnān* when quoting Aristotle became *al-Rūm* when it manned the Anatolian frontier, and *Rhōmanía* is the inside of that second name. The people of Rhōmanía prayed in Greek, argued in Greek, and insisted in Greek that they were Romans; the only Latin thing left in the empire was its name, and that was the possession it surrendered last.
