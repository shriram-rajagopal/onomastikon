*Kūšā* is the Old Persian name of the land of Kush, written *ku-u-š-a* with the long vowel spelled out, and its people are the *Kūšiyā*, the form the great lists prefer: on Darius's tomb at Naqsh-e Rustam (DNa 29), in the Susa foundation charter (DSe 30), and in Xerxes's daiva inscription (XPh 25) the Kushites stand near the end of the roll of subject peoples, beside the Libyans and the Carians, the African rim of the empire. Whether the empire ever held them is another matter. Herodotus reports Cambyses's expedition against the Ethiopians dissolving in the desert, and then reports (3.97) that the Ethiopians bordering Egypt brought Darius not tribute but gifts, gold and ebony and ivory and boys; the Apadana reliefs show them arriving with a tusk and a giraffe. The route by which the name reached Persian, through the Akkadian and Aramaic of the chancery or from Egypt directly, cannot be fixed, and the entry carries no edge.

The form replays, for Egypt's southern neighbor, the trilingual arrangement that the Egypt page records: *Kūšā* in Old Persian, *Kušaya* in the Elamite version, *Kūsu* in the Babylonian, one name carved three ways on the same monument. What distinguishes it is how little stands behind it. For Egypt the lists are confirmed by satraps, taxes, and a conquered country; for Kush the list is the whole of the evidence. On the tomb of Darius, Kush is a word at the end of a line, and that word is the only proof that the empire of the Persians ever reached it.
