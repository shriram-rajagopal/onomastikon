*Rhōmanía* (Ῥωμανία), "the land of the Romans," is the name the eastern empire's own people used for their country, formed from the ethnonym *Rhōmaîoi* with the *-ía* suffix that Greek uses to build country names. It surfaces in the fourth century, within a generation of Constantinople's founding; Athanasius of Alexandria uses it in the *Historia Arianorum*, written around 358, remarking that Rome is "the metropolis of Rhōmanía." The word belongs first to the everyday and Christian Greek of late antiquity, the register Lampe's lexicon catalogues, and its long medieval life belongs to the Byzantine Greek entry that continues it.

The name was coined while the empire still ran from ocean to desert, and that is its point on this page: *Rhōmanía* made the Roman state a *country*, a homeland with a Greek country-name, at a time when Rome itself still stood at its notional center. When the west fell away, nothing needed renaming; the Greek east had already been calling the whole Roman world by a word of its own making, and simply kept it. The empire that historians would later call Byzantine never had to invent a name for itself, because Athanasius's generation had done it a century before it was needed.
