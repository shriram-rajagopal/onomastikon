The Greek *Cházaroi*, the standing Byzantine name for the qaghanate of the northern steppe, written across the ninth- and tenth-century historians and codified in the *De Administrando Imperio*, Constantine VII's confidential handbook of steppe diplomacy, which devotes chapters to how the Khazars may be attacked and by whom. The relationship the name carries was, for Byzantium's dealings with nomads, uniquely close: Justinian II fled to the Khazars and married the qaghan's sister, Leo III wed his son to the Khazar princess whose son ruled as Leo the Khazar, and the emperors built the qaghan's fortress of Sarkel for him. An emperor of Byzantium could carry this page's ethnonym as his own byname, an honor no other steppe people of this atlas achieved.

The entry's texture is bureaucratic intimacy: where Byzantium wrote most steppe names in fear or contempt, *Cházaroi* appears in marriage contracts, engineering commissions, and the cold prose of a statecraft manual weighing the Khazars as one permanent piece on the northern board. The *De Administrando*'s chapters on unmaking them were written, in the event, a bare generation before the Rus did exactly that, so the name's Greek career closes as a case study in the handbook's own subject, the management, and the disposability, of steppe allies.
