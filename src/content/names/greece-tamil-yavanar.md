The Sangam poets' name for the westerners who came by sea, *Yavaṉar*: the Sanskrit *Yavana* taken into Tamil and fitted with the Tamil personal plural *-ar*. In the classical anthologies the word travels with cargo. Puṟanāṉūṟu 56 praises a Pandya king drinking "the cool, fragrant wine brought by the Yavaṉar"; Akanāṉūṟu 149 watches their fine ships ride the Periyāṟu to the port of Muciṟi, arriving with gold and leaving with pepper; elsewhere Yavaṉa bodyguards with their long coats stand at Tamil palace gates and Yavaṉa lamps light Tamil halls. By the time these poems were sung the ships were mostly Roman, and the Tamil word covered them without adjustment.

The entry is the far southeastern station of the atlas's widest family, the Ionian name that left Greece with the Persians and reached Tamil by the longest road of all: Greek *Iāones* to Persian *Yauna* to Sanskrit *Yavana* to a Tamil beach where the word finally met actual Greeks again, stepping off actual ships. No other branch of the family closes its circle so neatly. In Assyria and Judah the name labeled a distant people of the sea; in the Sangam country the sea delivered them in person, and the poets recorded not an abstraction but a trade balance, wine and gold in, pepper out. The Ionian name began as the Aegean shore's word for itself; five languages and four thousand miles later, it had become the Tamil word for the people who paid in gold.
