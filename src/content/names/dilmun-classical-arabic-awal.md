The Classical Arabic name of the Bahrain archipelago, *Awāl*, the standard medieval name of the island that had been Dilmun and Tylos. The geographers of the Islamic world, Yāqūt among them, treat Awāl as the island of the pearl-fisheries lying off *al-Baḥrayn*, a name which in classical usage meant the whole eastern Arabian coastland rather than the island itself; medieval tradition derived *Awāl* from the name of a pre-Islamic idol worshipped by the tribes of the coast. The name served throughout the Islamic middle ages and long past this atlas's boundary, well into the Ottoman and early modern centuries, before the mainland's name *al-Baḥrayn* finally migrated offshore and settled on the island in its place.

*Awāl* is the one name on this page that owes nothing to the Bronze Age thread: not a descendant of *Dilmun* or *Týlos* but a fresh start, the island renamed from within Arabia rather than from Mesopotamia or Greece. That break makes it the exception that maps the page's structure, marking where the world's longest-running commercial toponym finally lapsed. Yet the island's function under the new name was precisely its old one, pearls, palms, and the Gulf carrying-trade, and the later drift by which *al-Baḥrayn*, "the two seas," displaced *Awāl* even echoes the island's most ancient reputation, the sweet waters that rise up through the salt, which *Enki and Ninhursag* had made the gift of the god to Dilmun. The names broke; the island's story did not.
