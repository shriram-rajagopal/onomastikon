The Hittite name for Babylonia, *Šanḫara*, attested in a small number of texts from the archives of Hattusa in the empire period, where it designates the Mesopotamian south among the great kingdoms of the age. The same name had international currency beyond Anatolia: in the Amarna correspondence the king of Alashiya lists *Šanḫar* among the great powers whose envoys reach his court, and the Egyptian *Sngr* of the same diplomatic generation is its cognate. The form is written in the syllabic cuneiform of the Hittite chancellery; its origin is contested, connected by some scholars to the Kassite tribal name *Samḫarû* and by others to the name of Sumer itself.

*Šanḫara* shows what the Late Bronze Age remembered of Sumer, which is to say nothing at all. By the time the Hittite kings corresponded with the south, the land of the first cities had been Babylonia for half a millennium, and the international vocabulary of the great powers named it with a word that owed no debt to the Akkadian *Šumeru* preserved in Babylon's own scribal tradition. The age had a working name for the south of Mesopotamia and no longer a memory of Sumer inside it; the land's second life wore a name that knew nothing of its first.
