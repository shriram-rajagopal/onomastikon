*Kostantiniyye* (قسطنطينيه) is the Ottoman Turkish name of Constantinople, the Arabic *al-Qusṭanṭīniyya* taken over with the spelling intact and the pronunciation turned Turkish. From the conquest of 1453 it served as the city's formal name in the Ottoman state's own usage, on documents and, for centuries, on the coinage, the mint-name of the imperial capital; it belongs to this atlas under the continuity clause, the early Ottoman continuation of a naming tradition the page has carried since Constantine. Beside it in speech ran the popular name that would eventually win, *İstanbul*, from the Greek *eis tḕn pólin*, "to the City," whose story the page's Greek entries tell; the two names shared the city for over four centuries until the republic retired the formal one in the twentieth.

The entry's significance is who is using the name. Every earlier form in the constantinople family belongs to outsiders naming the Christian capital; this one belongs to the Muslim dynasty that took the city and then went on calling it Constantine's for as long as the empire lasted, the founder's name outliving the founder's faith in the founder's own city. Conquerors on this atlas usually rename or re-pronounce; the Ottomans did both at once and let the forms divide the labor, Constantine on the coins and the Greek street-phrase in the street. The emperor lost his city in 1453 and kept his name on it until 1922; few defeats have been so thoroughly survived.
