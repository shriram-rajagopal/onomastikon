*Bactra* is the Latin form of the Greek city-name, taken over with its neuter plural intact. Its most famous attestation is poetic: on the shield of Aeneas, Vergil sets Antony coming to Actium with the strength of the Orient, from "farthest Bactra," *ultima Bactra*, the city standing as the eastern limit of the world a Roman fleet might have to face. The geographers use the name soberly for the Bactrian capital, but it is the poets' Bactra, remote and ominous, that Latin literature remembered.

The entry shows what Rome did with a name it inherited but never approached: it made the city a measure of distance. No Roman army saw Bactra; the name served instead as the far end of imaginable empire, paired with Indus and Ganges in the rhetoric of boundless ambition. Where the Greek entry on this page records a capital Greeks actually ruled, the Latin one records a symbol, the same four letters doing entirely different work one language to the west.
