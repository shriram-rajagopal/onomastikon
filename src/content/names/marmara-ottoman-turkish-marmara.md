*Marmara* (مرمره) is the Turkish name of the sea, taken from its largest island. The island is ancient Prokonnesos, whose quarries of white-veined marble supplied Constantinople for a thousand years, Hagia Sophia's columns and pavements among the results, and whose Greek name had already yielded in the Middle Ages to *Marmarás*, from *mármaros*, "marble," the island called simply after its stone. Ottoman usage extended the island's name to the water around it, *Marmara denizi*, the sea of Marmara, and the name belongs to this atlas under the continuity clause, the early Ottoman renaming that displaced the twenty-century-old Propontis; it remains the sea's name in Turkish and, through it, in every modern language.

The renaming is a complete inversion of the name it replaced, which is the entry's point. *Propontís* defined the sea relationally, by where it led; *Marmara* defines it materially, by what it contains, and what it contains is a quarry. There is a quiet historical circle in that: the marble of Prokonnesos built the city that made this sea the center of the world, and when the city's builders' language gave way, the sea took the quarry's name, as if the stone had outlasted every empire that shipped it. The Greeks named the water for the journey; the Turks named it for the cargo, and the cargo, fittingly for this shore, was the ruins' own raw material.
