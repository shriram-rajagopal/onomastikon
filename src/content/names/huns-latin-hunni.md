The Latin *Hunni*, fixed in the language by Ammianus Marcellinus, whose final book opens with the most famous ethnographic set piece of late antiquity: the *Hunnorum gens*, barely known to old records, dwelling beyond the Maeotic marshes, exceeding every measure of savagery, seared cheeks, lives spent on horseback, a people he presents as the seed of all the ruin his history must then narrate. Jordanes, a century and a half later, embroiders the portrait with the Gothic legend that the Huns were born of witches exiled to the steppe and unclean spirits, hatred doing the work of ethnography.

The entry preserves the Western name at the moment of maximum incomprehension. Ammianus reaches for every trope of monstrosity precisely because the Huns arrived outside all the categories Rome sorted barbarians with; the portrait tells us little about Huns and a great deal about a world receiving a blow it could not classify. The name's afterlife kept that charge: *Hunni* became Latin Christendom's permanent shorthand for the scourge from the east, revived for Magyars in the tenth century and, as an insult, for enemies into the twentieth. Few names in this atlas have done so much work with so little content; from Ammianus forward, *Hunni* has meant less a people than the fear of one.
