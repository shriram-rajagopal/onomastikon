The crusaders' name for Cairo, *Babylonia*, continuing the Greek name of the Roman fortress of Babylon on the Nile beside which Fustat, and then Cairo, grew. In the Latin of the crusading age the Egyptian capital is *Babylonia* and its Ayyubid and Mamluk rulers are the *soldanus Babilonie*, the "sultan of Babylon"; Oliver of Paderborn's *Historia Damiatina*, the eyewitness account of the Fifth Crusade, narrates an army that took Damietta as the first step on the road to *Babylonia*. The usage held to the end of the period, long after the Mesopotamian Babylon itself was a ruin known mostly from books.

The name carried more than geography. The Latin West already had a Babylon, the Babylon of the prophets and the Apocalypse, and a war against the "sultan of Babylon" arrived already charged with that scripture; the accident by which a Roman garrison fort had borne the old Mesopotamian name let crusade preaching collapse the two cities into one. The page's pattern makes the irony visible: the Greek and Coptic entries above record how a fortress kept Babylon's name on the Nile for a thousand quiet years, and this entry records what happened when that quiet name met an apocalyptic imagination. The crusaders sailed against the great city of their own age under the name of a city that no longer existed; the real Babylon lay silent under Mesopotamian silt while its name, on the Nile, commanded armies.
