The Akkadian name of the far land, *Meluḫḫa*, identical in writing to the Sumerian and attested from the moment the Sargonic empire opened the long sea-route: Sargon of Akkad's inscription setting the ships of Meluhha at his quay beside those of Magan and Dilmun is the name's grand entrance into royal rhetoric. The most personal attestation is a cylinder seal, Akkadian in language and style, belonging to Shu-ilishu, *eme-bal Me-luḫ-ḫa*, "interpreter of Meluhha," a man who made his living carrying speech between Akkadian and a language of which not one word otherwise survives. After the Indus trade failed, the name lived on as pure inheritance, and the Neo-Assyrian scribes redeployed it: in the annals of Sargon II and his successors *Meluḫḫa* means Kush, so that a rebel fleeing Assyrian wrath in Philistia is said to run to the king of Meluhha, a Kushite pharaoh on the Nile.

The Akkadian form is where this name both did its work and lost its referent, and the loss is instructive. To the scribes of the seventh century BCE, *Meluḫḫa* and *Makkan* were a matched antique pair meaning "the uttermost south," and they reassigned both wholesale to Africa without any sense of contradiction, because the lands themselves had passed out of all living knowledge. A name is only as durable as the acquaintance behind it. Shu-ilishu's seal and Sargon II's annals use the same four signs, and between them the word has traveled from a working interpreter's job title to a geographical ghost, pointing at whatever far shore the empire currently found at the end of the sea.
