The Middle Persian name of the river in the *Bundahišn*'s catalogue of waters, *Diglat*, the Aramaic name of the Tigris taken into Persian unchanged. The form reproduces exactly the *Diglat* of the Aramaic entry on this page, the clipped descendant of Akkadian *Idiqlat* that had become the river's working name in the common speech of Mesopotamia, and it names the river that ran past the empire's own capital at Ctesiphon. The attestation is Book Pahlavi, which has no Unicode encoding, so the entry carries transliteration only.

The borrowing runs against the page's oldest Persian precedent. The Achaemenids had remade this river as *Tigrā*, the arrow, an Iranian pun carved at Behistun that the whole Western tradition inherited; the Sasanians, who unlike the Achaemenids lived on the river's bank, used no such invention. For the empire whose court, granaries, and taxes sat on the Tigris, the river needed no imperial rebranding, and its name was what the Aramaic-speaking boatmen and farmers of Asōristān called it. The Achaemenid name was made for inscriptions and the Sasanian name was learned from neighbors, and the difference measures how far Persia had moved onto the river it once merely crossed.
