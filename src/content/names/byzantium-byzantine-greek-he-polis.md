What the Byzantines actually called their capital, *hē Pólis*, "the City," with no further specification: in a Greek world full of cities, the definite article alone was address enough. The usage runs through the whole medieval period in histories, saints' lives, and everyday speech, beside the formal *Kōnstantinoúpolis* of documents and coins, and it survives in the cry that carried the news of 1453, *heálō hē Pólis*, "the City has fallen." From the phrase *eis tēn Pólin*, "to the City," on the roads and in the mouths of everyone who traveled there, Turkish usage eventually distilled *Istanbul*: the modern name of the metropolis is a fossilized Greek direction, the way to a city so singular it needed no name.

Every other form on this page is built from something, a founder's name, an emperor's name, a Greek compound; this one is built from nothing but a definite article, and it is the most Byzantine name of all. To say "the City" and mean only one place, from the Danube to the Euphrates, was to state the empire's geography of prestige in two syllables: there were other poleis, but no other Pólis. Emperors fell, the formal name changed hands and languages, and the article held; Constantinople could be captured, but "the City" could only be the place it had always been. It is the one name here that is less a word than a gesture, an entire civilization pointing at its own center.
