*Nīshāpūr* (نیشاپور) is the Classical Persian form of the name, the native continuation of *Nēw-Šābuhr* with the first element smoothed to *nī-* and the king's name to *-shāpūr*, the shape that English and the modern map inherit. The city under this name belongs to the heart of Persian literary history: Omar Khayyam was born, worked, and is buried there, ʿAṭṭār the mystic poet likewise, and the city appears across the classical literature as a byword for Khurasani civilization; the modern Iranian city of Neyshabur continues name and site.

The Persian entry completes a family whose whole story is erosion, and turns the erosion into the point. In Middle Persian the name was a legible sentence about a king; in Arabic it became opaque syllables requiring a gloss; in New Persian it is again a native word, but a sealed one, a city-name pure and simple that no Persian speaker parses as praise. The king dissolved into his city so completely that the city's own poets, Khayyam above all, could meditate on the vanity of kings inside a name that was itself a vanished king's boast, apparently without anyone finding the fact remarkable. This atlas has names that outlived their meanings everywhere; Nishapur is the one where the meaning's ghost stayed in the house, unnoticed, while the tenants wrote poetry about ghosts.
