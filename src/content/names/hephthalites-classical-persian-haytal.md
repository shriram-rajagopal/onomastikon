The Classical Persian *Hayṭāl*, the New Persian shape of the old enemy's name, standard in the historians and in Firdawsi, whose *Shāhnāmeh* carries the Hephthalite wars of Pērōz and the exile of Kavād among the Haytālians into the national epic. The form returned to Persian by a detour: the living Middle Persian name of the fifth century's enemy passed into the Arabic historical tradition as *Hayāṭila*, and the New Persian written after the conquest took the word back from the Arabic historians along with so much else of its own pre-Islamic memory, the emphatic *ṭ* of the Arabic spelling riding along as the fingerprint of the round trip.

The entry is the family's study in how a nation's memory can pass through a foreign archive and come home changed. Persian did not forget the Hephthalites; but the Persian that remembered them was a new language, reading its own Sasanian past largely as the Arabic chroniclers had preserved it, so that the name of the empire's worst eastern humiliation returned wearing Arabic morphology. On a page whose forms mostly radiate outward from the enemy's own name, this one traces a loop, out through the conquered empire's chronicle tradition and back into its successor language, the Hephthalites twice inherited by the people they once defeated.
