*Baîtis* (Βαῖτις) is the Greek form of the river's name, used throughout Strabo's account of the astonishing prosperity of its valley, the ships ascending to Hispalis and Corduba, the oil and wine and silver coming down. Strabo also preserves the river's deeper past in Greek memory: he reports that it was formerly called *Tartēssós*, the name of the half-legendary kingdom at its mouth that archaic Greek traders had known, so that Greek holds both ends of the river's ancient naming, the old name of legend and the current name shared with Rome. Whether Greek took *Baîtis* from Latin usage or directly from the Iberian coast cannot be settled, and the entry claims no edge.

The Tartessos memory is what distinguishes this from the page's other classical entry. For Latin, Baetis was simply the river's name; Greek alone remembered that it had had another, because Greeks had been trading at its mouth centuries before Rome arrived, when Tartessos was the far edge of the world and its king a figure of story. The Greek entry is thus the only one on the page that knows the river's name had already changed once before the Arabs changed it again: three names deep, and the middle one, the one Rome built a province on, proved the most mortal of the three. Legends and descriptions both outlived the proper name.
