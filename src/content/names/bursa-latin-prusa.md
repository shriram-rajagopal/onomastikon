*Prusa* is the Latin form of the name, and its classic attestation is the same Bithynian correspondence that carries Nicaea's: Pliny the Younger, Trajan's governor, wrote to the emperor about Prusa's affairs, a bath the citizens wanted built, accounts that wanted auditing, and an eye doctor's dubious claim on public funds, with Trajan's brisk answers preserved. The file even touches the city's most famous son, for among Pliny's Prusan problems was the estate of Dio Chrysostom, entangled in a local accusation; governor and orator, the two men through whom the city survives in two literatures, met in a provincial audit.

The entry is a companion piece to Latin Nicaea on this atlas, and the pairing defines a genre: Bithynia is the one province whose small cities live for us chiefly in an administrator's mailbag, their names attested not in epic or scripture but in requests for a building permit. That register is its own kind of preservation, humbler and more exact than fame; we know Prusa's name from documents that also record its plumbing. When the town under the false Olympus later became the cradle of an empire, nothing in the Latin record had foreseen it, which is the mailbag's honest charm; of all the atlas's sources, correspondence alone never knows the future.
