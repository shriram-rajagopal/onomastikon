*Marw* is the Middle Persian name of the city, the Old Persian *Margu-* worn down by the regular loss of its interior consonant, and the form in which the name passed to every later language. Under the Sasanians Marw was the great stronghold of the northeast, the seat of marzbans and the mint whose signature appears across the empire's late coinage; Šābuhr's third-century inscription counts it among the empire's lands, and the Pahlavi *Šahrestānīhā ī Ērānšahr* records its foundation legend among the cities of the sunrise quarter. The literary attestation is Book Pahlavi, which has no Unicode encoding, so the entry carries transliteration only. It was at Marw, in 651, that the last Sasanian king Yazdgird III was murdered in a miller's hut, the empire ending in the city that guarded its east.

Within the family this is the load-bearing form: Arabic and New Persian took *Marw* as they found it, so the two syllables that Sasanian speech had left of Darius's *Marguš* became the permanent shape of the name. The entry therefore records both a phonological event and a historical one, and they rhyme; the name and the empire were each reduced at Marw to what could survive. When the Abbasid revolution rose from the same city a century after Yazdgird died there, it rose under this name, the Sasanian form of the word, in a Persian mouth, against an Arab dynasty. The old east kept its own name for itself, and made history answer to it.
