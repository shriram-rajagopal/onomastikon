*Tūjué* 突厥 is the Chinese name of the Türk qaghanate, in continuous use from the dynastic histories of the sixth century through the Tang annals that record the empire's two incarnations and its fall. The transcription is phonetic, and its shape has long been read as evidence of the route the name traveled: the Middle Chinese value, roughly *thwot-kjut*, renders not the bare *Türk* but a form with a final dental, most plausibly the plural *Türküt* that the Rouran, the qaghanate's first overlords, would have used of their sometime vassals. The Chinese court, on this reading, learned the Turks' name from the power the Turks overthrew.

The entry preserves a fossil of steppe politics inside a phonetic spelling. The Orkhon inscriptions remember *Tabγač*, China, as the empire that subjugated the Türk people for fifty years; the Chinese name of the Türks remembers, in its final consonant, the earlier master neither empire mourned. Among the borrowed forms on this page *Tūjué* is also the earliest, on record within a couple of years of the qaghanate's founding, so that the first foreign archive to register the name *Türk* did so already secondhand, in the grammatical clothing of a defeated intermediary.
