The Akkadian name of the kingdom of Lake Van, conventionally Latinized in scholarship as *Urarṭu*, is written in cuneiform with several orthographies; the spelling shown here, *ú-ra-aš-tu*, is the one used in the Babylonian column of Darius's Behistun inscription, with the same alternation of *š* for *r* that runs through the Assyro-Babylonian writings of the name. The name first appears as *Uruaṭri*, a grouping of highland lands named by Shalmaneser I of Assyria in the thirteenth century BCE, and hardens into *Urarṭu* in the Neo-Assyrian annals of the early first millennium, when the kingdom had become Assyria's most formidable northern rival. The royal inscriptions of Sargon II, including the account of his eighth campaign against king Rusa in 714 BCE, are among the fullest Assyrian records of the wars in the mountains.

The Assyrian exonym is the form that travels, and the kingdom's own name, *Biainili*, never left the highland. It is *Urarṭu*, not *Biainili*, that the Hebrew Bible preserves as *Araraṭ*, and the Assyrian name therefore became the resting-place of the ark and the byword for the lost mountain kingdom. The same name was reused, two centuries after Urartu fell, to render the new Achaemenid province of Armenia in the Babylonian text at Behistun, binding the old name to the land that succeeded it. The whole later life of the name, biblical, geographic, and scholarly, descends from what Assyrian scribes called the enemy beyond their northern frontier.
