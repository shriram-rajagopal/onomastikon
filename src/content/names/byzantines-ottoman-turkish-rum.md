*Rūm* (روم) is the Ottoman Turkish name for the Byzantines, their lands, and their people, inherited through the Persian literary language that the Anatolian Turkish courts read and imitated. It is everywhere in the earliest Ottoman literature: Ahmedî's verse chronicle of the House of Osman, appended to his *İskendernâme* around 1412, narrates the dynasty's wars as campaigns against the lords of *Rūm*, and the Ottoman province spanning the conquered Balkans was named Rūm-ili, "the land of the Romans," the later Rumelia. After 1453 the word settled onto the empire's Greek Orthodox subjects, the Rūm millet, and *Rūmī* remained the adjective for the Greek of Anatolia.

The entry closes the roma family's longest arc with its sharpest turn: the people who ended the Roman state adopted, and kept, the Roman name for everything they took from it. The Ottomans called their European half the land of Romans, their Greek subjects Romans, and in their claims after 1453 the sultan himself *Qayṣar-i Rūm*, Caesar of Rome; the name survived the conquest on the conquerors' own tongues, demoted from an empire to a community but never discarded. Eleven languages deep, the family ends where it began, with rulers of Constantinople calling their world Roman; only the rulers had changed, and the name, as ever on this page, had not.
