*Toûrkoi* (Τοῦρκοι) is the Byzantine name for the Ottomans, the plain usage of the last Greek historians, Doukas and Chalkokondyles among them, who wrote the empire's end within a decade of living it; their histories, composed just past 1453 about the decades before it, are this atlas's continuity clause at its most literal. The word itself was old in Greek: Constantine VII had used *Toûrkoi* in the tenth century for the Magyars, and the name wandered among steppe peoples in Byzantine usage before settling permanently, from the eleventh century onward, on the Turks of Anatolia, first Seljuk and then Ottoman.

The refusal inside the name is the entry's point. The Ottomans styled themselves the House of Osman and, increasingly, heirs of Rome; the Byzantines, who called themselves Romans, could concede neither, and *Toûrkoi* names the enemy strictly by ethnicity, an ethnonym where the dynasty offered a dynasty and the Persians offered *Rūm*. It is the mirror of the Franks' *imperator Graecorum* aimed at Byzantium itself on this atlas's western pages: what a rival calls you is calibrated exactly to what it will not call you. After 1453 the Greek east lived inside the word's shadow, *tourkokratía*, the rule of the Turks; the last name the dying empire coined for its conqueror became the first name of the era that followed it.
