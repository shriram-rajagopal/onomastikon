The Sumerian name for the city, *KÁ.DINGIR.RA(KI)*, "gate of god," written with the signs *ká* (gate), *dingir* (god), and the genitive *-ra*, closed by the place-determinative *ki*. It is the writing from which the Akkadian *Bābilu* takes both its shape and its sense: one logogram, read by Sumerian scribes as *Kadingirra* and by Akkadian scribes as *Bāb-ilim*, two languages pronouncing the same signs in their own words for the same idea. Babylon appears under this writing in Sumerian administrative and royal texts from the late third millennium and the Ur III period, when it was still a minor town long before its rise under the Amorite kings.

The Sumerian form makes visible what the Akkadian only implies, that the city's written name was from the start a logographic compound any literate Mesopotamian could read aloud in their own language. *Kadingirra* is the same name as *Bābilu* and not the same word: the original behind the translation that the rest of this page descends from.
