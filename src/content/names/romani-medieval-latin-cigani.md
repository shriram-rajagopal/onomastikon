The Latin name under which the Romani enter the Western European record, *Cigani*, the Byzantine *Atsínganoi* worn down by its passage through the Balkans and Hungary. Its anchor attestation is a legal document: in 1423 the emperor-king Sigismund issued a safe-conduct at Spiš for "Ladislaus Wayuoda Ciganorum," Ladislaus voivode of the Cigani, commanding his towns to protect the voivode's people from hindrance or harassment. The same decade scatters the companies across the chronicles of Italy, France, and the Empire, usually under a second, stranger name, for the travelers presented themselves as pilgrims from Little Egypt, and the record calls them dukes and counts of Egypt as often as anything else; that misnomer, the future Gypsy and Gitano, never settled into one stable Latin form within this atlas's window.

The entry catches the moment a people acquires legal existence in a new civilization, and the terms of it. The safe-conduct is protection, which means it is also classification: to move through Christendom the companies needed a name the chancery could write, and the name the chancery wrote was Byzantium's old mistake, now two languages and four centuries from the heresy it originally meant. Everything about the document is characteristic of the centuries that followed, a people defined in other men's paperwork, under a borrowed name, with their safety contingent on a king's seal. The atlas's youngest name for any people is also its bluntest lesson in who gets to write.
