*Toûrkoi* enters Greek with the diplomacy of 568, when an embassy from the qaghanate, led by the Sogdian Maniakh, reached Justin II to propose an alliance against Persia over the silk routes, and Zemarchus was sent back across the steppe to the qaghan; Menander Protector's account of these exchanges is the West's first record of the Turks under their own name. The form stayed in the Byzantine bureaucratic vocabulary long after the qaghanate itself dissolved, and with a referent that wandered: by the tenth century the *De Administrando Imperio* uses *Toûrkoi* chiefly of the Magyars, and later Byzantine writers of the Seljuks and Ottomans, the name shifting to whichever steppe-descended power currently mattered.

The first attestation is also a small history of how names travel: Byzantium learned the word for the Turks from a Sogdian merchant speaking for a Turkic empire against an Iranian one, three languages deep in a single sentence. And the later drift of *Toûrkoi* onto the Magyars and then the Ottomans makes this entry the hinge between two of the atlas's threads, the qaghanate whose name it transcribes and the later powers, treated on their own pages, that inherited the label. Byzantium kept one word for a thousand years and let the steppe decide, generation by generation, whom it meant.
