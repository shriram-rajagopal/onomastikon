*Naysābūr* (نيسابور) is the Arabic form of the Sasanian name, the compound's edges rounded and its meaning left behind in the crossing. Under this name the city had its centuries of greatness: capital of the Tahirids, a Samanid and then Ghaznavid metropolis contending with Baghdad in size, and one of the most prolific producers of scholars in Islamic history, whose biographical dictionaries needed multiple volumes for the *Naysābūrī*s alone. Yāqūt, whose entry preserves the tradition that the name was Persian for "the thing of Sābūr," wrote after the city's fall; earthquake and the Mongol massacre of 1221 ended its great period within a generation of Merv's.

The Arabic entry shows the name working loose from its king. Arabic transmitted the royal syllables faithfully but not the royal meaning; *nēw* is not an Arabic word, Šābuhr not an Arabic king, and the geographers had to be told, by Persians, what the city's name had once boasted. What replaced the lost meaning was a new one the city earned itself: in the scholarly literature *Naysābūr* connotes learning the way Athens does elsewhere on this atlas, a brand built by its jurists and hadith masters rather than its founder. The entry's quiet lesson is that cities re-etymologize themselves by reputation; by the eleventh century the name meant "the city of scholars" in practice, and no one needed the Sasanian gloss to feel it.
