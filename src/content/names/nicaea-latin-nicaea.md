*Nicaea* is the Latin form of the Greek name, a plain transliteration. Its most vivid classical attestation is administrative: Pliny the Younger, governing Bithynia for Trajan around 110, wrote to the emperor about Nicaea's troubles, a theatre swallowing money into unstable ground and a gymnasium rebuilt badly, and Trajan's replies survive beside the questions, the city's building problems preserved in the correspondence of the man who also asked the emperor what to do about the Christians. Two centuries later the church council made the Latin form permanent in the west: *fides Nicaena*, the Nicene faith, entered Latin theology as the standard of orthodoxy itself.

The entry's arc, from Pliny's file of municipal complaints to the adjective for correct belief, is the sharpest register-jump of any Latin form on this atlas. Latin first knew Nicaea as a badly run provincial town and last knew it as a word meaning true doctrine, the same four syllables promoted from an auditor's headache to a creed; and it was the creed-sense alone that the west carried through the Middle Ages, when *Nicaenus* was on every literate churchman's tongue and the town itself was beyond the Turks. On this page Greek built the city and Turkish inherited it; Latin's contribution was to turn its name into an abstraction, which proved the most durable building on the site.
