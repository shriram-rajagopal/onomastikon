*Kūšāyē*, the Kushites, is what the Syriac writers of the sixth century called the Aksumites. The *Book of the Himyarites*, the Syriac account of the persecution of the Christians of Najran and of Kaleb's expedition across the Red Sea to avenge them in 525, calls the king and his army Kushites throughout; Simeon of Beth Arsham's letter on the martyrs, written as the news arrived, does the same, and John of Ephesus and the Syriac chronicles follow. The usage is the Peshitta's: the Syriac Bible, reading the Hebrew, had kept *Kūš* for the land south of Egypt, and when a Christian kingdom became the great power of the Red Sea, Syriac applied the biblical ethnonym of that quarter of the world to it, exactly as Greek had applied *Aithíopes*.

The entry completes the transfer the page records. The two biblical names for Kush, the Hebrew one and the Greek one, both migrated to Aksum, each along the churches that read the Bible in that language: Greek-readers called the Aksumites Ethiopians, Syriac-readers called them Kushites, and in the one language of the atlas where the old name of Nubia kept any currency at all, it did so by naming the kingdom that had destroyed Nubia's last capital. The kingdom that broke Meroë inherited both of Meroë's names, and in Syriac it inherited the older one.
