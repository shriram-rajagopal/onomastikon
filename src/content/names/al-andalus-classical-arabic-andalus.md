*al-Andalus* (الأندلس) is the Arabic name of Islamic Iberia, and it appears almost at the moment of the conquest itself: a bilingual dinar struck in 716, five years after the crossing, carries *al-Andalus* in Arabic on one face and *Span(ia)* in Latin on the other, the new name and the old presented as equivalents by the mint that had to talk to both audiences. From then on the name is universal in Arabic usage for the peninsula under Islam, narrowing over the centuries with the territory it described. Its origin is a genuine unknown. The old derivation from the Vandals has no textual support; Heinz Halm's proposal reads it as Gothic *landahlauts*, "allotted land," the conquerors' term for the Visigoths' distributed estates; others have looked to an Atlantic term; none has won the field.

The entry's significance is a name with a birth certificate but no parentage. Most disputed etymologies on this atlas fade backward into prehistory; *al-Andalus* surfaces in a dated, datable document within living memory of its coinage, and is opaque anyway, which suggests the Arabs took over a word already current on the ground whose own history was short and unrecorded. The name then outlived its country twice over: as the shrinking realm itself until 1492, and as Spanish *Andalucía*, the modern region that preserves the Arabic name of everything Spain reconquered. The coin of 716 shows the name being born; nothing shows where it came from, and thirteen centuries of scholarship have not moved past that first dinar.
