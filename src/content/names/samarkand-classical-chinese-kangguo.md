*Kāngguó* (康國), "the state of Kang," is the Tang name for Samarkand, the head of the "nine clans of Zhaowu" under which the dynastic histories catalogued the Sogdian principalities, each reduced to a single-character surname: Kang for Samarkand, An for Bukhara, and their lesser neighbors down the list. The account of the Western Regions describes the Kang state as the greatest of the nine, its king enthroned facing east, its people gifted traders whose sons were taught commerce from boyhood, and its embassies bringing lions, dancers, and golden peaches to Chang'an; Sogdians settling in China took Kang as their family name, and it survives as a Chinese surname still.

Beside the transcriptions that fill this atlas, *Kāng* is a different operation entirely: not the city's name adapted but the city replaced by a Chinese surname, the polity domesticated into the empire's own kinship vocabulary. The choice was administrative but its effect was intimate; a Samarkandi in China was not a foreigner with an unpronounceable city but a Mr. Kang, his origin worn as a family name, and the golden-peach embassies came, in the registers, from a relative's house. Of all the ways one civilization has written another's city into its records, this is the most absorbing, in both senses: Samarkand entered China not as a word but as kin.
