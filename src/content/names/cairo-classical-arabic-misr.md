*Miṣr* (مصر) is, on this page, a city: the name of Egypt applied to Egypt's capital. The usage began with the conquest, when the garrison city of Fustat, founded in 641, became *Miṣr al-Fusṭāṭ* and then simply *Miṣr*; the common noun *miṣr*, "garrison town," plural *amṣār*, was the standard term for the conquest settlements, and in Egypt the country's own ancient name and the generic word collapsed into each other. When al-Qāhira absorbed Fustat's role, it inherited the usage whole, and *Miṣr* has meant the capital in everyday Egyptian speech ever since, down to the modern colloquial *Maṣr* for Cairo.

The entry is the strangest member of the *mṣr* family, the atlas's oldest Semitic name-thread, which runs from Akkadian *Miṣru* through Hebrew *Miṣrayim* to the Arabic name of Egypt and, in the Phase 2.5 sweep, as far as Chinese *Wùsīlí*. Everywhere else the thread names the country; here alone it narrows to a single city, four thousand years of a name funneled to a point at the head of the Delta. The metonymy runs opposite to the world's usual habit, where capitals lend their names outward (a "Rome" that means the empire); Egypt instead poured the country's name into its capital. Ask for Miṣr inside Egypt and you will be shown not the country but Cairo, as if the whole land kept one front door and hung its name on it.
