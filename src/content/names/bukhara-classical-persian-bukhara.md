*Bukhārā* (بخارا) in Classical Persian is the name of the language's own first capital. The Samanid court at Bukhara in the ninth and tenth centuries is where New Persian poetry began its recorded life, and the name is embedded in its founding monument: Rūdakī's famous lines urging the amir home, "the Jū-yi Mūliyān's scent comes to us; long live Bukhārā, be glad amid your friends, the amir is the moon and Bukhārā the sky," are among the earliest celebrated verses in the language, and they are about the city itself. Persian usage carries the name unchanged to the present.

The entry marks a unique relationship on this atlas: not a language naming a city, but a city hosting a language's rebirth and receiving its first great compliment in return. Persian literature's opening canon includes Bukhara's own praises, so the city is written into the tradition's foundation the way Troy is written into Greek's, except as home rather than ruin. There is an irony the entry should not hide: the court that midwifed Persian's return was itself Turkic-ruled within a century, and Bukhara's speech today is Tajik Persian in an Uzbek state, the language and the city still entangled a millennium after Rūdakī rhymed them. The first city Persian poetry ever praised is still answering to the same name, in the same language, against considerable odds.
