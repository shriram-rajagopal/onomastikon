*Proûsa* (Προῦσα) is the Greek name of Bursa, a Bithynian royal foundation of around 200 BCE named for a king Prusias; Strabo places it on the Mysian Olympus and calls it a well-governed city, and preserves the tradition that connects its founding to Hannibal, who ended his exile at Prusias's court and was said to have advised the siting. The city produced one Greek celebrity, the orator Dio Chrysostom, whose civic speeches to his quarrelsome hometown survive; Cassius Dio records its fortunes in the Roman civil wars. The name continued in Byzantine use until the Ottoman capture and after, Greek Prousa beside Turkish Bursa into modern times.

The entry's texture is its supporting cast: few small cities on this atlas keep such company in their attestations. A name from one king, a site legend starring Hannibal, a resident orator who scolded the town in classical periods, all for a provincial spa under a mountain confusingly named Olympus; the name *Proûsa* is ordinary, but everything recorded around it is slightly too grand for the place, as if the city kept better archives than its size deserved. History repaid the archives in 1326, when the town became the first capital of an empire, and the Greek name found itself, briefly, naming the seat of the power that would end the Greek world's independence; the well-kept provincial record had been rehearsal.
