*Qurṭuba* (قرطبة) is the Arabic form of the Roman name, taken over at the conquest of 711 from the Romance speech of the city's inhabitants. The adaptation is textbook: the back consonant hardened to *qāf*, the dental picked up the emphatic *ṭāʾ*, and the ending settled into the feminine *-a*, the same substitutions that produced Maqadūniya from Makedonía and Ṭulayṭula from Toletum across this atlas. Under the name Qurṭuba the city had its greatest era, capital of the Umayyad emirate and caliphate, and Yāqūt's gazetteer, written after the city had passed its peak, still describes it as the seat of al-Andalus's glory. The name remains the Arabic word for Córdoba today.

The entry's payoff is what the Arabic stage did to the name's future. Spanish *Córdoba* does not continue Latin *Corduba* directly; it continues the Arabic pronunciation, as the vowels show, so the modern city bears its Roman name only as the Arabs said it. Five centuries of Arabic speech are permanently audible in the name of a city now Christian for eight, the exact acoustic counterpart of the Great Mosque with the cathedral standing inside it. Elsewhere on the atlas conquest replaces names; in al-Andalus it re-pronounced them, and the re-pronunciation is what survived. Córdoba never lost its ancient name; it lost the ancient way of saying it.
