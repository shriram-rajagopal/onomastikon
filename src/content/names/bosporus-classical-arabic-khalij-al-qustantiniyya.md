The standard Arabic name for the strait, a descriptive genitive rather than a transcription: the *khalīj*, gulf or channel, of Constantinople. The geographers and chroniclers describe it as the narrow water that issues from the Black Sea, passes beneath the walls of the city, and runs down to the Mediterranean; Ibn Kathīr's cosmographical chapter on the seas and rivers presents it under exactly this name, and the campaign narratives of the Umayyad sieges measure their progress by reaching it. The name covers what Greek divides into Bosporus and Hellespont, one channel with one city at its throat.

The naming logic inverts the Greek entries above it. *Bósporos*, the ox ford, remembers a myth about crossing the water; the Arabic name ignores the crossing entirely and records the power that guarded it. For the geographers writing in Baghdad and Damascus, the strait mattered not as a passage between two shores but as the moat of the city that had stopped every eastern army since Xerxes, their own twice included. A strait can be named for those who cross it or for what stands in the way; Arabic chose the obstacle, and named the water after the prize.
