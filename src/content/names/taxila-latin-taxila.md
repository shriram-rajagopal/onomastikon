*Taxila* is the Latin form of the Greek name, entering Roman literature through the geographers' digest of the Alexander historians. Pliny the Elder, tracing the road down from the Hindu Kush in the *Natural History*, sets *Taxilla*, as his manuscripts double the consonant, in the flat country where the mountains give out onto the Indus plain, a "famous city" on the itinerary that Alexander's surveyors had measured and Rome still copied four centuries later. The name occurs in Latin as a stage on that inherited route, never as a place any Roman reports visiting.

The Latin entry closes the family's western transmission with a detail worth keeping: the doubled *ll* of Pliny's spelling, a scribe's ordinary wobble, is the only change three hundred years of Latin custody made to the name. Against the family's Indian branch, where the same span of time produced systematic vernacular reshaping and a traveling legend, the classical channel was an amber one; what it received from the Greek it delivered unaltered to the medieval geographers, and through them, eventually, to the trowels of modern archaeology.
