The medieval Latin name of Anatolia, *Turchia*, with the spellings *Turkia* and *Turquia*: the land of the Turks, the ethnonym of its new masters with the plain Latin country-suffix, whether formed within Latin from *Turci* or taken over from the Greek *Tourkía*. The name follows the Seljuk conquest at a generation's distance; by the crusading era Western itineraries were crossing a country they called *Turchia*, and William of Rubruck, riding home from the Mongol court in 1255, descends from Armenia into *Turkia* and takes ship from its southern coast as from any settled kingdom of the map. Cahen's standard history of the period wears the usage as its title: the Anatolia of these centuries is, in the West's own sources, simply pre-Ottoman Turkey.

The page's other names all define the land by someone else: the sunrise east of Greek *Anatolḗ*, seen from the Aegean; the Rome of Arabic and Persian *Rūm*, held over from the empire the Turks displaced. *Turchia* is the first name on the page to define Anatolia by who actually held it, and the atlas's clearest case of enemies agreeing about the facts on the ground: at the same moment the Seljuks themselves were styling their realm *Rūm*, claiming the land by its old possessor, the Latins named it for its new one. Each side reached for the other's identity, and the West's coinage won the longer game; carried through *Turchia* to Turkey, it is the only name born in the crusades still on every map of the region.
