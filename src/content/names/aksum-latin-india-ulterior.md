*India ulterior*, Further India, is what Rufinus of Aquileia called the kingdom of Aksum when he told, around 402, the story of its conversion. The philosopher Meropius of Tyre, sailing to "the further India, inhabited by many and various tongues and peoples," was killed on the coast; his two young wards, Frumentius and Aedesius, were taken to the king's court, rose in his service, and Frumentius, sent to Alexandria, was consecrated by Athanasius as the country's first bishop. The country is Aksum and the king's son is Ezana, as Athanasius's own correspondence confirms, but Rufinus never calls it so: in the Latin and Greek of Late Antiquity, India was the whole monsoon world, every shore the ships from Egypt reached, and the Red Sea's African coast was its nearer part. Socrates, Sozomen, and Theodoret copied the story from Rufinus, Indians and all.

The entry puts Aksum, for a century or two, inside the India family, and it does so by honest usage rather than error: Rufinus used the only word his geography had. The form shows how elastic the great names were at the edge of knowledge, where a Latin churchman could place a Christian kingdom in Africa on the far side of India and be understood. The Greek description Ethiopia would eventually settle on the highlands; the Latin church, for its part, first learned of the kingdom under the name of a subcontinent it was not in.
