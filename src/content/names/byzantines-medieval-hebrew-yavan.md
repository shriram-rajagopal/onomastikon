The biblical *Yāwān*, Greece, as the Hebrew of the twelfth century applied it to the living empire of Constantinople. Benjamin of Tudela, reaching the city in the late 1160s after five days' journey from Abydos, calls it the capital of all the land of *Yāwān*, "which is called Greece," the seat of the emperor Manuel and the twelve ministers who rule the land under him; at the far end of Anatolia he marks Malmistras, the ancient Mopsuestia, as the point to which the kingdom of the *Yəwānīm*, the Greeks, extends. The form is that of Genesis unchanged, vowels and all, and its grammar is the Bible's: a people's name doing duty for the people's land. What has changed is the thing it points to, the Roman empire of the East as a Jew from Navarre found it under the Komnenoi.

Every other name on this page descends from Rome, the name the empire insisted on for itself and the name its Arabic, Persian, Armenian, and Syriac neighbors all granted it. This one alone refuses it. Arabic and Persian had split the Greeks in two, *al-Yūnān* for the ancients and *al-Rūm* for the state; Hebrew made the opposite division, reserving *Edom* for Rome and Latin Christendom, so that in Benjamin's pages the Romans of Constantinople are Greeks and the Romans of Rome are Edom, and the empire that called itself Roman is the one place in his itinerary where the word Rome does not reach. The Ionian family's rabbinic station had frozen *Yāwān* at the moment of the Maccabees, a kingdom of sacred history with no living address; Benjamin thaws it, and for one traveler the oldest Hebrew name for the Greeks named a current state with an emperor in residence and a border two days' journey from Antioch.
