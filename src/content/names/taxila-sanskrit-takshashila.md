*Takṣaśilā* is the Sanskrit name of the city of Gandhāra, a compound whose second element is plainly *śilā*, "stone, rock," and whose first was explained variously by the tradition: from the root *takṣ-*, "to hew," giving "hewn stone," or from Takṣa, the son of Bharata who founds the city in the *Rāmāyaṇa*'s closing book, or from the nāga king of that name. The city appears across Sanskrit literature as the far northwest's metropolis, where the *Mahābhārata* has Janamejaya hold his snake sacrifice and where narrative literature routinely sends its princes to be educated.

The name is a rare case where the etymological legend, not the etymology, drove the transmission. The Buddhist retelling reshaped "the rock of Takṣa" into *taksha-śiras*, "the severed head," commemorating a birth in which the Bodhisattva gave his head at this spot, and it was that gruesome and edifying reading that the Chinese pilgrims translated into their own name for the site. Few names in this atlas show a folk etymology powerful enough to cross a language boundary intact while the sounds themselves stayed home.
