*ʾks¹mn*, the Aksumites, is the oldest written form of the kingdom's own name after the Greek sailors', and it comes from the other side of the Red Sea. The inscriptions of the Sabaean kings ʿAlhān Nahfān and Shaʿr Awtar, carved at the temple of Taʾlab on Jabal Riyām around the turn of the third century, record first an alliance with and then a war against Gadarat, "king of the Aksumites," *mlk ʾks¹mn*, the earliest Aksumite king known by name and the first to carry his arms into Arabia. The spelling is the city's name with the suffixed article of Sabaic, the form the language gave every definite noun, and it names the people rather than the place, as the Sabaic and Greek ethnics both tend to.

The entry is the Sabaic end of the Aksum family and the first notice of the kingdom as a power rather than a port. The *Periplus* had known a king and a metropolis at the head of an ivory road; the Yemeni inscriptions know an army, a treaty, and a war, and they know them because the Aksumites had crossed the sea. For the three centuries that followed, Sabaic was the language in which Aksum's Arabian ambitions were recorded, and the name the Sabaeans wrote was the native one, Aksum, not Ḥabašat: to the kings of Marib the enemy was the city on the highlands, named by its capital as they named themselves by theirs.
