*ḥbs²tn*, the Ḥabašat, is the name by which the Sabaeans called the people of the Aksumite highlands, and the headwater of the name that became Abyssinia. The same Jabal Riyām inscriptions that call Gadarat king of the Aksumites call him also *mlk ḥbs²tn*, king of the Ḥabašat, the two titles side by side around 200 CE; the Himyarite texts of the sixth century, when Aksumite armies had conquered Yemen, speak of the *ʾḥbs²n* as the occupying power, and Abraha, the Aksumite general who made himself king there, styles himself in the Sabaic of his own inscriptions an Ethiopian king. The origin of the word is unsettled, a South Arabian coinage for the highland peoples or, on one recent proposal, an Egyptian one; the Sabaic writing with the second sibilant is the earliest attestation there is.

The entry is where the page's third name begins. Aksum was the kingdom's word, Ethiopia the Greeks', and Ḥabašat the Arabians', and of the three it was the Arabians' that had the longest run, through Ezana's own titles, through the Arabic of the Hijra, and through Portuguese into every European language as Abyssinia. A name first carved by the kingdom's enemies across the sea outlasted the kingdom by a millennium and a half, and it did so because the kingdom accepted it: Ezana put the Sabaeans' word for his people into his own royal style, next to the name of his city.
