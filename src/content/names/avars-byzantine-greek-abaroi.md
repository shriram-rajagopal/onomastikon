*Ábaroi* enters Greek in 558 with the embassy of the qaghan Bayan's people to Justinian, long-haired envoys whose appearance astonished the capital, demanding subsidies and land; Menander's fragments record the negotiation and the decades of extortionate diplomacy that followed. The name itself became a Byzantine controversy: Theophylact Simocatta's famous excursus insists these Danube Avars were *Pseudábaroi*, false Avars, fugitive tribes who had stolen the terrifying name of the true Avars of the east, the Rouran whom the Türks had just destroyed, precisely because the name alone frightened nations into submission. Byzantine usage kept the name regardless, through the great siege of 626 down to the historians who recorded the qaghanate's fall.

The excursus makes this entry the atlas's most explicit ancient meditation on its own subject matter: a sixth-century historian arguing that an ethnonym could be looted like treasure, worn as armor by people with no right to it, because the name, not the nation, carried the power. Whether Theophylact's genealogy is sound is still debated, but his premise is the premise of this whole catalogue, stated fourteen centuries early. The Avars, true or false, ran a two-century empire on tribute and terror, and by the historian's own account the first thing they extorted was the name.
