*Kꜣš* is the Egyptian name of the land beyond the Second Cataract, written *k-ꜣ-š* with the three-hills determinative that Egyptian put on every foreign country. It first appears under the Twelfth Dynasty, in the records of the kings who fortified the cataracts in the early second millennium BCE, and it comes with an epithet attached: the boundary stela of Senusret III at Semna and the texts of the New Kingdom speak of *Kꜣš ẖst*, "wretched Kush," the standing formula for the southern enemy. When the New Kingdom conquered the south, the same word named the province, and the viceroy who governed it from the First Cataract to Napata carried the title King's Son of Kush, *sꜣ-nswt n Kꜣš*, through the whole of the empire period. The word is not Egyptian; it is the Egyptian spelling of a native name whose meaning is unknown, and whose relation to the Meroitic *qes* of a later millennium remains an open question. The reconstructed vowel follows the Akkadian and Hebrew transcriptions, which agree on a long *u*.

The entry is the headwater of the page's largest family. Every form in it, from the Assyrian annals to the Talmud, descends from these three signs, and the fact worth holding onto is the circumstances of the first writing: the name was set down by a hostile neighbor, on fortress walls and boundary stones, with a curse attached. It was then borne by an Egyptian viceroy, written by an Assyrian scribe recording the defeat of Taharqa, and taken into the Bible as the name of a son of Ham, losing the epithet along the way and keeping the name. The pharaohs wrote it with a curse, and everyone downstream took it as simply the name of the country; the only people who never used it, so far as their stones can be read, were the Kushites.
