The Middle Persian *Xyōn*, the Zoroastrian books' name for the steppe enemy, inherited directly from the Avestan *Xiiaona-* of the entry beside this one. In the epic *Ayādgār ī Zarērān* the Xyōn are the scriptural foe of Wištāsp's holy war; in the fourth century the name walked out of scripture into the field, for the *Chionitae* whom Ammianus watched besieging Amida in 359, allied troops of Šābuhr II under their king Grumbates, are the *Xyōn* in Greco-Latin dress, real steppe invaders of the Iranian northeast wearing the old sacred ethnonym. The apocalyptic *Zand ī Wahman Yasn* then counts the Xyōn among the peoples of the world's last ages. Book Pahlavi has no Unicode encoding, so the entry carries transliteration only; the disputed label covers the relation of these Chionites to the Huns of the West, which is precisely what scholarship cannot settle.

The entry documents the strangest mechanism on the page: Persia is the one civilization that did not need to borrow a name for the Huns, because its scripture had one waiting. Whether that is because the Xiiaona legend preserved a real ancient people whose descendants returned, or because priests fitted a new enemy into an old prophecy, or because the names *Xyōn* and *Hun* are at bottom the same word, is the eastern half of the identification dispute this page exists to hold. Rome met the Huns and invented monsters; Persia met them and recognized, or thought it recognized, something foretold.
