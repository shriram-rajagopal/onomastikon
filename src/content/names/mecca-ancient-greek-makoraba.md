*Makóraba* (Μακόραβα) is a town of inland Arabia in Ptolemy's *Geographia*, compiled around 150 CE, one of dozens of bare names with coordinates in his catalogue of the peninsula. Since the sixteenth century, European geographers have identified it with Mecca, and the identification hardened into fact in atlases and reference works; its supporters connected the name to Old South Arabian *mkrb*, read as "sanctuary," which would make Ptolemy's entry a pre-Islamic attestation of Mecca as a holy place. The identification, never actually argued so much as inherited, was systematically examined by Ian Morris in 2018 and found to rest on nothing: the coordinates fit poorly, the *mkrb* etymology is linguistically doubtful, and the resemblance between the names amounts to a shared *m* and *k*.

The entry is kept, labeled as disputed, because the dispute itself is the significant fact. If *Makóraba* is Mecca, the city has a name in the classical record five centuries before the Qur'an; if it is not, Mecca is unattested by any outside observer before Islam, an extraordinary silence for a supposedly ancient center of pilgrimage and trade, and one that historians of early Islam now weigh seriously. No other identification on this atlas carries such a load: a single line in an Alexandrian catalogue has had to bear, or fail to bear, the whole pre-Islamic history of a holy city. Five centuries of maps bet on the identification; the evidence, on inspection, was never there to bet with.
