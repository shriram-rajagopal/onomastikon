The Persian name of Anatolia, *Rūm*: the Roman name settled onto the land itself, so that when the Seljuk princes carved a state out of the peninsula they styled it the Sultanate of Rūm, and the greatest Persian poet of the age is known to half the world as Rūmī, "the man of Anatolia." In classical Persian usage the word does double duty, and the page's siblings show the split: on the Byzantines' page *Rūm* names the empire, while here it names the country, a land that kept the name of its old masters under its new ones.

The entry completes in Persian what the Arabic entry beside it records: Anatolia is the one great region of this atlas that the Islamic world named not for a people living there but for a people who had ruled and left. A Muslim, Turkish-speaking Anatolia remained *Rūm*, Rome, for centuries, in chancery style and poetry alike; the name outlived the empire, the border, and finally the religion of the land it described. Rome fell in Anatolia twice, to the Seljuks and the Ottomans, and both times the conquerors moved into the name along with the country, sultans of Rome in a Rome that Rome had lost.
