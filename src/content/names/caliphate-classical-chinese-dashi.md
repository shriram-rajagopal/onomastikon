*Dàshí* (大食) is the Chinese name for the Arabs and the caliphate, in use from the mid-seventh century through the Song. The Tang histories record the first Arab embassy arriving at Chang'an in 651, within twenty years of the Prophet's death, and carry accounts of the *Dàshí* realm, its dress, its worship, and its conquest of Persia. The characters read "great eat," but the meaning is no more Chinese than *Fúlǐn*'s: they transcribe the Iranian *Tāzīg*, the Persian word for the Arabs, delivered to China by the same Iranian middlemen who had carried Rome's name east. By Zhao Rukuo's *Zhufanzhi* of 1225 the *Dàshí* are a whole world with dependencies catalogued one by one, Baghdad and Mecca among them.

The symmetry with the Byzantines' page is exact and telling: China named both great empires of the medieval west, *Fúlǐn* and *Dàshí*, in Persian, because Persian was the language of the road between. The two names even met in battle once, at the Talas river in 751, the only time the caliphate's and China's armies faced each other, a collision the annals record under this borrowed Persian word. China had no direct word for the Arabs because it had almost no direct contact; what it had was the Silk Road, and the road spoke Persian. On this atlas Chang'an consistently sees the west through an Iranian interpreter, and *Dàshí* is the interpreter's word, kept for six centuries.
