The Arabic name for the crusaders and their Levantine states, *al-Ifranj*, "the Franks," with the settled lands as *bilād al-Ifranj*, the Franks' country. The word itself was old, the name of Charlemagne's people and their far country in the earlier geographers; what the crusades did was bring its referent to the next valley. From Ibn al-Athīr's great chronicle to Usāma ibn Munqidh's memoirs of a Syrian gentleman who hunted and argued with them for a lifetime, *al-Ifranj* is the standing term for the new neighbors, their kings, and their two centuries of kingdoms on the coast.

The entry pairs precisely with the Latin one beside it. The settlers' own name for their home, *partes transmarinae*, "the lands across the sea," located it from France looking east; *al-Ifranj* located the same states from Damascus looking west, as an extension of France that happened to be nearby. Both names agree on the essential point while disagreeing about everything else: that the states were fundamentally *from somewhere else*. In two centuries the crusader Levant never acquired, in the languages of the land around it, a name that was not a direction or an ethnonym; it was "overseas" to its settlers and "the Franks" to its neighbors, a country that existed for two hundred years without ever being a place.
