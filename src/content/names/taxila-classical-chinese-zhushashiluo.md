*Zhúchàshīluó* is the Chinese transcription of Taxila's name, recorded by the pilgrim Faxian, who passed through Gandhāra around 403 CE on his way to the Buddhist heartland. The four characters render a local pronunciation of the name syllable by syllable, and Faxian immediately adds the meaning he was taught on the spot: the name, he writes, signifies "the severed head," because here in a former birth the Bodhisattva gave his head to a suppliant, and the place was named from the deed. The great stupa of the head-gift stood at the site, one of the four famous stupa-legends of the northwest.

The entry is the rare case in this atlas of a folk etymology traveling as part of a name's baggage. Faxian's gloss translates the Buddhist reinterpretation of *Takṣaśilā* (as if *śiras*, "head," stood in the compound) that had grown up around the pilgrimage site itself; the Chinese name therefore preserves not what the name meant but what fourth-century Gandhāra believed it meant. A pilgrim's transcription plus a pilgrim's explanation, it is a snapshot of the city as sacred geography at the moment Chinese Buddhism first mapped it.
