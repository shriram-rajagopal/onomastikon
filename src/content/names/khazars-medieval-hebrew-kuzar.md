The Hebrew *Kūzār*, the form of the Khazars' name in the tenth-century Khazar Correspondence: Ḥasdai ibn Shapruṭ, the Jewish minister of the caliph of Córdoba, hearing that a Jewish kingdom stood by the Caspian, wrote across the world to its king, and the reply that circulated in Joseph's name recounts the land of the Kuzar, its rivers, its cities, and the famous story of the disputation by which its kings chose Judaism. Two centuries later Judah Halevi built his philosophical masterwork on that story, casting its argument for Judaism as the dialogue that persuaded the king, and his title fixed the Hebrew name permanently: the *Sefer ha-Kuzari*, the Book of the Khazar.

Nothing else in this atlas is quite this entry. Every other name on the page is a neighbor's word for the qaghanate; *Kūzār* is the name as the kingdom's own court wrote it, yet in a language that was no more natively Khazar than Greek was, adopted with the faith the ruling house had chosen. The correspondence is the closest thing to the Khazars' own voice that survives, and it speaks Hebrew from the Volga to Córdoba, diaspora and steppe empire discovering each other by post. A people whose own tongue is lost thus keeps its name in the atlas's records twice over as other people's word, and once, movingly, as its own word in a borrowed holy alphabet.
