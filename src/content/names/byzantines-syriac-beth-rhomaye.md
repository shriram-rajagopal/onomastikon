*Bēth Rhōmāyē* (ܒܝܬ ܪܗܘܡܝܐ), "the land of the Romans," is the Syriac name for the eastern empire's territory, formed with the toponymic prefix *bēth-*, "house, land of," on the gentilic *Rhōmāyē*, Romans. It is the standard geographical term of the Syriac chronicles and church documents: the chronicle attributed to Joshua the Stylite, written at Edessa around 507 in the middle of a Roman-Persian war, uses it throughout for the Roman side of the frontier, and the acts and correspondence of the Church of the East distinguish their own Persian territory from the bishops and fathers of *Bēth Rhōmāyē* to the west. The prefix builds the same construction as *Bēth Nahrīn* for Mesopotamia and *Bēth Sīnāyē* for China, the Syriac map assembled out of "houses."

What distinguishes this form is where its speakers stood. Syriac Christians lived on both sides of the world's most durable border, and for the Church of the East, seated inside the Sasanian and then the Islamic realm, *Bēth Rhōmāyē* was the near abroad: the empire next door that shared their scriptures, harbored their theological rivals, and fought their kings. After the fifth-century schisms the phrase carried a confessional edge as well, since the church of the Roman land was the church of the emperor's councils. Most of this page names Byzantium from a single vantage; *Bēth Rhōmāyē* names it from a community that could see it from outside while praying, in the same language, on both sides of the line.
