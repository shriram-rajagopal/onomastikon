The oldest name on this page by six centuries: *Rṯnw*, Retjenu, the Egyptian name for the Syro-Palestinian lands, attested from the Middle Kingdom and carried through the imperial itineraries of the New Kingdom. Its first great appearance is literary, for when Sinuhe flees Egypt in the twelfth-dynasty tale, it is the ruler of Upper Retjenu who takes him in, gives him a daughter and a frontier, and hears his homesick poetry; five centuries later the annals of Thutmose III count the same country in campaigns, tribute, and town-lists, dividing an Upper Retjenu around the Canaanite hills from a Lower Retjenu toward the Phoenician coast. The name's own etymology is unknown, and it belongs to no family on this page; Egypt's word predates and ignores the Aram and Assur roots that would later contend for the region.

Every other name here comes from inside the Semitic world or from Greeks renaming it; *Rṯnw* is the view from the Nile, the one great neighbor that named the country before any of the page's other namers existed as powers. It is also the page's reminder that a name can be a genre: Retjenu is where Egyptian literature put exile, where its armies put glory, and where its accountants put tribute, three registers of one word across six hundred years. When the name finally faded with the Bronze Age, the country passed to other people's words for it; Sinuhe's refuge is the region's oldest name and the only one that remembers it as somewhere an Egyptian could be homesick.
