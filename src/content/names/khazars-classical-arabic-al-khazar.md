The Arabic *al-Khazar*, the geographers' and historians' name for the qaghanate the caliphate could conquer least of all its neighbors: the Arab-Khazar wars of the eighth century twice carried armies through the Caucasus in each direction and ended in a standing frontier at Darband. The classical geographers then mapped the people in detail, al-Iṣṭakhrī describing the twin city of Atil on its river, Ibn Faḍlān reporting the strange dual kingship of sacred qaghan and working king, and al-Masʿūdī, writing within living memory of the fact, recording that the king of the Khazars had become a Jew in the days of Hārūn al-Rashīd. From the people's name the Arabic tradition also named the sea, *Baḥr al-Khazar*, the form the Caspian's page of this atlas already holds.

The entry sits at the head of the name's most durable line. Arabic fixed *al-Khazar* into the standard geography of the world, Persian carried it on, and the sea-name outlived the qaghanate by a millennium: the Caspian is still the Khazar Sea in Persian and Turkish. The people themselves the Arabic tradition treated with the respect of an unresolved war, a power beyond the Gate whose conversion it recorded without polemic, one book-religion noting, almost as a colleague, that the steppe had chosen another.
