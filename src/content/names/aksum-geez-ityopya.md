*ʾItyoṗya* is the Greek *Aithiopía* in Geʽez letters, taken into the language when the Bible was translated from Greek in the fifth and sixth centuries and standing wherever the Septuagint had written Ethiopia for the Hebrew Kush. The verse that mattered most was the Psalter's: "Ethiopia shall stretch out her hands to God," which a Christian kingdom on the highlands, hearing the Greek word its own kings had used for themselves, took as a prophecy about itself. The spelling preserves the Greek consonants with the ejective *ṗ* for the Greek *p*, and the Geʽez Bible's usage became the literary one: by the time the *Kebra Nagast* set down the national legend of the Solomonic line, Ityoṗya was simply what the country was called, and the Queen of Sheba was its queen.

The entry is the point at which a borrowed name became an identity. Ezana's Greek scribes had equated Ethiopia with Ḥabašat as a translator's convenience; the Bible made the equation scripture, and scripture made it destiny. Every mention of Cush in the Old Testament, the Cushite wife of Moses, the Ethiopian eunuch of Acts, the kingdom that would stretch out its hands, was now a mention of the highlands, and a kingdom with its own name adopted another's because the holy book appeared to speak of it. The name of Kush had traveled through Greek to become the name of the kingdom that ended Kush; the Ethiopia of today is the living result, a country named by the Greek word for its ancient rival.
