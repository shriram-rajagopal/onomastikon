The Armenian name of Egypt, *Egiptos*, taken directly from the Greek *Aígyptos* in the first great act of Armenian literature: the fifth-century translation of the Bible, begun within a generation of Maštocʿ's invention of the alphabet. Egypt saturates the translated scriptures, from Joseph to the Exodus, and the Greek name came over with them, its ending intact; the form has served Armenian ever since.

On a page that runs from *Kemet* through the Semitic *mṣr* family, *Egiptos* marks the third route by which the world learned Egypt's name, the Greek one, reaching a country that had no border with Egypt and no reason to name it except the reason that mattered most in the fifth century: scripture. Armenia's neighbors to the south knew Egypt as *Miṣr* through trade and conquest; Armenian knew it through Genesis. It is a reminder visible across the whole Armenian column of this atlas that a language's map of the world is drawn by its libraries as much as its borders, and Armenia's first library was the Bible: Egypt entered Armenian not as a country up the road but as the house of bondage in a book.
