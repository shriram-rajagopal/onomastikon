The land's own name for itself, *māt Šumeri u Akkadi*, "the land of Sumer and Akkad," written in the Cyrus Cylinder *šu-me-ri ú ak-ka-di-i*. The double formula descends from the royal title "king of Sumer and Akkad" that the kings of Ur took up at the end of the third millennium, and it remained the standing designation of the Babylonian heartland for fifteen hundred years, through the title of Hammurabi's successors and the Neo-Babylonian kings down to 539, when Cyrus the Persian, taking Babylon, took the old title with it and styled himself king of the land of Sumer and Akkad like every legitimate king before him.

Every other name on this page looks at the country from outside and sees rivers: the Greek land "between the rivers," the Hebrew "Aram of the two rivers," the Syriac and Armenian calques, the Arabic "island" between the streams. The native name never mentions water at all. It names the land by its two ancient peoples, and it kept naming them long after one of them had vanished: Sumerian had been dead as a spoken language for a millennium while kings still claimed its half of the formula, so that every coronation recited the name of a people no living person had heard speak. The outsiders named the geography; the land itself named its ancestors, and would not stop.
