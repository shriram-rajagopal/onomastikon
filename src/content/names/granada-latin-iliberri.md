*Iliberri* is the ancient name of the town on Granada's hill, attested on its coinage and in Pliny's survey, which records it as a Latin-right municipality with the honorific Florentinum; the spelling wobbles across sources, Iliberri, Illiberis, Eliberri, as Latin tried to hold a foreign shape. The name is pre-Roman, and its usual explanation is one of the most suggestive in Iberia: it is commonly read as an old Basque-type phrase, *ili berri*, "new town," the same elements that survive in the Pyrenean Elne, anciently Illiberis, and in modern Basque *hiri berri*. Around 305 the town hosted the Council of Elvira, the earliest church council whose canons survive, and its later name-form Elvira clung to the district into the Islamic period.

Two things earn the entry its place. First, the identification: that ancient Iliberri stood on the Albaicín hill of Granada itself, long contested against sites in the plain, is now the general view, supported by excavation, and it makes Granada's page reach a millennium behind its Arabic name; the prose records the debate because the older literature still carries it. Second, the meaning: if the "new town" reading holds, then the last capital of al-Andalus stands on a site whose first recorded name already meant what every Naples and Novgorod on this atlas means. The city that ended Iberia's Islamic centuries began, in another millennium and another language family entirely, as somebody's Newtown.
