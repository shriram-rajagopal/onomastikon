The Latin name of the island, *Tylos*, taken directly from the Greek and used by Pliny the Elder in his survey of the Gulf coast, where he sets Tylos among the islands off Arabia and reports it famous for its pearls; in his botanical books the same island returns for its wool-bearing trees, the cotton notice inherited from Theophrastus and Androsthenes. Rome never sailed these waters in any strength, and Latin knowledge of the Gulf is secondhand Greek knowledge throughout, so the form is a bookish one, at home in encyclopedic geography rather than in any Roman's sailing directions.

Latin *Tylos* matters less for what it adds than for where it carries the name: into the encyclopedia that medieval and early modern Europe read. Pliny's notice, pearls and cotton-trees around a far island, is the shape in which the West remembered the Gulf's holy isle until modern archaeology recovered Dilmun itself, and when nineteenth-century scholars first connected Bahrain's mounds to the Dilmun of the tablets, it was partly the classical Tylos, sitting in Pliny at the same coordinates, that let the identification close. A name that began in archaic Uruk ends its ancient run as two syllables in a Roman natural history, waiting two millennia to be recognized.
