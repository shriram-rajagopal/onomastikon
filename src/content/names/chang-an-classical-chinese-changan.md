*Cháng'ān*, "perpetual peace," is the Chinese name of the Han capital, bestowed when the dynasty's founder fixed his seat in the Wei valley in 202 BCE; the histories record the choice, and the name belongs to the established repertoire of programmatic Chinese place-names in which a government writes its hopes onto the landscape. Through the Han centuries the name meant the metropolis itself, its palaces, markets, and imperial university; the *Shiji* and *Hanshu*, both written in the city, are its fundamental attestations.

The name is the page's only self-designation and the only one that means anything: the road's name for the city, *Khumdān*, is an opaque label, but *Cháng'ān* is a legible political sentence, and its irony was visible within a generation, as the city fell to rebellion, fire, and the Xiongnu wars that the Sogdian letter on this page reports. The wish outlasted every failure of it. Restored as the Tang capital's name, borrowed into Japanese for Kyoto's ceremonial districts, *Cháng'ān* remained East Asia's byword for the ideal capital, perpetual peace as a permanent aspiration rather than a fact.
