*Palibothra* is the Latin form of the city's name, taken over unchanged from the Greek of Megasthenes' tradition. Pliny the Elder, cataloguing the peoples of the Ganges in the *Natural History*, names the Prasii as the greatest of them and *Palibothra* as their capital, so eminent, he adds, that some writers call the whole people Palibothri; he repeats the itinerary measurements the Greek ambassadors had recorded and locates the city at the great river-junction. The name appears in Latin only as geography, never from contact: no Roman is known to have seen the city.

The entry belongs to the standard Greek-to-Latin channel that carried eastern names into Rome's world-picture, but with the transmission run at maximum length: a Prakrit street-name, heard by a Seleucid Greek, quoted by a geographer, excerpted by a Roman encyclopedist, each step further from the Ganges. Pliny wrote four centuries after Megasthenes, and his Palibothra is a city of the reign of Candragupta frozen in amber, still walled in wood and counting its towers long after the real city had passed to other dynasties. The Latin name preserves not the city but the embassy's memory of it.
