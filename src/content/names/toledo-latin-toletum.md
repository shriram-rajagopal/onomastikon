*Toletum* is the Latin name of Toledo, first attested in Livy's account of the campaigns of 193 BCE, with the remark that it was then a small city but strong by its position; the name is pre-Roman, presumably Celtiberian, and unexplained. The small city grew into the hub of the central plateau's roads, and under the Visigoths became the royal city of the kingdom, the seat of the councils of Toletum whose acts governed church and crown alike; by the end of the Latin file's window the name denoted the capital of Christian Spain.

The interest of the Latin stage is how far the city outran Livy's dismissal while the name held still. Every promotion the place received, road-hub, bishopric, royal capital, happened under the same unglossed Celtiberian syllables the legions had recorded when it was a hill fort; the Visigoths, who renamed nothing anywhere, made a pre-Roman name the title of Iberia's first unified kingdom. That gave the name prestige that the next conquerors would preserve in their own mouth as *Ṭulayṭula*, and the one after that as *Toledo*. Livy's "parva urbs" is the atlas's reminder that a name's first attestation is a floor, not a forecast: the small city he footnoted ended as the word for where Spain's kings were crowned.
