*Palíbothra* is the Greek name of Pataliputra, carried west by Megasthenes, the Seleucid ambassador resident at Candragupta's court around 300 BCE, whose lost *Indica* survives in the quotations of Strabo and Arrian. Megasthenes described the city concretely: a parallelogram along the river some eighty stadia by fifteen, palisaded in wood with five hundred and seventy towers and sixty-four gates, the greatest city in India. Strabo spells the name *Palíbothra*; Arrian's excerpts give *Palímbothra* with a nasal, and both shapes render a Middle Indo-Aryan pronunciation of the name rather than the Sanskrit, its *-putra* heard as *-bothra*.

The form is the family's best evidence that Greek ears recorded the spoken vernacular, not the court language: the aspirate wandered, the vowels blurred, but the giveaway voiced *b* for *p* and the cluster's collapse mark a name learned in conversation. For all its distortion the transcription anchored India in Western geography for centuries; Ptolemy still plots the city by it, and when eighteenth-century Europeans sought the lost capital of the Mauryas, it was Megasthenes' *Palibothra*, matched at last to Patna, that guided the search.
