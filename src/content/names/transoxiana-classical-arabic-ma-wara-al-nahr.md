*Mā warāʾ al-Nahr* (ما وراء النهر), "what lies beyond the river," is the Arabic name of the lands past the Oxus, standard from the conquests of the early eighth century and treated as a province-name by the geographers, Yāqūt among them, who file it as a single headword despite its being, grammatically, a whole relative clause. The river needs no name in the phrase because for the Arabic geographical tradition the Oxus, their Jayḥūn, was the river of the east, the boundary between Iran and Turan that Persian epic had drawn long before Islam. The name remains alive, continued in the Uzbek and Tajik *Movarounnahr*.

The entry is the atlas's purest specimen of a name that is entirely point of view. It contains no place, no people, no feature of the land it names, only a preposition and a boundary, and it cannot be used without standing, mentally, on the southwestern bank; the region's own older name, Sogdiana, sits one entry away on this atlas facing the other direction. Europe then compounded the perspective rather than correcting it, coining *Transoxiana*, "across the Oxus," a Latin translation of the Arabic vantage by people even further away. Half the world's map-names work this way and hide it; this one shows its work. Nobody who lived beyond the river ever called it that, and everybody else did, which is how the phrase became, and remains, its name.
