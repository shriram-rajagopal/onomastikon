The Lycian name of Athens and its people, taken from the Greek *Athênai* and written twice on the inscribed pillar of Xanthos, the longest of all Lycian texts. In the narrative of the north face the form *Atãnas* stands whole: *se Parzza: Xbide: se Sp[part]ali[j]ahe: trbbi: Atãnas: zxxãte*, "when the Persians at Kaunos and the troops of the Spartans fought against the Athenians." A second occurrence, the ethnic adjective *atãna[zi]*, sits in a row of peoples on the east face beside the Ionians and the Spartans. The nasalized *ã*, a vowel the Lycian alphabet writes with its own letter, carries the Greek name into Anatolian phonology.

Athens enters the Lycian record as an enemy. The pillar is the boast of a dynast who fought for the Persian side in the years Thucydides describes, when the Athenian general Melesander sailed to Lycia to collect tribute and died there in battle; the Lycian text counts the Athenians among the powers its author faced, not among the peoples he traded or prayed with. The page's Mycenaean entry knows the city as *a-ta-na* a thousand years earlier, and the Lycian consonants echo that older shape almost exactly. Most names in this atlas survive because someone wished to reach the place they name; *Atãnas* survives because someone wished to say he had beaten it.
