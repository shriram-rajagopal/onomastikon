The Armenian name of Jerusalem, *Erusałēm*, from the Greek Bible's indeclinable *Ierousalēm*, the form the Septuagint and the Gospels kept close to the Hebrew, rather than the hellenized *Hierosolyma* of the geographers that this page records beside it. The fifth-century translators took the scriptural form, and the dark *ł* that Armenian set where Greek wrote lambda gives the name its unmistakably Armenian sound. Armenians did not stay readers only: by the early Middle Ages an Armenian quarter stood in Jerusalem itself, and it stands there still, the oldest of the city's diaspora communities.

Within the *yerushalem* family the Armenian form is a witness for the scriptural branch: Greek held two Jerusalems, the transliterated one of its Bible and the Grecized one of its administrators, and each language downstream chose its parent. Latin's church took both; Armenian, whose literature began as translation, never hesitated, taking the Bible's form because for Armenian there was no other register to take it from. The choice has outlived every empire on this page's timeline, for the community that prays in Armenian at St. James still calls the city by the name the translators set down: a page of this atlas where the name's fifth-century form can be heard on its own streets today.
