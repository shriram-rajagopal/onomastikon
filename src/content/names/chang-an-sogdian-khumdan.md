*ʾxwmtʾn*, conventionally read *Khumdān*, is the Sogdian name of Chang'an, attested in the second of the Ancient Letters, written by the merchant Nanai-vandak around 313 CE and never delivered. The letter reports catastrophe in China, famine in the capital, the sack of Ye and Luoyang, the emperor's flight, and names Khumdān among the cities of the disaster; the initial aleph of the spelling is the script's way into a vowel (the second is partly restored in the manuscript, printed ʾxwmt(ʾ)n), and the name's own origin is disputed, with no agreed Chinese or Iranian etymology despite long argument. It is, whatever its source, the road's name for the city, used by the people who carried the road's trade.

The form is the headwater of the page's second family: for half a millennium after Nanai-vandak, travelers from the west called the city Khumdān and not Chang'an, the Byzantine *Khoubdán* on this page descending from exactly this Iranian shape, and the same name later greeting readers of the Nestorian monument. A capital so great that it bore two names at once, one for the empire that ruled it and one for the road that reached it, and the road's name was the one the world to the west knew.
