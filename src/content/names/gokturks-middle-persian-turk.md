The Middle Persian *Turk*, written *twlk'* in Book Pahlavi, borrowed from the Turkic self-name once the qaghanate reached the Sasanian frontier in the sixth century. The word belongs above all to the apocalyptic and geographical layers of the Zoroastrian books: the *Zand ī Wahman Yasn* counts the Turks among the invading peoples whose irruptions mark the ages of the world's decline, alongside Arabs and Romans, the frontier experience of the late empire recast as prophecy. Book Pahlavi has no Unicode encoding, so the entry carries transliteration only.

The borrowing carries an irony the Zoroastrian books themselves institutionalized. Persian tradition already possessed an ancient name for the steppe enemy, *Tūrān*, from the Avesta's *Tūra*; when actual Turks appeared, the two names' likeness proved irresistible, and the tradition folded the new people into the old category, Turks into Turanians, an equation Firdawsi's epic would fix permanently in the Persian imagination, as the entries for Scythia and the Persian *Turk* on this page record. This Middle Persian word is where the confusion begins: the last generation of Zoroastrian scholarship met the first generation of Turks, and mistook them, creatively, for an enemy their scripture had always known.
