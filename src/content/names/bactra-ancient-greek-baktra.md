*Báktra* is the Greek name of the city, a neuter plural distinguished in Greek usage from *Baktrianḗ*, the land; Herodotus already uses it in the fifth century BCE, when Masistes flees the Persian court by the road to Bactra, and for him the city is simply where the eastern empire's power sits. Strabo, drawing on the historians of Alexander and the Greek kingdom that followed, lists Bactra first among the Bactrian cities and records its other name, *Zariaspa*, a doublet that has exercised geographers since. Under the Greek kings of Bactria the city on Greek lips was a capital of their own.

The form's interest lies in that shift of standpoint. Most Greek names for eastern places in this atlas are horizon-words for somewhere Greeks had heard of; *Báktra* began that way in Herodotus and then, uniquely among them, became the seat of a Greek throne for two centuries, a name Greeks wrote home from rather than about. The city-form also anchored the whole family in the West: Latin took it over unchanged, and it is the reason the modern language of scholarship says Bactria at all.
