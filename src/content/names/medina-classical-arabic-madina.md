*al-Madīna* (المدينة), "the City," is the name Yathrib acquired with the hijra, understood as the clipped form of *madīnat al-nabī*, "the city of the Prophet." The Qur'an already uses it, naming "the people of al-Madīna" in its ninth sura, and it displaced the old name almost immediately and permanently. The common noun beneath it has its own history: *madīna* is at root an Aramaic word, *mdintā*, "jurisdiction, province, city," from the Semitic root of judging, one of the many Aramaic administrative terms naturalized in Arabic; so the city of the Prophet bears, at bottom, the ordinary bureaucratic word for a town that Arabia had absorbed from the older world to its north.

The entry closes a pattern that spans the atlas. On the Byzantium page, the Greeks of the medieval empire called their capital *hē Pólis*, "the City," a phrase so habitual it eventually hardened into Istanbul; here, in the same centuries, the first city of Islam became *al-Madīna*, "the City," by the same act of language. Two faiths, two empires, no contact between the two usages, and one identical solution: when a city becomes singular enough in the minds of its speakers, description gives up and the definite article does the naming. The humblest word on this page, a borrowed common noun for "town," turned out to be the strongest; it has named the Prophet's city for fourteen hundred years without ever needing to say more.
