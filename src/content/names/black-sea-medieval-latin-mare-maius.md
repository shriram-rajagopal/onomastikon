The medieval Latin name of the Black Sea, *Mare Maius*, "the Greater Sea." William of Rubruck opens his *Itinerarium* of 1253 with it: he entered "the Sea of Pontus, which is commonly called *Mare Maius*," his *vulgariter* marking the name as the working usage of the sailors who carried him, the Latin of the Italian mariners' *mare Maggiore*. The name belongs to the great age of Genoese and Venetian commerce in the sea, and it is the standard label of the portolan charts, the practical harbor-to-harbor maps of the Mediterranean sailing world; "greater" measures the sea against the small Sea of Azov beyond its strait, and against the familiar basins of the sailors' home sea.

The page's Greek entry records a name of fear managed by flattery, *Póntos Eúxeinos*, the Hospitable Sea, the euphemism that renamed an *Áxeinos*, an Inhospitable one. The medieval name has no such literature in it. It is a measurement, coined on deck by men who crossed the sea for freight and needed to distinguish it from the smaller water next door; Rubruck learned it not from a book but from the ship he boarded. Between the two names lies the whole difference in how the sea was held: the Greeks approached it as colonists propitiating a power, the Italians worked it as a route. The sea the ancients flattered, the medievals simply sized.
