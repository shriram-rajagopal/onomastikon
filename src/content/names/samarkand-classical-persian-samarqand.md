*Samarqand* (سمرقند) in Classical Persian shares its written shape with the Arabic, but its channel is local: Persian rose to literary life in the very courts of Transoxiana and Khurasan for which Samarqand was a home city, not a foreign wonder, and the name passed into the new language from the region's own speech as Sogdian itself faded before Persian and Turkic. The city appears in the *Shāhnāma*'s Turanian geography and across the classical literature, and at the era's end it became the capital of Timur, who piled the plunder of half the world into it and made the name, for Persian letters and every literature touching them, a synonym for imperial splendor at the edge of the earth.

The Persian entry records the name's change of register. In Sogdian mouths *Smārkanθ* was home; in Arabic, a provincial metropolis; Persian made it an image, the golden city of the east, a career crowned centuries later when European poetry borrowed the Persian resonance whole, the "golden road to Samarkand" being the Persian city of the imagination rather than the Sogdian city of stone. The family's plainest etymology, stone fort, thus ends in the least stony of meanings; by the time the name reached its widest audience it referred to a dream. Cities are named by their founders and renamed by their poets, without a letter needing to change.
