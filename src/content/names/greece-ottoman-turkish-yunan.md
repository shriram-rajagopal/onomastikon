*Yûnân* (يونان) is the Ottoman Turkish name for the ancient Greeks and their land, inherited through the Persian literary culture that the early Anatolian courts read, and itself the Arabic *Yūnān* that goes back through Syriac to the Ionians. It is the word of the earliest Ottoman letters: Ahmedî's *İskendernâme*, the first Turkish telling of the Alexander Romance, made İskender the king of *Yûnân* and its philosophers his teachers, so that the Greece that entered Old Anatolian Turkish did so as the homeland of Alexander and of wisdom, a country of the learned past rather than a neighbor on the map.

*Yûnân* completes into the Ottoman layer the split that the Islamic world had drawn between two Greeces. The same courts that called the ancient Greeks *Yûnân* called the living empire at Constantinople *Rûm*, exactly as Arabic divided *al-Yūnān* from *al-Rūm* and Persian *Yūnān* from *Rūm*: the Greeks of philosophy and the Greeks of the frontier sorted into two words that never met. The atlas records the Ottoman *Rûm* on the Byzantines' page and *Yûnân* here, the pair inherited whole from the Persian and Arabic before them, so that at the atlas's closing date the oldest of the eastern names for Greece and the youngest are the same name, the Ionians reaching the Turks a full two thousand years after the Assyrians first wrote them down.
