*Yǎncài* 奄蔡 enters the Chinese record with Zhang Qian's report of his western journey in the second century BCE, preserved in the *Shiji*: a nomad state two thousand *li* northwest of Kangju, on a great marshy lake without shores, which the Han histories place at the northwestern limit of the known world. The standard identification reads *Yǎncài* as the Aorsi, the easternmost great Sarmatian confederation of the Caspian and Aral steppes, on the fit of geography, way of life, and the transcription's plausible echo of the same name; the identification is well entrenched but not demonstrable, and the disputed label records that. The *Hou Hanshu* adds the decisive later note: Yǎncài had changed its name to *Ālánliáo*, the form treated on the Alans' page. Chinese IPA is omitted per this atlas's policy for reconstructed ancient readings.

The entry is the point where the Chinese and Greco-Roman world-maps, drawn from opposite ends of Eurasia, touch the same nomads. Strabo's Aorsi trading down the Caspian coasts and Zhang Qian's lakeside state are, on the standard reading, one people described by two empires that did not know of each other's descriptions. And the *Hou Hanshu*'s rename is the page's single most valuable sentence of foreign testimony: a Chinese clerk, noting an administrative name-change, unknowingly recorded the historical moment the Sarmatian order was consolidating into the Alanic one, the transition the rest of this atlas can only infer from the west.
