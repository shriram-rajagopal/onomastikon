*Marákanda* (Μαράκανδα) is the Greek name of Samarkand, fixed by the historians of Alexander: Arrian names it as the royal city of Sogdiana with the ruler's palace, taken in 329 BCE, and Curtius describes its long walls. It was Alexander's base for the hard Sogdian campaigns, and the scene of the expedition's darkest evening, the banquet at which the king killed Cleitus the Black; the city thus enters western literature attached to a moral exemplum, quoted for centuries by philosophers who never asked where Marakanda was. The Greek form renders the local name heard in speech, with its first sibilant lost at the doorstep; the entry claims no written parent, since the Sogdian spelling surfaces in the record only centuries later.

The entry's philological interest is that the exonym is older than any native attestation, by more than six centuries. Greek caught the name in 329 BCE; the earliest surviving Sogdian writing of it belongs to the Ancient Letters of the fourth century CE, so the city's own documentation runs behind its conqueror's. The atlas meets this inversion at Sogdiana itself, whose Achaemenid attestations precede the native ones, and it is the standard predicament of oral civilizations conquered by literate ones: the visitors wrote first. Marakanda is Samarkand as the west first heard it, one syllable short and one murder richer, and for the ancient Mediterranean that abbreviation was the whole city.
