The Akkadian name of Magan, *Makkan*, the same name as the Sumerian in a Semitic mouth, written for the most part with the same *má-gan* signs. Its most dramatic attestations are military: Naram-Sin of Akkad records marching against Magan and capturing Manium, its lord, the only time a Mesopotamian king claims to have conquered the far side of the Lower Sea, and booty of Magan stone was carved with his name. Thereafter the name follows the trade until the trade dies, and then does something stranger: it refuses to die itself. The Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions revive *Makkan* as a grand archaism, no longer for Oman but for Egypt, so that Esarhaddon, conqueror of the Nile, can style himself king of the kings of Muṣur, Paturisi, and Kūsi and have the scribes gloss the far southwest as Magan and Meluhha, the edge-of-the-world words of the Sargonic golden age.

*Makkan* is this atlas's clearest case of a name outliving its referent and then drifting clean off it. For the scribes of Nineveh the word no longer pointed at a place; it pointed at an idea, the uttermost south reachable by ships, and when Egypt became that uttermost south the old name simply slid west to cover it, a deliberate echo of Naram-Sin by kings who wanted his stature. The drift is also the strongest internal evidence that by the seventh century BCE no one in Mesopotamia any longer knew, in any practical way, where old Magan had been, which is exactly the darkness out of which the Achaemenid *Maka*, treated later on this page, so intriguingly surfaces.
