The Medieval Latin *Avari*, the name under which the Frankish annals narrate the qaghanate's destruction. The Royal Frankish Annals track Charlemagne's Avar wars of the 790s to the capture of the Ring, the qaghanate's treasure-fortress between Danube and Tisza, and Einhard's life of the emperor pauses over the result: fifteen wagons of gold and silver hauled to Aachen, all the plunder of two centuries of Byzantine tribute changing hands in a season, so that the Franks, in his phrase, who had been almost poor, became rich. Frankish usage often glosses the people as the Huns of old, the new steppe enemy filed under the ancient name, but *Avari* is the annalists' working term.

The entry is the only one on the page written by the people who ended its subject. Greek named the Avars at their arrival and through their extortions; Latin named them chiefly to record their obliteration, and the name's densest cluster of attestations is an inventory of loot. Within a century a Frankish chronicler could cite a proverb of the region: they vanished like the Avars, who have neither offspring nor successor. A steppe empire that left no writing of its own thus survives in Latin mainly as the measure of what was taken from it, its name preserved, like its treasure, in the victor's accounts.
