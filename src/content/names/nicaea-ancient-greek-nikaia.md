*Níkaia* (Νίκαια) is the Greek name of the city from its Hellenistic founding: established around 310 BCE by Antigonus as an Antigoneia, it was promptly taken and renamed by Lysimachus for his wife Nikaia, her name itself built on *níkē*, victory. Strabo describes the city's perfect rectangular plan, all four gates visible from the central stone; Stephanus catalogues it among the many cities of the name, for queens called Victoria were not rare. The councils of 325 and 787 made this Nikaia the one that mattered, fixing the name in the permanent vocabulary of Christian doctrine, and after 1204 the empire-in-exile made it briefly a capital, the "empire of Nicaea" of the historians.

The name's fortune is the entry's study. Of the two founders' names the city bore in its first decade, the loser's queen outlasted the winner's dynasty, an accident; but what made the accident permanent was the church, which attached the name to a creed recited continuously for seventeen centuries, giving a Bithynian lake town the widest circulation of any city-name of its size on this atlas. Nikaia the woman is otherwise a footnote of the successor wars; her name is said aloud, in a hundred languages, every Sunday. Emperors on this atlas name cities to be remembered and are forgotten; a queen was remembered because a committee met in hers.
