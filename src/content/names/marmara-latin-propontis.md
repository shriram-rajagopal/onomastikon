*Propontis* is the Latin form of the Greek name, borrowed whole; Pliny's geography walks the reader up the same corridor as the Greek pilots, Hellespont, Propontis, Bosporus, Pontus, and the poets use the word for the sea of the Argonauts' outbound voyage, since the epic route to Colchis ran through it. Latin never translated the name, though it easily could have; *pro* and *pontus* were both at home in Latin, and the loan arrived almost pre-parsed, a Greek compound a Roman reader could read.

That untranslatability-by-neglect is the entry's small distinction. Elsewhere on the atlas Latin translates Greek sea-names or replaces them; here it kept a name whose meaning depends on another sea's name, because *Propontis* only works if *Pontus* stands behind it, and Latin had adopted that too. The pair travelled together, a name and its reference point, one of the few cases where borrowing one toponym obliged a language to borrow a second. When the Latin west later needed the sea in crusading itineraries, the old borrowed name was still there, doing what it had always done: telling the traveler what came next.
