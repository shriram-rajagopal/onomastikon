The far-western sea-name of the Hebrew Bible, *Taršîš*: the place Jonah flees toward, the source of Tyre's silver in Ezekiel's lament, the destination of the deep-water "ships of Taršîš" that became Hebrew's generic term for ocean-going vessels. Where it lay is among the oldest unsettled questions in biblical geography, with Tartessos in Spain the leading candidate and Tarsus in Cilicia a persistent rival; its claim to this page rests on the oldest interpreters of all. The Septuagint's translators, working in the third and second centuries BCE when Carthage ruled the western sea, rendered *Taršîš* as *Karchēdōn*, Carthage, in Isaiah's oracle against Tyre, and made Ezekiel's merchants of Taršîš into Carthaginians; for Hellenistic Jewish readers, Tyre's far-western partner could only be Tyre's own great colony.

The identification is probably wrong as philology and perfectly reasonable as history, and that is its interest. By the translators' day the actual Taršîš, wherever it had been, was centuries gone, while Carthage sat exactly where the prophets' western horizon pointed, a Phoenician-speaking metropolis at the far end of the same trade routes; the LXX simply updated the map. The entry thus records a name Carthage wore only in translation, assigned by readers matching an old text to their own sea. Every other name on the city's page was given by people who knew Carthage; this one was inherited from a prophecy about somewhere else, the west end of the world relabeled for its newest landlord.
