Latin *Romania* is the twin of the Greek *Rhōmanía* above, and its peer in age: the word appears in Latin writers of the later fourth and fifth centuries and in Jordanes's sixth-century *Getica*, meaning the Roman world as a country, the ordered territory of the empire as against the *barbaricum* beyond the frontier. Its most famous early appearance is a renunciation: Orosius reports the Gothic king Athaulf's boast that he had once meant to erase the Roman name and make *Gothia* of what had been *Romania*, before deciding to restore the Roman order instead. It is a late formation from *Romanus*, built exactly like the Greek word, and the two arose together in the fourth-century empire, one name in its two languages; which language coined it first cannot be settled from the attestation, and the entry makes no claim.

This is the only Latin form on the Byzantines' page, and it marks the moment the empire's name came loose from the city of Rome: *Romania* is a country that exists wherever Roman order runs, and after 476 that was, increasingly, the east. Its afterlife runs in two directions. In Italy the name contracted around the territory that held to Constantinople while the Lombards took the rest, and survives today as *Romagna*, the region around Ravenna, a Byzantine province fossilized in an Italian place-name. In the east the Greek twin carried the meaning forward to 1453. A word coined to mean "everywhere Roman" ended as the name of the places that stayed Roman longest.
