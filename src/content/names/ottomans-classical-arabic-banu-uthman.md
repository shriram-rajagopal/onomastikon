*Banū ʿUthmān* (بنو عثمان), "the sons of ʿUthmān," is the Arabic name for the Ottoman house, the usage of the Mamluk chancery and the Arabic historians of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries; al-Qalqashandī's great secretarial manual, compiled in Cairo around 1412, prescribes the correct forms of address for correspondence with the Ottoman ruler, styled the lord from the line of ʿUthmān, one princely house among the many the chancery ranked. The construction is the ordinary Arabic tribal genitive, *banū*, the same frame that holds Banū Umayya and Banū ʿAbbās, and the founder's Turkish name Osman is restored to its Arabic original ʿUthmān, the caliph's name it descends from.

The entry preserves a hierarchy at the moment before it inverted. To the Cairo chancery of 1412 the sons of ʿUthmān were a frontier dynasty to be addressed with measured courtesy, junior to the sultan of Egypt who sheltered the Abbasid caliph himself; the grammar of *banū* quietly filed them among the Arab world's familiar family-states. Within a century the sons of ʿUthmān had taken Constantinople, and within two they had taken Cairo, the caliphal title migrating to the house the manual had ranked below its own master. Chanceries fix names at the moment of writing; this one caught the Ottomans in the last decades when the Arabic world could still address them as one family among many.
