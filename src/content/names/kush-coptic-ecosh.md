*Ečōš* is the Coptic descendant of the Egyptian *Kꜣš*, by way of Demotic *ỉkš*: Sahidic ⲉϭⲱϣ, Bohairic ⲉⲑⲱϣ, with a plural ⲉϭⲟⲟϣ. The Bohairic spelling with *th* for the Sahidic *č* records the different fates of the old *k* before a front vowel in the two dialects, and the long *ō* of the stressed syllable records the vowel the hieroglyphs never wrote. In Coptic the word is chiefly an ethnic term: it renders Greek *Aithíops* in the Coptic Bible, where the eunuch of Acts 8 and the Cushites of the prophets are *ečōš*, and it names the Nubian neighbor in the texts of Egypt's southern frontier, the monastic and hagiographic literature in which Blemmyes and Nubians raid, trade, and convert.

The form is the last Egyptian stage of the Egyptian name, in use twenty-five centuries after the fortress-builders of the Twelfth Dynasty first carved it. The hieroglyphs were gone, the gods were gone, the pharaohs were gone, and the word for the people south of the cataracts was still the Middle Kingdom's word, now written in Greek letters and used to translate a Greek Bible. On this page it closes the Egyptian thread with a symmetry: the oldest name for Kush and its youngest are the same word, and the Christian monk who called a Nubian *ečōš* was using, without knowing it, the vocabulary of Senusret's garrisons.
