The *Auxōmîtai* are the first people in the written record to bear the name of Aksum. The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, a Greek merchant's handbook of the first century CE, describes the port of Adulis and the road inland, eight days' journey, to "the metropolis of the Auxomites," where the ivory of the interior was gathered, and names the king of the coast, Zoskales, a man well read in Greek. The spelling with the diphthong is the sailors'; Ezana's own Greek inscriptions three centuries later write *Axōmitôn* without it, and Procopius, recording the embassy Justinian sent in the 530s, calls the capital Auxomis. Cosmas Indicopleustes, who copied the Greek inscription at Adulis around 525, knew the city as Axōmē. The name reached Greek from the Aksumites themselves, in whatever form the merchants heard it, and it kept its shape through five centuries of Greek writing about the Red Sea.

The entry is the oldest attestation on the page and the one that fixes the kingdom's position in the world: a Greek-speaking king, a metropolis at the head of an ivory road, a port the Roman trade with India knew well. Greek was the language in which Aksum first spoke to the world and in which its kings carved their victories, so that this foreign ethnic was, for a long time, as close to an official name as the kingdom had abroad. On a page where the kingdom's own word is overtaken by the Greek description Ethiopia, the Auxomites stand for the other possibility, the one Greek did not take: the Greeks had the native name first, and let it go.
