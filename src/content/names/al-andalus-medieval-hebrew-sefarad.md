The Hebrew *Sǝfārad*, the medieval Jewish name of Iberia and above all of al-Andalus. Its origin is a single verse: Obadiah's prophecy names "the exiles of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad," a place whose actual referent, plausibly Sardis in Anatolia, was already obscure in antiquity. Medieval tradition identified the verse's mysterious exile-land with Spain, and the identification became the name: Ḥasdai ibn Shapruṭ, writing from Córdoba to the Khazar king, calls his own country Sepharad, and the entire literature of the Andalusi golden age is written, in its own eyes, in Sepharad. The name's living descendants are the Sephardim, the Jews of the Iberian tradition, who carried it into exile in 1492 as their own designation.

The entry is the atlas's purest case of a name created by exegesis. Every other form for this land describes something, the Vandals in *al-Andalus* on this page, the rabbit-coast or the west in the Latin and Punic threads; *Sǝfārad* describes nothing, because it is not a description but a citation, a scriptural blank filled in with a homeland. The choice made geography into theology: to live in Sepharad was to live inside a verse about exiles from Jerusalem, and when the expulsion came, the name completed its own prophecy, the exiles of Sepharad joining the exiles of Jerusalem in one sentence of Obadiah. A people that named its golden age out of a prophecy of exile turned out, in the end, to have read the verse correctly.
