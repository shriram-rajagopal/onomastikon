*Aithiopía* is the Greek name for the land beyond Egypt, and unlike every other form on this page it is not a borrowing but a description. The Greeks themselves derived *Aithíopes* from *aíthō*, "burn," and *ṓps*, "face," the burnt-faced people; modern etymology is less sure, and Beekes treats the ancient analysis as folk etymology on a name of unknown origin. Homer puts the Aithiopes at the ends of the earth, split between the setting and the rising sun, where the gods go to feast. Herodotus makes them concrete: Meroë is "the mother city of the other Ethiopians" (2.29), the long-lived Ethiopians receive Cambyses's spies and send the Persians home with a bow they cannot draw (3.17–25), and the Ethiopians on Egypt's border bring Darius their gifts of gold and ebony and elephant tusk (3.97). Eratosthenes and Ptolemy inherit the name, and the Septuagint writes it wherever the Hebrew says Kush.

The form is the one name on this page that describes rather than names, and it has traveled the farthest. Because the Greek Bible chose it for Kush, Latin Christendom read Ethiopia for a thousand years where the Hebrew said something else; because the kings of Aksum, writing in Greek, took it for their own kingdom, it crossed the Red Sea and the Ethiopian highlands and settled on a different state, which keeps it today. The Greeks who coined it meant the Nile above Egypt. The name moved across a sea and a mountain range to another kingdom, and the geographers who first wrote it would not recognize the map it now labels.
