*al-Khilāfa* (الخلافة) is the Arabic name of the office and, by extension, the state: the verbal noun of the root *kh-l-f*, "to come after, to succeed," whose agent noun *khalīfa* names the successor himself. Abu Bakr took power in 632 as *khalīfat rasūl Allāh*, "successor of the Messenger of God," and the abstract *khilāfa* became the standing name for the institution and the realm it governed, used throughout Arabic historiography for the whole line from the Rashidun through the Abbasids. The word carries a Qur'anic resonance older than the office: the Qur'an calls Adam a *khalīfa* set upon the earth, and later caliphs were not above preferring the more expansive reading, "deputy of God," to the modest "successor of the Prophet."

What distinguishes this endonym from every other on the atlas is that it names neither a land, a people, a city, nor a founder. *Rhōmanía* is a geography, *Ērānšahr* a people's realm, *Zhōngguó* a position on the map; *al-Khilāfa* is a constitutional claim, the assertion that legitimate rule descends by succession from the Prophet. That is why it could pass intact from Medina to Damascus to Baghdad while dynasties rose and fell, and why rival courts at Córdoba and Cairo could each claim the same name for themselves; a name that asserts a right can be contested in a way no place-name can. Most states on this atlas are named for where they are or who lives in them. This one is named for an argument about who should rule, and the argument outlasted the state by centuries.
