*Saljūqiyān* (سلجوقیان) is the Persian name of the dynasty, the eponym with the Persian plural, used by the historians who wrote the Seljuks' own official record; Rāvandī's *Rāḥat al-ṣudūr*, composed around 1204 and dedicated to the Konya sultanate, is a history of the house written in the language of its own chancery. That is the entry's peculiar situation: Persian was not a neighbor's language to the Seljuks but their language of state, the tongue of their bureaus, poets, and court historians, so this exonym is also, functionally, the closest thing to an official self-designation the dynasty ever committed to writing.

The entry therefore hosts the page's structural irony. A Turkish house whose own speech went unwritten adopted Persian so thoroughly that its identity survives in Persian grammar, its Turkish ancestor pluralized with a Persian suffix; the conquerors of Iran were archived by their own secretaries as a Persian word. The pattern would outlive them, for the Ottomans began the same way, Persian verse chronicles first, and the whole Turco-Persian synthesis this atlas's eastern pages keep meeting, from Delhi's chancery Persian to Konya's, is present in miniature in this one plural. The Seljuks ruled in Turkish, prayed in Arabic, and are remembered in Persian; of the three, they could write only the last two, and remembrance belongs to the written.
