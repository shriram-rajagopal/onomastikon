A name that appears exactly once in the Hebrew Bible, *Sĕpārad*, in Obadiah's vision of the return: "the exiles of Jerusalem who are in Sĕpārad shall possess the cities of the Negev." Where Sĕpārad lay is the dispute the entry's label records, but the identification most scholars now accept is Sardis, the Lydian capital: the Aramaic face of the Lydian-Aramaic bilingual found at Sardis itself calls the city *sprd*, the same consonants, matching the *Sfard* of Lydian coins and the *Sparda* by which the Persian empire knew its Lydian satrapy. On that reading, Obadiah's verse is a glimpse of a Judean exile community in Asia Minor within a generation or two of the fall of Jerusalem.

The name's later history is larger than its verse. Medieval Jewish tradition, reading Obadiah without a map of Achaemenid Anatolia, identified *Sĕpārad* with the far west, with Spain, and the identification stuck: the Jews of Iberia became the Sephardim, and their whole civilization, liturgy, and language took its name from this word. The result is one of the strangest journeys on any page of this atlas: a Lydian city's name, filtered through one prophetic verse, migrated three thousand kilometers west and became the permanent name of a diaspora that had never seen Sardis. Lydia's page holds the name's true address; Jewish history kept the misdelivered letter.
