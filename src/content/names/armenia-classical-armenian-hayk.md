*Haykʿ* is the Armenians' own name for their nation and land, the plural of *Hay*, "an Armenian," attested from the beginnings of Armenian literature in the fifth century CE. The land is *Haykʿ* or, in the locative that became the standard country-name, *Hayastan*; the people are *Haykʿ*; the language is *Hayerēn*. The fifth-century historian Movsēs Xorenacʿi derives the name from the eponymous patriarch Hayk, the giant archer who, in the national legend, slays the Babylonian tyrant Bēl and founds the Armenian people at the foot of the mountains. Whatever the true etymology, the self-designation is independent of and far older in feeling than the literary record that first preserves it.

This is the single entry on the page that is genuinely Armenia's own name for itself, and it stands entirely apart from the *Armina* tradition that gave every other language its word. The Armenians never called themselves Armenians; the world has never called them *Hay*. The gap is among the sharpest endonym-exonym splits in the project, and it is no accident that the self-name surfaces in writing only with Mesrop Maštocʿ's alphabet of around 405 CE, the moment Armenian could record its own voice rather than be named by its neighbors. Armenians are *hay* and the country *Hayastan* to this day; the eponym's name has never once left its people's mouths.
