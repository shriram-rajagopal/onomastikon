*Ṭulayṭula* (طليطلة) is the Arabic name of Toledo, the Romance pronunciation of *Toletum* recast with two emphatic *ṭāʾ*s and a shape that to Arabic ears carries the ring of a diminutive. As capital of the Middle March it anchored al-Andalus's central frontier; Yāqūt's entry, written long after the city had passed to Castile in 1085, still describes its walls, its bridge, and its unmatched situation above the river. The name remains the Arabic word for Toledo, and its Mozarabic pronunciation fed directly into the Castilian *Toledo* of the conquerors.

The name's most productive years came after its city fell, which is the entry's distinction. Toledo under Castilian rule kept its Arabic libraries and its Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews, and through the translation movement of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Gerard of Cremona alone rendering dozens of works, *Ṭulayṭula*'s books became Latin Europe's science: the Almagest, Avicenna's Canon, Aristotle with Averroes's commentaries. Arabic geographers went on writing the name of a city that was no longer theirs while that city translated their civilization's learning for its new one. Elsewhere on this atlas names outlive their states; *Ṭulayṭula* names the room where one civilization formally handed its library to the next.
