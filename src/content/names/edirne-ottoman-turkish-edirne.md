*Edirne* (ادرنه) is the Turkish form of Adrianople, the spoken Greek name taken over at Murad I's capture of the city around 1369 and worn to three light syllables, the emperor's name no longer audible in it. As the Ottoman capital in Europe for some eight decades it was the seat from which the Balkans were organized and the final campaign against Constantinople prepared; Mehmed II's great cannon were cast at Edirne, and the army of 1453 marched from it. The city remained a beloved imperial residence afterward, crowned in the next century by Sinan's Selimiye mosque, which its architect judged his masterpiece; the name continues unchanged in modern Turkish.

The entry completes the phase's second dissolved-emperor chain, the Balkan twin of Nishapur's: a ruler's dedication ground down by a later language into a plain place-name, Hadrian going the way of Shapur. What distinguishes Edirne's case is the freight of the final scene. The atlas's last act was staged between two emperors' cities, planned in Hadrian's and executed against Constantine's, by a dynasty that pronounced the first beyond recognition and preserved the second on its coinage; the two Roman names met on opposite sides of the wall of 1453. Hadrian's name lost its emperor and kept the city, Constantine's kept the emperor and lost the city, and the atlas closes with both names still on the map, spelled by the victors.
