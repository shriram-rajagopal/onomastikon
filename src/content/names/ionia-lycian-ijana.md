The Lycian word for the Ionians, attested twice on the Xanthos pillar: *Ijãnã* in the south-face narrative, where the dynast takes a place "of the Ialysian Greeks," and *Ijãnisñ* on the east face, in the line that files it beside *sppartazi* and *atãna[zi]*, the Spartans and the Athenians. Melchert's dictionary reads the second form as the place-name Ionia; Oreshko takes both as ethnics, "Ionians" or simply "Greeks," since Ialysos, the city the first passage attaches the word to, was Doric and no Ionian settlement at all.

That looseness is the word's pedigree. *Ijãnã* belongs to the atlas's widest-flung family, the Ionian name that the Near East applied to every Greek: Akkadian *Yamānu*, Hebrew *Yāwān*, Old Persian *Yauna*, and eventually the Arabic *al-Yūnān*. Lycia sat between the two sources, a Greek-facing coast under a Persian crown, and its form could descend from the Greeks' own *Iāones* or from the *Yauna* of its overlord's chancery; the word's shape allows either, and the atlas asserts neither. What the Lycian usage does show is the family's defining habit fully at work: a Doric city labeled Ionian, because from the Anatolian shore, as from Susa and Jerusalem, all Greeks answered to the one name.
