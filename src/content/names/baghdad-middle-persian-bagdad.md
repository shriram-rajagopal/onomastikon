*Baγdād* is the Middle Persian form of the city's name, and its attestation is the last entry of the *Šahrestānīhā ī Ērānšahr*: the Pahlavi catalogue of the cities of the empire ends by recording that the *šahrestān* of Baghdad was built by Abū Jaʿfar, the caliph al-Manṣūr, who founded the Abbasid capital in 762 CE. The name itself is Iranian and older than the caliphal city, a compound of *bag*, "god," and *dād*, "given," borne by the village on the Tigris that the new foundation absorbed; the Jewish Aramaic form recorded on this page attests the settlement centuries before the caliphs. The attestation is Book Pahlavi, which has no Unicode encoding, so the entry carries transliteration only.

No other entry in this atlas documents quite so exact a changing of the guard. A Middle Persian text, in the script and language of the fallen Sasanian empire, closes its register of cities by logging the construction of the capital that replaced Ctesiphon and sealed that empire's end; the dying tradition wrote its successor's birth certificate. And the name it wrote was its own: the caliphs built the City of Peace, *Madīnat al-Salām*, as the official style, but what the world adopted was the old Persian village name, "given by God," with *bag* the old Iranian word for a god. The Islamic world's greatest city bears, to this day, a name in the language of the empire it superseded.
