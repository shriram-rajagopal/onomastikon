*Arwand* is a Zoroastrian book-name for the Tigris, an Iranian adjective meaning "swift, valiant" serving as a river-name. It designates the Tigris in passages of the ninth-century compendium of Zādspram and in the Zand of the *Vidēvdād*, giving the sacred geography of the tradition a native word for the river the administrative language called *Diglat*, the borrowed Aramaic form recorded beside this entry. The usage carries a tangle: the Pahlavi script spells *Arwand* almost identically to *Arang*, the westward-flowing world-river of Avestan cosmology, and the Tigris application may rest partly on that near-identity, whether as a copyist's slip or as a deliberate pinning of the scriptural river to a real western stream. The attestations are Book Pahlavi, which has no Unicode encoding, so the entry carries transliteration only.

The name is a folk-etymology reborn as an original. The Achaemenids had bent the river's old Mesopotamian name into *Tigrā*, the arrow, so that it would sound swift in Persian; the Zoroastrian tradition, a millennium later and with no sign of remembering the precedent, reached for the same idea and said "the swift" outright, in an Iranian word owing nothing to *Idigna* at all. The same river drew the same metaphor from Persian twice, once as a pun and once as a plain description. Alone among the ancient names on this page, this one is still on the map and still contested: New Persian carried *Arwand* forward as *Arvand Rūd*, Iran's name for the Shatt al-Arab, the river's joined mouth, where it faces the Arabic *Dijla* descended from this entry's own neighbor.
