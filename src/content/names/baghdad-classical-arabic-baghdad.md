*Baghdād* (بغداد) is not the name the caliph gave his capital but the name of the ground he built it on, a Sasanian-era village on the west bank of the Tigris whose name al-Manṣūr's city inherited and made famous. The name is Iranian, generally explained from Old Iranian *baga* "god" and *dāta* "given," a "god-given" of the same type as many Iranian personal and place names. The geographer Yāqūt, writing before the Mongol sack, opens his great gazetteer's entry with the name's variant spellings and the discomfort it caused: pious opinion disliked a pagan etymology attached to the seat of the caliphate, and some scholars are reported to have preferred the official name for exactly that reason.

The entry's significance is the vernacular's total victory. Against *Baghdād* stood everything the state could muster: an official name on the coins, the chancery, and the Friday sermon, and even a theological objection to the old word. None of it mattered. The village name took the metropolis within a generation, travelled with the merchants to appear as *Báidá* in the Song gazetteers, and never afterward yielded. It also carries a quiet historical correction, since the Talmudic *Baḡdāṯā* shows the name in use centuries before Islam: the most famous of all Islamic capitals bears a pre-Islamic, Persian, pagan name. Al-Manṣūr built the city; the name was already living there, and it simply moved into the new house.
