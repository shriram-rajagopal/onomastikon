*Pāṭaliputra* is the Sanskrit name of the Mauryan and Gupta capital, a compound of *pāṭali*, the trumpet-flower tree, and *putra*, "son": the city as child of the tree, a name the tradition explained with more than one legend and no certainty. It is the standard form across Sanskrit literature, in the grammarian Patañjali (second century BCE), whose *Mahābhāṣya* reaches for the city as a ready example the way a modern grammar reaches for London, in the political drama *Mudrārākṣasa*, whose plot turns on Candragupta's seizure of the city, and in the Purāṇic king-lists.

The Sanskrit form is the family's reference point but not its oldest attestation; the Pali canon's *Pāṭaligāma* narrative and Megasthenes' Greek transcription both precede Patañjali. What the Sanskrit register adds is permanence: while the spoken forms drifted and the Greek report faded, the literary name held its shape for a thousand years of texts composed long after the city had declined. When the name finally wore down to Patna in the modern vernacular, it was the Sanskrit spelling that preserved, letter by letter, what the city had been called at its height.
