*Kûš* is the Hebrew Bible's name for the land south of Egypt, taken, as the lexicon states without hedging, from the Egyptian *Kꜣš*, with the long *u* that the Akkadian transcription also shows. It occurs some thirty times. In the Table of Nations Cush is a son of Ham and the father of Nimrod and of the peoples of the Red Sea shore; in the prophets it is a country, the "land of whirring wings beyond the rivers of Cush" of Isaiah 18, the ally whose fall with Egypt Isaiah 20 foretells; in Kings it has a king, Tirhakah, the Taharqa of the Assyrian annals, marching out against Sennacherib; in Jeremiah its people have the skin that cannot be changed, and in Esther it bounds the Persian empire, "from India to Cush," exactly where Darius's own lists put it.

The entry is where the page's two great threads cross. When the Hebrew scriptures were translated into Greek, *Kûš* the land became *Aithiopía* in nearly every verse, the Greek description substituted for the Egyptian name, and the Latin Vulgate followed; the Geʽez Bible followed the Greek and wrote *Ityoṗya*, and a kingdom on the far side of the Red Sea read the verses about Cush as verses about itself. The Syriac Peshitta and the Talmud, reading the Hebrew, kept *Kûš*. So the two names divided the world by the language a church read its Bible in: east of the Greek, Kush; west of it, Ethiopia. A verse in Hebrew says Kush, the same verse in Greek says Ethiopia, and modern Ethiopia is named by the second word.
