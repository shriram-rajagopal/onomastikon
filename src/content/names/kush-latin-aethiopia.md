*Aethiopia* is the Greek name taken into Latin entire, *ae* for the diphthong and *th* for the aspirate, and it names Kush in its Roman centuries. Pliny's sixth book is the fullest Latin account: the expedition of Petronius, prefect of Egypt under Augustus, who in the 20s BCE fought the forces of the Meroitic queen, the *candace*, and reached Napata; and the praetorian soldiers whom Nero sent south with a tribune to explore, who came back to report that the country up to Meroë was solitude, *solitudines*, where the Greek writers had placed cities. Seneca, who heard the centurions himself, records their account of the Nile's marshes. The Roman Aethiopia was the far side of a frontier, visited twice and conquered never.

The Latin name records the kingdom in its decline. Where Herodotus's Ethiopians were long-lived and rich in gold, Pliny's explorers find emptiness, and the contrast between the two reports is a measure of what had happened to Meroë's northern reaches in four centuries. The form's longer life lies elsewhere: when Jerome made the Vulgate around 400 he rendered Hebrew *Kûš* as *Aethiopia*, following the Greek, and so fixed the Greek description into the Bible of Latin Christendom for a thousand years. What the Greek name described, the Latin one inherited, and through Jerome the West read Ethiopia wherever the Hebrew said Kush.
