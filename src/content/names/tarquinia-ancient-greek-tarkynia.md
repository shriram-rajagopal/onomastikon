The Greek name of the Etruscan city, *Tarkynía*, in Strabo and Dionysius of Halicarnassus, both of whom tell the same story about it: that Demaratus, a merchant-aristocrat of Corinth fleeing the tyranny of Kypselos in the seventh century, settled there with his craftsmen, and that his son moved to Rome and reigned as Tarquinius Priscus. The Greek form renders the name the atlas records at its source as Etruscan *Tarchna*, softened through the Latin-facing shape that also gives *Tarquinii*.

Greek had no independent knowledge of the city; it knew *Tarkynía* almost entirely as the setting of the Demaratus legend, a story Greeks told to give Rome's kings a Corinthian grandfather. That is the entry's interest: the name enters Greek not as geography but as genealogy, an Etruscan city adopted into Hellenic family history so that the Tarquin dynasty could be half-Greek. The Etruscans on this page name the city from inside and the Romans administer it from next door; the Greeks, farthest away, used it as a stage, and their name for the place survives mainly as the birthplace of a convenient ancestor.
