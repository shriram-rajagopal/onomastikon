The name of Israel as its enemy wrote it: *Yšrʾl*, the consonantal skeleton carved repeatedly into the stele that King Mesha of Moab raised at Dibon around 840 BCE, in lines that name "Omri king of Israel" as the oppressor of Moab and count the towns taken back from him. The form is the Hebrews' own name for their kingdom, carried across the Dead Sea into the mouth and script of the neighbor fighting them; Moabite and Hebrew were close enough that the borrowing required no alteration at all, and the stele's five letters match the Hebrew spelling exactly. What the stone cannot show is the vowels, for Moabite left no reading tradition, and the atlas writes the name as the mason did, consonants only.

This is the oldest securely dated occurrence of Israel's name in any Semitic inscription, older than every biblical manuscript that preserves the Hebrew form above it on this page. The witnesses to this name run hostile: a Moabite king boasting of Israel's ruin here, an Aramaean king's claims at Tel Dan a few decades later, Assyrian annals reckoning the kingdom as the House of Omri. The name of Israel enters the durable record almost entirely in the voices of its adversaries, each conqueror preserving, in the act of claiming victory, exactly the name he meant to erase.
