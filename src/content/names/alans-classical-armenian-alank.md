The Classical Armenian *Alankʿ*, the name of the neighbors beyond the Caucasus crest, wearing the same plural *-kʿ* as the *Honkʿ* and *Hepʿtʿałkʿ* of this atlas's other steppe pages. Its richest early setting is Movsēs Xorenacʿi's tale of the war between king Artašēs and the Alans: the Alan princess Satʿenik, seeing her captive brother from the rampart, bargains across the river, and Artašēs, advised that heroes take their brides by force, rides across, throws a red-leather lasso across the princess's waist, and carries her home to be queen of Armenia, in lines Movsēs quotes from the songs the storytellers still sang.

The entry is the family's one name embedded in kinship rather than fear or administration. Rome drafted battle plans against Alans and Constantinople hired them; Armenia, which fought them too, also married them, and put an Alan queen into its national legend, her lasso-capture a courtly mirror of the lassos Josephus's raiders threw in earnest. That the wooing weapon of the legend is the signature weapon of the steppe is the detail that gives the story its truth: the neighbors knew each other's hands. Among the records of the Alans, only the Armenian one remembers them as in-laws.
