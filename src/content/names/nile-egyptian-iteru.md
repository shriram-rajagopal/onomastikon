The ordinary Egyptian word for the Nile as a watercourse, *jtrw*, "the river," distinct from the divinized inundation *Ḥꜥpy*. Where *Ḥꜥpy* names the flood as a god, *jtrw* names the channel itself, and because Egypt has only one river of consequence, "the river" sufficed to designate the Nile without qualification; the fuller *jtrw-ꜥꜣ*, "the great river," appears where emphasis is wanted. The word is attested from the Old Kingdom through the latest stages of the language, and its reconstructed vocalization moves from Middle Egyptian *\*jatraw* to a much-reduced Late Egyptian *\*jaʔr*, the form that the borrowings preserve.

*jtrw* is the headwater of a transmission that carries the Egyptian name for the Nile out of Egyptian and across the Near East. Its eroded late form survives natively in the Coptic *eioor* (ⲉⲓⲟⲟⲣ), and it was borrowed into Biblical Hebrew as *Yeʾōr* (יְאֹר), the standard word for the Nile throughout Genesis and Exodus; it may also stand behind Greek *Neîlos*, if that name derives from the plural *nꜣ-jrw*, "the rivers." The Egyptians' plainest name for their river, the one that simply called it "the river," is thus the form that traveled furthest. Where the divine *Ḥꜥpy* stayed inside Egyptian religion, *jtrw* became the root from which the neighboring languages built their own names for the Nile.
