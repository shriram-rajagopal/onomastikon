The Greek name of Bahrain, *Týlos*, brought back by the surveyors Alexander sent down the Gulf in the 320s BCE, Androsthenes of Thasos foremost among them. Theophrastus, drawing on Androsthenes' report, describes the island of Tylos and its wool-bearing trees, the most detailed early Greek account of cotton; Strabo places Tylos beside a second island called Arados and reports that their people pointed to temples like the Phoenicians' and claimed the great cities of Phoenicia, Tyre and Arados, as their colonies, the Gulf-origin theory of the Phoenicians that Herodotus had already heard. The name stays on the classical map through Pliny and into Ptolemy's geography, naming the island through the Seleucid and Parthian centuries in which it was a garrisoned and prosperous place.

*Týlos* is generally taken as the old name *Tilmun* itself, worn smooth in transmission and fitted with a Greek ending, and if so it is the hinge of this page: the point where a name that had lived its whole life in cuneiform crossed into the alphabet and the West. The identification cannot be proven outright, since no ancient writer connects the two words, but the continuity of place, the initial *t* of the Akkadian form, and the lack of any competing explanation have satisfied most scholarship. Read that way, a Greek botanical notice about cotton-trees quietly carries the last living syllables of the Bronze Age Gulf, a name already two and a half thousand years old when Androsthenes wrote it down.
