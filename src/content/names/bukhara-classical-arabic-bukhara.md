*Bukhārā* (بخارى) is the Arabic form of the city's local name, in use from the conquest of the early eighth century and carried through all of Arabic geographical and biographical literature; the most consequential bearer of its adjective is al-Bukhārī, compiler of the collection of hadith that Sunni Islam ranks second only to the Qur'an, whose name has put the city into Muslim mouths daily for eleven centuries. Yāqūt's entry records the city's learning and its unhealthy climate with equal fidelity. What the name means, Arabic could not say; the old derivation from Sanskrit *vihāra*, a Buddhist monastery, has yielded in modern scholarship to a probably Sogdian origin, with no reading established.

The entry's payoff is the afterlife of the adjective. *Bukhārī* began as a plain toponymic surname, one scholar's tag among the thousands in the biographical dictionaries; the authority of one book made it a holy word, so that the city's name achieved its widest circulation detached from the city, as the title of a text recited from Senegal to Java by people who could not place Bukhara on a map. No other city on this atlas is known to more people through fewer facts. The msr family shows a country's name narrowing to a city; *Bukhārī* shows a city's name escaping geography altogether, into the running text of a civilization's piety.
