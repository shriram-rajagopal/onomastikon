*Meṣrēn* (ܡܨܪܝܢ) is the Syriac name of Egypt, standard across the whole Syriac corpus from the Peshitta Old Testament (c. 2nd century CE) onward: Abram goes down to *Meṣrēn* in Genesis 12:10, and in Matthew 2:13 the holy family flees there from Herod. The noun is feminine, with the gentilic *Meṣrāyā*, plural *Meṣrāyē*, "Egyptian(s)," and the lexica record *Meṣrēn gawwāytā*, "inner Egypt," for the country's far south. Spelled with the same five consonants as the Imperial Aramaic *Mṣryn* of the Elephantine papyri, the form is not a new borrowing but the same Aramaic word more than half a millennium later, re-dressed in Edessene script and vocalized by the grammarians.

On this page *Meṣrēn* is the youngest member of the Semitic *mṣr* family and the last ancient link in its chain. The root that names Egypt in Akkadian *Miṣru*, Hebrew *Miṣrayim*, Phoenician and Ugaritic *Mṣrm*, and the Aramaic of the Achaemenid chancelleries here continues into the Christian literature of Late Antiquity, still carrying the dual-or-plural ending that marks its Northwest Semitic branch. When the Arab conquest brought the cognate *Miṣr* to the Nile in the seventh century, it arrived in a region whose Aramaic-speaking churches had never stopped using the root; Semitic speakers had by then been calling Egypt by these three consonants for over two thousand years, and Syriac is the form in which that habit crossed from the ancient world into the medieval one.
