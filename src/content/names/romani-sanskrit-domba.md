The Sanskrit *ḍomba*, the name of the itinerant musician and performer castes of medieval India, attested across the lexica and, most vividly, in Kalhaṇa's *Rājataraṅgiṇī*, the twelfth-century chronicle of Kashmir, where ḍomba singers rise scandalously high at royal courts and fall again. The word is the accepted etymon of the Romani self-name: *Rom*, with its kin *Lom* in Armenia and *Dom* across the Middle East, continues *ḍomba* through the regular sound changes of the migration, and the three branches preserve in their own names the Indian caste-world the migrants left. The dispute is one of referent, not derivation: *ḍomba* in its Sanskrit attestations names groups inside India, and no Sanskrit text names the departed diaspora; the entry stands on this page the way *Kaptara* stands on Crete's, the ancestor of the name rather than the name itself.

It is nonetheless the only word on the page that the Romani would recognize as their own. Every other entry records what someone called them; *ḍomba* is what, through thirty generations of sound change, they have never stopped calling themselves. The atlas holds the endonym after all, hidden at the page's root: unwritten in Europe until after 1453, the name *Rom* was in writing all along, in Sanskrit, in a Kashmirian chronicle composed while the migration was still on the road. The people every language on this page misnamed carried the correct answer with them the entire time.
