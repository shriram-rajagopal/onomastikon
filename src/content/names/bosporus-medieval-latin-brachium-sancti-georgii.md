The crusaders' name for the strait at Constantinople, *Brachium Sancti Georgii*, "the Arm of Saint George." The chronicles of the First Crusade use it as a fixed landmark: the *Gesta Francorum* moves its armies to the *Brachium Sancti Georgii*, ships them across, and counts the campaign in Anatolia as begun. The name is usually explained by the monastery of St George of Mangana on the Constantinopolitan shore, and the chroniclers apply it with a traveler's looseness, most often to the Bosporus at the crossing point, sometimes stretched along the whole chain of water toward the Hellespont; Odo of Deuil, chaplain of the Second Crusade, uses it the same way a half-century later.

The Greek name on this page tells the myth of Io, the ox that forded; the Latin pilgrims' name tells where the pilgrim stood, at the edge of the last Christian capital, under the protection of a warrior saint, about to cross into a hostile continent. It belongs to a whole pilgrim geography in which the East was mapped by its shrines rather than its legends, and it names the strait by what mattered at the moment of crossing: not the water's story but the crosser's patron. Each age took the strait under its own escort, the Greeks behind a mythical ox, the Franks behind Saint George; the water in between never needed a name of its own, only a guarantee that one could get across.
