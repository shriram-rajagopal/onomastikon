The Babylonian rendering of Gandhara in the Achaemenid inscriptions, *Gandari*, written *gan-da-ri*, standing in the satrapy lists of Behistun and the later royal inscriptions beside Old Persian *Gadāra* and Elamite *Gandara*. The Babylonian and Elamite columns both preserve the nasal that the Old Persian spelling leaves unwritten, exactly as they do for *Hinduš*; the cuneiform scribes heard *Gandāra* and wrote what they heard, while the Persian syllabary silently dropped the *n* before the consonant.

On a page anchored by Sanskrit *Gandhāra*, the Behistun trilingual shows the name passing through the empire's three chancelleries with different losses in each: Old Persian loses the nasal to its script's conventions, and the aspirate *dh*, which no cuneiform system could write, is already gone from all three. The Babylonian column is the farthest west the name ever traveled in cuneiform, a district of the Indian borderlands filed in the same script that had recorded Sargon's empire two thousand years earlier; the scribes who wrote it had no idea what a *Gandhāra* was, and wrote it more faithfully than the Persians who ruled it.
