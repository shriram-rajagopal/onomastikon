The medieval Latin continuation of *Constantinopolis*, the name the Latin entry above carries to 600. Through the Middle Ages the city's Latin name never wavered, in papal letters, crusade chronicles, and merchant documents alike, and for fifty-seven years it was the name of the government itself: after the Fourth Crusade took the city in 1204, the crusader realm styled its ruler emperor of *Constantinopolis*, and a Latin patriarch of Constantinople sat in Hagia Sophia until the Greeks retook the city in 1261. The name held from there to the morning in 1453 when the last emperor died at the walls.

On a page where the city's name migrates through Greek, Arabic, Syriac, and Ottoman Turkish shapes, the Latin thread runs eight and a half centuries without change, indifferent to whether the Latins were the city's pilgrims, its conquerors, or its mourners; that constancy is its distinction. The other languages renamed the city as their relation to it changed, and the change of masters in 1453 promptly produced *Kostantiniyye*; Latin's relation to the city changed as much as anyone's, and its name never moved. It was in this fixed name that Latin Europe wrote down the news of 1453, the one stable coordinate from which the fall of everything else could be measured.
