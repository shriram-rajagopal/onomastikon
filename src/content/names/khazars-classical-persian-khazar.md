The Classical Persian *Khazar*, carried by the geographical tradition that New Persian shares with Arabic and given its fullest early Persian treatment in the anonymous *Ḥudūd al-ʿĀlam* of 982, whose chapter on the Khazar country describes Atil, the qaghan and his deputy, the export of cattle and captives, and a people, it notes, of wealth and armies. The book was written on the eve of the qaghanate's fall, an obituary that did not know itself. The living part of the Persian name is the sea: *Daryā-yi Khazar* remains the ordinary Persian name of the Caspian, the form the Caspian's page records beside its Arabic twin.

The entry is the family's demonstration of how a name survives its bearer by changing hosts. The people vanished within a century of the *Ḥudūd*'s chapter, but Persian had already lodged their name in a geographical fixture that no dynasty could dissolve, and eleven centuries of Persian speakers have named that water daily without a Khazar among them. On the Khazars' own page the Persian entry is thus the one written closest to the end, the last portrait of the living kingdom in a language that would go on to keep, in a sea's name, the longest memorial anyone kept of it.
