The Classical Persian *Turk*, inherited from the Middle Persian of the late Sasanian frontier and universal in the New Persian written from the tenth century onward. In the geographers it is plain ethnography; in the poets it is something larger, because Firdawsi's *Shāhnāmeh* systematically identifies the Turks with Tūrān, the ancient enemy-land of Iranian epic, so that the historical qaghanate's people inherit the role of Afrāsiyāb's hordes in a story set long before any Turk existed. The Scythia page's *Tūrān* entry records the older name; this one records the living people the epic poured into it.

The identification is the most consequential act of naming in the family. It gave a millennium of Persian literature its standing antagonist, made "Turk and Tajik" the shorthand for the steppe and the sown, and handed every Turkic dynasty that ruled Iran, and most did, a schizophrenic inheritance: sultans who patronized the *Shāhnāmeh* were financing the epic of their own villainy. The word also acquired, in the poets, a second life as the type of the beautiful beloved, so that in a single Persian ghazal *Turk* can mean marauder and darling in successive lines. No other language put the name to such contradictory work, or enjoyed the contradiction more.
