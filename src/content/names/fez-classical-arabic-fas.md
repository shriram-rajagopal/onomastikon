*Fās* (فاس) has named the city since the Idrisid foundations of 789 and 809 on the two banks of the river that shares the name. The origin of the single syllable is unknown, and the city's own chroniclers filled the gap with legends collected in the *Rawḍ al-Qirṭās*: that a pickaxe, *fa's*, of silver and gold broke the ground and gave the town its name; that an old city called Sāf had stood nearby and the name was its reverse; that the founder was handed the tool by a hermit. The stories contradict each other cheerfully, which is the surest sign none is a memory.

What the entry documents, then, is not an etymology but the manufacture of one, observable in an unusually well-lit workshop. Fez was a city of scholars almost from its founding, and its historians were not going to leave the capital's name unexplained; by the fourteenth century the blank had been filled three different ways, each with circumstantial detail, each transparently reverse-engineered from the sound *fās*. The atlas meets folk etymology everywhere, usually as a single fossilized guess; here the whole assortment survives together, the workshop's rejects still on the shelf beside the products. The name of Fez means nothing recoverable, and the city's own books, read honestly, record exactly that, at length, in the form of three good stories.
