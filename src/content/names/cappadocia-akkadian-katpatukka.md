The Babylonian rendering of Cappadocia in the Achaemenid royal inscriptions, *Katpatukka*, written *ka-at-pa-tuk-ka*: the third voice of the trilingual chancellery, beside Old Persian *Katpatuka* and Elamite *Qatbaduqa*. The province appears in the land-lists of Darius's inscriptions at Behistun, Persepolis, and Naqsh-e Rustam and in Xerxes's daiva inscription, and the Babylonian version names it for the empire's Mesopotamian readers in a spelling that tracks the Persian sound for sound.

Unlike its neighbors on this page's map, the name had no Mesopotamian past to draw on. Assyria had known central Anatolia for over a millennium, as the karum-colony country of the Old Assyrian traders and later as the lands of Tabal and Mushku, yet the Babylonian scribes of Darius reached for none of those names; whatever *Katpatuka* meant, and its etymology is still unsettled, it was the Persian administration's own label, and all three chancellery languages simply transcribed it. The page's Greek and Latin entries descend from the same act of administrative naming, which makes Cappadocia a country whose every surviving name, east and west, radiates from a single Persian word; the Babylonian column shows the moment even Mesopotamia, with a millennium of its own names for Anatolia in its archives, took dictation.
