The Akkadian name of Dilmun, *Tilmun*, the Sumerian name carried into the Semitic lingua franca of Mesopotamia, most often written with the same *NI.TUK* logogram the Sumerian scribes had fixed and voiced in Akkadian with an initial *t*. It appears in the inscription in which Sargon of Akkad has the ships of Meluhha, Magan, and Dilmun moor at the quay of Agade, and it frames the best-documented private commerce of the ancient world: the Old Babylonian tablets of Ur name the *ālik Tilmun*, the "Dilmun-farers," the merchant partnerships that sailed south for copper, among them the archive of Ea-nasir, whose customers' complaints about his copper are still legible. A millennium and a half later the name is unchanged in the annals of Sargon II of Assyria, who counts Uperi, king of Dilmun, among his tributaries, and it survives to the empire's end in Neo-Babylonian administrative use.

*Tilmun* is the workhorse of this page, the form under which the name did its two thousand years of commercial duty. Sumerian coined the writing, but Akkadian was the language of the contracts, the loan documents, and the diplomatic annals, so nearly everything we actually know about historical Dilmun comes to us under this form of the name. It also shows the standard Mesopotamian pattern, seen likewise in *Elamtu* beside *Elam*, of one written sign group voiced two ways in two languages; the difference is that here the scribes never added a Semitic ending, keeping the foreign word foreign. The name the Greeks later heard around the Gulf, and rendered *Týlos*, was in all likelihood this Akkadian form on its last living breath.
