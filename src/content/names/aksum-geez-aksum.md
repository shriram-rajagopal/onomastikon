*ʾAksum* is the kingdom's name for itself, the name of its capital city extended to the realm the city ruled. It stands at the head of every royal title the Aksumite kings carved: Ezana is *nəguśa ʾAksum*, king of Aksum, before he is king of Ḥimyar and Raydān and Sabaʾ and the rest, in the unvocalized Geʽez of his early trilingual (RIE 185) and in the fully vocalized script of his Christian inscription (RIE 189), where the spelling given here is read. The name is older than the writing: the Sabaic inscriptions of Yemen already call Gadarat king of the Aksumites around 200 CE, and the Greek *Periplus* knew the Axomite metropolis a century and a half before that. What the word meant, no one knows; it is not Semitic in any transparent way and may belong to the Cushitic substrate of the highlands.

The entry is the one native name on a page of borrowed ones, and its fate is the page's lesson in reverse. Every other kingdom in this atlas whose endonym survives kept it as the name of the country; Aksum's did not. The state's own word shrank, after the tenth century, to the name of a town with a church in it, while the names outsiders had used, Ethiopia from the Greeks and Abyssinia from the Arabs, went on to name the country. The kings of the highlands did not stop reigning; they stopped reigning as kings of Aksum, and the word that had led their titles for six centuries became a place they were crowned in.
