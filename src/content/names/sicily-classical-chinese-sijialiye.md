The Chinese name for Sicily in the *Zhufanzhi* of c. 1225, an island Zhao Rukuo places beside the country of *Lúméi*, the Rūm of his Arab informants. The characters carry the Arabic *Ṣiqilliya* into Chinese, and with it came the island's most memorable feature: Zhao describes a mountain with a cavern that burns without pause, its fire visible from afar, the earliest Chinese notice of Etna, reported to a Fujian customs office by sailors who had it from the central Mediterranean.

This is the farthest west the Greek *Sikelía* family stretches on any page of the atlas, and it arrived by relay: a Greek island name, Arabized by the emirs who held Palermo for two centuries, then shipped east along the whole length of the monsoon trade. Zhao recorded it a scant generation after the Norman kingdom's Arabic chancery closed, so the name reached China in the Arabic dress it was already losing at home. An island can outrun its own history: Sicily entered Chinese as a Muslim place-name just as it stopped being one.
