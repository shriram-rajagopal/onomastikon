The Classical Arabic name of the Oman peninsula, *ʿUmān*, the name the land of Magan has carried from the coming of Islam to the present day. The geographers know it as a fertile and seafaring province on the outer ocean, its ports the outfitting places of the India and China trade; Yāqūt records the etymologies the philologists offered, an eponymous founder or a place in Syria transferred by settlers, none of them certain. The name is in fact older than Arabic writing: the first-century *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* and Pliny both know an *Omana* on this coast, so the Arabic form continues a local name already current in the classical geographers half a millennium before the hijra.

*ʿUmān* closes the page as the third and only surviving thread in the land's onomastic braid. The *Magan* of the cuneiform world died with the Gulf trade; the *Maka* of the Persian chancellery, whatever its ancestry, died with the empire; but *Omana*, surfacing quietly in a merchants' pilot-book while those older names were already antiquarian, proved to be the one with the future. There is a fitting symmetry in that: Magan's name was always a trader's word, and when the land finally renamed itself for good, it did so in a trade manual. The copper country of Naram-Sin sails on under the name the pilots used.
