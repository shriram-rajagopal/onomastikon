The westernmost country in the Chinese record of the world, 木蘭皮 *Mùlánpí*, in Zhao Rukuo's *Zhufanzhi* of 1225: a phonetic transcription of *al-Murābiṭūn*, the Almoravids, the Berber dynasty whose realm ran from the Sahara across the Maghreb into southern Spain. Zhao, superintendent of maritime trade at Quanzhou, compiled his gazetteer from the Arab merchants in his ports, and through their accounts the Chinese learned of a land at the far end of the far sea, reached by the largest ships in the world, where wheat grew in grains of extraordinary size and the sheep were as tall as donkeys; the marvels grew with every stage of the relay.

The entry belongs to the same harvest as the atlas's *Wùsīlí*, *Ègēntuó*, and *Sījiālǐyě*, the Egypt, Alexandria, and Sicily that Zhao's Arab informants gave him, and it shows the naming pattern at its limit. China did not name the Maghreb; it named what the Arabs called their own far west, a dynasty rather than a place, heard across the full width of the world. The one Chinese name for northwest Africa is thus a Berber confraternity's Arabic title worn as a country: at maximum distance, the difference between a state, a dynasty, and a land disappears, and whatever name survives the journey becomes all three.
