The Chinese name for the Eastern Roman Empire from the Sui and Tang periods onward, replacing the older *Dàqín* in the dynastic annals: both Tang histories carry accounts of *Fúlǐn*, its capital, its walls, and its embassies, and the name persists in Song usage. The characters are a transcription, not a description; the scholarly consensus derives them from the Iranian name for Rome carried by the traders of Central Asia, the word written *frwm* in Parthian, with Sogdian and Bactrian cognates, whose shape the Middle Chinese pronunciation of the characters closely renders.

The page's two Chinese names for Rome thus differ in kind, not just in date. *Dàqín* was a Chinese idea, the "Great Qin," a mirror-empire imagined at the far end of the world; *Fúlǐn* is a foreign sound faithfully copied, the Silk Road's own word for Rome delivered to the annalists by the people who actually went there. Between the Han and the Tang, China's knowledge of Rome improved from a legend to a name, and the name it settled on was Persian. Rome reached China the same way Chang'an reached the west on this atlas's maps: not as its own word, but as the road's word for it.
