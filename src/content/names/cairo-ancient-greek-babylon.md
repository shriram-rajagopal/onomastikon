*Babylṓn* (Βαβυλών) is the Greek name of the fortress town at the head of the Nile Delta, on the east bank opposite the pyramids, that underlies Old Cairo. Diodorus, in the first century BCE, records the legend that explained the transplanted name: captives brought from Mesopotamian Babylon by a conquering pharaoh were said to have settled and named the place. Strabo, who saw it a generation later, describes a strong natural fortress held by one of Egypt's three Roman legions. The legend is almost certainly folk etymology; modern scholarship suspects the Greek name reshaped some Egyptian original, though no candidate has won acceptance, and the entry keeps the derivation open.

The name is the atlas's best example of a toponym stranded far from its family. Its consonants belong to *Bāb-ili*, "gate of the god," on the Euphrates, and the family tree binds this entry to that page; but nothing else connects the two places except the name and a story invented to excuse it. What the name did next matters more than where it came from: the fortress was the hinge of the Arab conquest of Egypt, besieged and taken in 640, and Fustat was founded in its shadow, which is why Cairo's page begins here. The most famous city-name of Mesopotamia thus ends its ancient career in Egypt, guarding the river crossing where the next civilization's capital would grow; Babylon's name, like its captives in the legend, finished its service far from home.
