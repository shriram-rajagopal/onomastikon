*Ephthalîtai* is the Greek name of the White Huns, and Prokopios's introduction of it is one of the most careful paragraphs of ethnography in Byzantine literature. Opening his Persian war with Pērōz's catastrophe, he explains that the Ephthalites are of Hunnic stock and name yet mingle with no other Huns: they are not nomads, they hold land and cities, they alone among Huns are white-bodied and comely, they live under one king and a lawful polity, and they bury their dead. The name itself continues the dynasty's own, which the coins of Bactria write *ēbodalo*; Greek heard it through Persian report, for Byzantium knew these Huns only across the width of its enemy.

The entry is the page's exercise in classification under strain. Prokopios inherited "Hun" as a category meaning everything Rome had learned from Attila, then met, in intelligence from the Persian front, a people who broke every clause of the definition while bearing the name; his solution, Huns in name and race but not in life, preserves the category by hollowing it. The phrase he coined for them, the White Huns, has organized and bedeviled steppe history ever since, a reminder that the atlas's families sort names, and names, as the Hephthalites prove, are the last thing to respect the sorting.
