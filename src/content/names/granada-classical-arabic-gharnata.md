*Gharnāṭa* (غرناطة) is the Arabic name of Granada, a name that steps into history in 1013, when the Zirid emirs abandoned declining Elvira and moved its people to the stronger hill-site that already bore this name as a village or quarter. Under the Naṣrids it named the last Islamic state of Iberia, and it received the fullest civic biography any Andalusi city ever got: Ibn al-Khaṭīb, vizier of the Alhambra, titled his great history of the city *al-Iḥāṭa fī akhbār Gharnāṭa*, "the complete account of the reports of Gharnāṭa." The name's own origin, though, is disputed in the classic manner: a Romance *granata*, "pomegranate," which the city's arms still advertise; an Arabic "hill of strangers"; or a pre-Roman survival, with no reading commanding assent.

The pomegranate deserves the second look, because it shows folk etymology winning a whole country. Whatever *Gharnāṭa* first meant, Romance speakers heard "pomegranate," Castile took the city in 1492 with the fruit already on its banner, and Spanish *granada* means both the city and the pomegranate to this day, the false etymology made true by heraldry and habit. Against Fez's pickaxe legends, which stayed stories, Granada's fruit became fact: the misreading was adopted, painted, and planted until it was simply the truth. Names on this atlas usually carry their pasts; this one had a past assigned to it retroactively, and wears it in every Spanish dictionary.
