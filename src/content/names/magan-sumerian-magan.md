The Sumerian name of the copper-land down the Gulf, *Magan*, written *má-gan* with the place-determinative and entering the record in the Sargonic period. In Sumerian texts it is above all a name for material: Gudea of Lagash records bringing diorite from Magan to carve into his statues, and the phrase "diorite of Magan" became almost a formula, the black stone of royal statuary named for its far source. The Ur III archives document the working trade behind the formula, boats of the merchants of Ur going down to Magan with wool and garments and returning with copper, and the literary tradition keeps the land in view a while longer, pairing Magan with Dilmun and Meluhha as the exotic south from which good things come by sea. The name's own meaning is unknown, and it is likely the local country's name as the scribes of the plain received it.

On this page *Magan* is the anchor, the earliest and plainest form of a name whose later history is all argument. Within Sumerian itself the word is concrete to the point of homeliness: not a myth-country like Dilmun across the water, but a supplier, the place your statue and your kettle ultimately came from. That very concreteness is what gives the page's later entries their interest, because when the name possibly resurfaces after a thousand years in Persian and Greek dress, it does so stripped of all this commercial reality, a label on a remote province. Here, at the head of the page, it still smells of copper smoke.
