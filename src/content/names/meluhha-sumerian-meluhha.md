The Sumerian name of the farthest trade-land, *Meluḫḫa*, written *me-luḫ-ḫa* with the place-determinative, attested from the Sargonic period through the Ur III archives and the Old Babylonian literary tradition. Gudea of Lagash gathers Meluhhan goods for the building of his temple, and the administrative record gives the name a domestic turn: Ur III tablets from the Girsu region document a "Meluhha village," a settled community of Meluhhans farming Sumerian land under Sumerian administration, foreigners from the edge of the world entered in the ledgers like anyone else. In the literary texts the land is a standing image of the exotic south; *Enki and the World Order* blesses Meluhha's trees and birds and its gold and tin, the poets' inventory of a country most Sumerians knew only as cargo.

What this entry preserves may be more than a Sumerian word: since the name arrived with the traders themselves, *Meluḫḫa* is the best candidate we have for the Indus civilization's own name for itself or its land, caught secondhand by scribes who could not have cared less that they were doing so. The Indus script being unread, every other name of that civilization is a modern label; this one alone was written down while the cities lived, by people who had met them. If the identification holds, the only surviving name of the largest civilization of the Bronze Age is a foreign clerk's spelling of a word no one now can hear properly, which is as sharp a lesson in the fragility of names as this atlas contains.
