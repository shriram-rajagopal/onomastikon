*Âl-i ʿOsmân* (آل عثمان), "the House of Osman," is the dynasty's own name for itself, and it stands in the title of the first Ottoman history ever written: Ahmedî's verse chronicle, the *Dâstân-ı Tevârîh-i Mülûk-i Âl-i Osmân*, "the tale of the histories of the kings of the House of Osman," appended to his *İskendernâme* and presented to the sultan Mehmed I around 1412. The construction is Persian, *āl* "house, family" with the connecting *-i*, wrapped around the Arabic name ʿUthmān that the founder bore in its Turkish shape, Osman: three languages in three words, which is the early Ottoman court in miniature. From the same name Europe made "Ottoman," and Turkish itself *Osmanlı*, "of the House of Osman."

The endonym is the atlas's last, and its logic closes a long series. The atlas's states have named themselves for lands, peoples, cities, and, in the caliphate's case, an argument; the Ottomans named themselves for one man, a frontier bey dead around 1324 whose personal name became an empire, an ethnicity in foreign eyes, and eventually the English word for a footstool. No other polity in these pages is so completely a family name worn by a state. The chronicle that first wrote the name down was finished in the dynasty's darkest decade, between Timur's hammer and the civil war; the house survived, and the name Ahmedî fixed on paper in 1412 had, within his readers' lifetimes, taken Constantinople.
