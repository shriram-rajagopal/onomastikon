The Arabic name for Naples in al-Idrīsī's *Nuzhat al-mushtāq*, which calls it *madīnat Nābul al-sāḥiliyya*, "the coastal city of Nābul," and measures the sailing distances from its harbor to the islands of the bay. The shape records the Latin name in its Italian vernacular development rather than the classical form: by the twelfth century *Neāpolis* had contracted on local tongues toward *Napoli*, and Arabic, which lacks the sound *p*, rendered the result with *b*, exactly as it does elsewhere on the Italian coast.

The entry's interest is where it was written. Al-Idrīsī described Naples from Palermo, ninety leagues down the same coast, working in Arabic at the court of a Norman king; this is not a distant report from Baghdad but Mediterranean geography written in Arabic from inside Italy. The city the Greeks had founded as the "new city" was, by the time this name was recorded, more than sixteen centuries old, among the oldest continuously inhabited names in al-Idrīsī's whole book, and still called new in every language that spoke of it.
