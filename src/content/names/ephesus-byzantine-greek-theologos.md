The medieval name of Ephesus, taken from its patron rather than its past. As the classical city emptied, its harbor silted and its plain grown unhealthy, the population withdrew to the fortified hill around the great Basilica of St. John, and the settlement came to be called after the church's saint: *ho Theológos*, "the Theologian," the standing Byzantine epithet of John the Evangelist, whose tomb the basilica enclosed. Foss traces the shift through the middle Byzantine centuries, when documents and travelers name the town Theologos and "Ephesos" recedes into the titulature of its archbishop.

The entry records something rarer than a renaming: a name that moved. Every other form on this page, from Hittite *Apaša* onward, points at the same harbor city; *Theológos* points at the hill a mile away where the survivors regrouped, and it stuck because that hill was now the city. Turkish carried the name forward as *Ayasoluk*, from *Hágios Theológos*, and the modern town of Selçuk still stands on the saint's hill while the tourist buses drive to ruins labeled Ephesus. The ancient name kept the ruins; the saint's name kept the living town.
