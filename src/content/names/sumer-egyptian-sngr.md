The Egyptian name for southern Mesopotamia, *Sngr*, first attested in the annals of Thutmose III at Karnak in the mid-fifteenth century BCE, where gifts from *Sngr* appear among the offerings of the great powers that courted Egypt after its Syrian campaigns. The name remains in use through the Eighteenth and Nineteenth Dynasties; the battle reliefs of Sety I at Karnak write it in the group spelling *sꜣngꜣr*, the form shown above. Throughout its attested life the word designates the Kassite-ruled kingdom of Babylon, the land that a millennium earlier had been Sumer.

*Sngr* belongs to the family of second-millennium names for the Mesopotamian south that also surfaces as Hittite *Šanḫara* and, much later, as the Hebrew *Šinʿār* of Genesis: a name of contested origin, connected by some scholars to the Kassite tribal name *Samḫarû* and by others, more distantly, to the name of Sumer itself. What the Egyptian branch records most clearly is a gap in time: Egypt never knew Sumer, whose cities had fallen before the Middle Kingdom rose, and when the Nile finally learned a name for the far river-country, the land had already been Babylonia for centuries. The pharaohs' scribes wrote down a name for the place where civilization began without knowing there was a beginning to name.
