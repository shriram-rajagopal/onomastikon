The Sanskrit *Hūṇa*, attested from the mid-fifth century when the name enters the epigraphy of the embattled Gupta empire: Skandagupta's Bhitari pillar records his violent collision with the Hūṇas, and within two generations the inscriptions name Hūṇa kings, Toramāṇa and the temple-burning Mihirakula, ruling in India itself. The invaders were the Alchon and their successors, the "Iranian Huns" of the northwest, kin to the Hephthalites treated on their own page rather than to Attila's horde; Sanskrit tradition also knows the *Śveta-hūṇa*, the White Huns of the historians. The name then outlived the invasions by centuries, settling into the epic catalogues and the lists of northwestern peoples as a standing item of Sanskrit ethnography.

The entry shows the name's southern branch doing what the western branch did at the same moment, and to eerily similar effect: in the same generation that *Hunni* came to mean the destroyer of Rome's order, *Hūṇa* came to mean the destroyer of India's classical golden age, two literatures independently making one steppe name the sound of an era ending. That the two literatures meant different armies, joined only through the contested middle links this page's Iranian entries carry, is the family's central irony. East and west, "Hun" became the word for the world before it broke; the peoples who broke it may never have shared more than the name.
