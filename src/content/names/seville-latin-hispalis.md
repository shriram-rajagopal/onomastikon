*Hispalis* is the Latin name of Seville, prominent from Caesar's civil war and Pliny's survey of Baetica, where it appears as a colonia and conventus capital, the river port of the province. The name is pre-Roman and unexplained; it has been guessed at as Phoenician since antiquity, plausibly for a trading settlement at the head of a navigable estuary, but no derivation holds. The most engaging failure is native: Isidore, the city's own bishop and the last encyclopedist of the ancient world, derived his see's name in the *Etymologies* from houses driven on piles in swampy ground, a folk etymology delivered from the one desk in Europe best placed to know better.

Isidore's guess is the entry's real cargo, because of who made it. The *Etymologies* rescued the learning of antiquity precisely by explaining names, and for his own city the method produced a story rather than a history, which measures how completely the name's origin was already lost by the seventh century. There is also a neat succession in the manuscript record: the bishop of Hispalis wrote the book that medieval Europe read for five hundred years, and the city he wrote it in would spend most of those centuries under another name, *Ishbīliya*, that his Latin readers would not have recognized. The last scholar of the ancient city could not explain its name; the next civilization to hold it did not try, and simply said it their own way.
