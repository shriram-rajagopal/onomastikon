The Arabic name of the city of Rome, *Rūmiya*, kept carefully distinct from *al-Rūm*, the Arabic name of the Roman-Byzantine power: one word for the empire the caliphate fought every summer, another for the old city in the far west that almost no one from the caliphate ever saw. The geographers' accounts of *Rūmiya*, gathered at fullest length in Yāqūt, describe a city of marvels at second and third hand, thousands of columns, churches roofed in bronze, markets of brass and gold, a wonder-catalogue that grew with distance and repetition.

The split between the two words is the entry's lesson, and it is the page's story told from outside. Arabic met "Rome" twice, as a state and as a place, and discovered they were no longer in the same location: the state was in Constantinople, where the emperor of *al-Rūm* reigned in Greek, while the place was a half-legendary city of columns beyond the sea. The name *Rūmiya* holds the residue of that separation, Rome after the empire had moved out. On this page the Latin *Roma* is the working name of a capital; the Arabic is what the same name becomes when the capital has been gone for centuries, a city surviving in rumor, magnificent in proportion to how few of the tellers had been there.
