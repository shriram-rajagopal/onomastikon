The Arabic name for Chang'an in the merchant literature of the ninth and tenth centuries, most famously in the *Akhbār al-Ṣīn wa-l-Hind* of 851, the earliest surviving Arabic account of China, compiled from the reports of traders on the sea route through Sīrāf. The form does not transcribe the Chinese name at all; it is the Iranian caravan name, Sogdian *Khumdān*, carried into Arabic along with the trade itself, the same shape the Nestorian monument at Chang'an had rendered in Syriac letters in 781.

Arabic thus completes a pattern this page already displays: Byzantine Greek *Khoubdán* and now Arabic *Khumdān* are parallel westward branches of the Sogdian name, and neither Rome's heirs nor Baghdad's merchants ever called the city anything the Tang court would have recognized. The Chinese name traveled with the empire's documents; the Sogdian name traveled with the road. When the road reached the Arabic-speaking world, it delivered its own name for the greatest city on earth, and the empire's name arrived centuries too late to compete.
