The everyday Byzantine name for the strait: *to Stenón*, "the Narrows," a bare adjective promoted to a proper name. The classical *Bósporos* remained available to the learned, but the chronicles, saints' lives, and documents of the medieval city speak of the Stenon, the narrow water that carried the City's grain, guarded its flank, and separated its European and Asian suburbs; Janin's topography of Byzantine Constantinople treats the strait's shores under this name.

The page now holds three names for one strait, and each carries a different logic. The Greek myth-name remembers Io's crossing, an ox ford; the Arabic name records the power that guarded it, the gulf of Constantinople; and the people who actually held the water called it nothing grander than the Narrows. Outsiders named the strait for what it meant, a passage or an obstacle; its owners named it for what it was. Possession, it turns out, needs no ornament: everyone else reached for a story, and the Byzantines reached for a measurement.
