The Latin *Ararat* survives by half a translator's decision. Jerome, making the Vulgate at the end of the fourth century, met the Hebrew *Ăraraṭ* three times and split it: in Jeremiah's summons of the northern kingdoms he transliterated, *regna Ararat*, but in the flood narrative and the murder of Sennacherib he translated, so that the ark of the Latin Bible comes to rest *super montes Armeniae*, on the mountains of Armenia, and the assassins flee to the land of the Armenians. Jerome knew, as the scholarship of his day did, that Ararat meant the old northern kingdom whose land was now Armenia, and he modernized the geography where the story seemed to want a real place.

The consequence is one of the atlas's quietest ironies. Because Jerome translated Genesis, the medieval Latin West grew up with an ark that landed in "Armenia," no Ararat in the verse at all; the name's modern fame, and its eventual attachment to the great volcano this page maps, flowed through other channels, the Hebrew text and its vernacular translations, until "Mount Ararat" became one of the most recognized mountain names on earth. The Latin entry is thus the family's near-miss: the one great transmission language that chose, at the crucial verse, to explain the name instead of passing it on, and so let the ark's mountain slip through its fingers.
