The Persian name of the steppe world, *Tūrān*, the land of Tūr: in the Shāhnāma's frame of sacred history the world was divided among the sons of Ferēdūn, and Tūr received the lands beyond the Oxus, whose people, the Turanians under their great king Afrāsiyāb, wage the epic's central, generations-long war against Īrān. The dispute is a referent question rather than a doubtful form: by Ferdowsi's day *Tūrān* was identified with the Turks, but that identification is a documented anachronism, made only after the sixth century when Turks first reached the Iranian frontier, and the name's older layers, going back to the Avesta's *Tūra*, denote the Iranian-speaking nomads of the steppe, the very Saka world this page records.

The entry therefore names this page's subject in the strangest way any language does: from inside the same family. Greek, Akkadian, and Hebrew named the steppe peoples as outsiders looking north; Persian named them as estranged kin, descendants of one grandfather divided by an inheritance, and then spent the national epic fighting them. When the real steppe Iranians were replaced by Turks, the name simply slid onto the newcomers and the epic went on making sense, because *Tūrān* had never really named a people; it named the other side of the river, whoever lived there. Scythia's page ends with the one name that remembers the steppe not as a place on a map but as the half of the family that got the other half of the world.
