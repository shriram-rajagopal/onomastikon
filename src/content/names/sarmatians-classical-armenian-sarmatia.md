The Classical Armenian *Sarmatia*, carried by the *Ašxarhacʿoycʿ*, the seventh-century Armenian Geography attributed to Anania of Širak, which inherits Ptolemy's world-picture and with it his two Sarmatias, European and Asian, divided at the Don. The Armenian geographer does not copy blindly: his chapters on the Sarmatian lands are thick with peoples Ptolemy never knew, Huns, Khazars, Bulgars, the actual seventh-century occupants of the steppe, catalogued inside the old frame; the name is Greek scholarship naturalized into Armenian letters, given here in the Armenian script with the loan declined as an Armenian noun.

The entry is the family's afterlife in miniature. By the time Armenian wrote the name down, no Sarmatian confederation had existed for two centuries; *Sarmatia* survives in the Geography as pure frame, a classical container holding Turkic contents. That a mountain kingdom south of the Caucasus kept the steppe's obsolete name current is a fact about the prestige of Ptolemaic geography, not about the steppe, and it makes this the page's clearest specimen of a name outliving its people as an instrument of scholarship. The Armenians, who fought actual steppe raiders through their whole history, catalogued those raiders under the name of nomads their grandfathers' grandfathers had never met.
