The medieval Latin continuation of *Mare Caspium*, carrying the classical name and the classical error together. Isidore of Seville, whose *Etymologiae* transmitted ancient geography to the Middle Ages, lists *Caspius* among the names the world-encircling Ocean takes from neighboring regions: for Isidore the Caspian is not a sea beside the Ocean but the Ocean itself, wearing a local name. The mappae mundi drew it accordingly, a gulf opening from the encircling waters, and seven centuries of Latin geography repeated the picture. Then the Franciscan William of Rubruck, sent across the steppe to the Mongol court in 1253, passed along the sea's northern side in person and wrote the correction plainly: it is not true what Isidore says, for the sea nowhere touches the Ocean, but is on every side surrounded by land.

Herodotus had said the same thing seventeen centuries earlier, and the report of Alexander's surveyors had overruled him; Isidore inherited the surveyors' gulf, and Rubruck's own eyes overruled Isidore. The name *Mare Caspium* held steady through the whole dispute, while the thing it named changed shape twice, from enclosed sea to gulf of Ocean and back. No entry on this page shows more plainly that a name can be far more stable than the geography behind it: the medieval West knew exactly what the sea was called through centuries in which nobody could say what it was.
