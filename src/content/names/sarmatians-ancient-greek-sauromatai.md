*Sauromátai* is the older of the two Greek names on this page, the form Herodotus uses for the people beyond the Don, three days' journey east of the Scythians. His fourth book gives them the most famous origin story in ancient ethnography: they are the children of Scythian youths and Amazons, which is his narrative dress for the report that Sauromatian women rode, hunted, and fought, and could not marry until they had killed an enemy; the burials of armed women that archaeology has since recovered from their steppe sit strikingly close to the tale. The name stays standard through the classical period, and its own etymology, like most steppe names in Greek garb, is uncertain.

The entry's philological interest is that it may not name the same people as its neighbor. Whether Herodotus's *Sauromátai* and the later *Sarmátai* are one name eroded or two groups conflated is a genuinely unresolved question, and this atlas keeps both spellings because the doubt is the datum: the Greek record itself cannot say whether it watched one people's name change or watched one name swallow a second people. Either way, the older form preserves what the later one forgets, the fighting women who so struck the first Greek observers that they explained an entire nation as descended from Amazons.
