*İznik* (ازنيق) is the Turkish name of Nicaea, in use from Orhan's capture of the city in 1331, one of the young Ottoman state's first great prizes; under the sultans the lakeside town found a new fame, its kilns producing the İznik tiles and ceramics that line the empire's mosques. The name is not the Greek name borrowed but a Greek phrase fossilized: *eis Níkaian*, "to Nicaea," the preposition heard as part of the word and the ending worn off, the exact process that made *eis tḕn pólin* into Istanbul, and the pair are the standard textbook examples of it.

The mechanism is the entry's cargo, for what it reveals about how the handover of Anatolia actually happened. Names borrowed from maps and chanceries keep their nominative dignity; names borrowed from conversation come wrapped in grammar, and *İznik* preserves the shape of a question asked on a Bithynian road, where are you going, and its answer, to Nicaea. The Turks who settled Bithynia learned its places from Greek neighbors in Greek sentences, not from Greek documents, and the preposition fused to the town's name is the fossil of that spoken cohabitation. The city of the councils, whose name Latin turned into doctrine, ends as a piece of everyday speech overheard and kept; the creed preserved the queen's name, and the road preserved the phrase.
