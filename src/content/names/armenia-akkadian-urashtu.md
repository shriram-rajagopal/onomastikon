In the Babylonian column of Darius's trilingual at Behistun, the province that the Old Persian text calls *Armina* is named *Uraštu*. This is not a new word but the old Assyrian and Babylonian name of the kingdom of Urartu, which had ruled the same Armenian highland until its fall two centuries earlier. The Persian chancellery, writing in classical Mesopotamian cuneiform for its Babylonian subjects, reached for the name those readers already knew for that country, even though the kingdom of Urartu was long gone and a new people and a new name had taken its place.

The equation is one of the most instructive moments in the whole transmission of the name. It shows two naming traditions meeting on a single rock: the Iranian *Armina*, which would become the world's word for the land, and the Mesopotamian *Urarṭu*, which the Bible would carry forward as *Ararat*. For the duration of one inscription they denote exactly the same province, the new name and the old, and the Babylonian scribe's choice records the simple geographic truth that Achaemenid Armenia occupied the ground of fallen Urartu.
