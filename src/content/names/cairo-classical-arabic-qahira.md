*al-Qāhira* (القاهرة), "the Victorious," is the name of the palace city the Fatimid general Jawhar founded north of Fustat in 969, after conquering Egypt for the caliph al-Muʿizz. The tradition reported by the Egyptian historians, at length in al-Maqrīzī, holds that the city was first called al-Manṣūriyya, after the caliph's father's capital in Ifriqiya, and renamed al-Qāhira by al-Muʿizz on his arrival; a famous founding legend adds an astrological gloss, that the planet Mars, *al-Najm al-Qāhir*, the subduing star, was in the ascendant when the walls were traced, making the city named for the planet as much as for the victory. Through Italian *Cairo* the name entered the European languages.

Among the victory names of this atlas, *al-Qāhira* is the one that grew into its boast. Founded as a closed royal enclosure, a rectangle of palaces the public could not enter, it became under the Ayyubids and Mamluks the open metropolis of the Islamic world, the seat to which even the caliphal title retreated after Baghdad fell in 1258. The name's career thus ran opposite to Baghdad's on this cluster: there the official name lost to the ground's old name, while here the dynasty's chosen name conquered completely, erasing al-Manṣūriyya within a decade and eventually standing for the whole ancient stack of cities beneath it. The Fatimids' astrologers, for once, advertised accurately; the subduing star's city subdued even its own past.
