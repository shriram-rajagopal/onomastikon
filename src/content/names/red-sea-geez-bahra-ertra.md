The Geʿez name of the Red Sea, *Baḥrä ʾĒrtrā*, "the sea of Ertra": the Semitic word for sea in construct with the Greek name, for *ʾĒrtrā* is *Erythrá*, the "Red" of the Greek geographers, borrowed whole and worn smooth. The sea in question was Aksum's own front door, the water its ports faced and its trade crossed, and the kingdom that adopted the Christian scriptures in the fourth century took the sea's Greek name along with them, fixing it in the Ethiopic Bible and the literature that followed.

The entry is the *erythra* family's strangest fate. Greek coined the name from an unexplained redness; Latin translated it; the Hebrew and Syriac Bibles kept their own reeds; but Geʿez, the one language of the family actually spoken on the sea's shore, adopted the foreign name for its own water, a coastal civilization calling its sea by the word of merchants from another one. The name then outlived every party to the exchange in the most unexpected way: when a modern state was carved from Aksum's old coast, its colonizers named it from the same Greek adjective by their own route, and *Eritrea* stands today on the map of nations where Geʿez had domesticated the word as *ʾĒrtrā* fifteen centuries before, a country named, twice over, after the color of its water in someone else's language.
