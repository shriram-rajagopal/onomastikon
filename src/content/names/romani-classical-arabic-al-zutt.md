The Arabic name *al-Zuṭṭ*, an arabization of the Indian ethnonym Jāṭ, applied in the early Islamic centuries to communities of Indian origin settled in the marshlands of lower Iraq, buffalo-keepers and soldiers transplanted by Sasanian and then Muslim rulers, prominent enough to mount a famous revolt against the caliphate in the ninth century that al-Ṭabarī chronicles at length. Later Arabic usage stretched the word into a general term for Indian-descended wanderers, and the Cairo Geniza and Syrian sources know Zuṭṭ far west of Iraq. The dispute is whether any of this belongs to the Romani: the Zuṭṭ were real and Indian, the Romani's ancestors were real and Indian and crossed the same lands in the same centuries, and scholarship has connected and disconnected the two for two hundred years without a verdict.

The entry earns its place as the page's cautionary layer. The names above it show societies mislabeling the Romani with confidence; *al-Zuṭṭ* shows the opposite failure, a label so broad that historians cannot now tell whether the people of this page are inside it. Somewhere among the Zuṭṭ of the chronicles the proto-Romani may well be standing, unmarked, one Indian migrant community among several the word swallowed. Every other name on the page names the Romani and means someone else; the Arabic name may mean the Romani and names everyone.
