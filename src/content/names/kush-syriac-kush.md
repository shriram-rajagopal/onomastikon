*Kūš* is the Syriac form of the biblical name, the Hebrew consonants carried unchanged into the Peshitta. Because the Syriac Old Testament was translated from the Hebrew in the second century rather than from the Greek, it keeps the Egyptian-derived name at every verse where the Septuagint had written Ethiopia: the son of Ham in Genesis, Tirhakah king of Kush in Kings, the land beyond the rivers of Kush in Isaiah. From the Bible the name passed into Syriac usage generally, with the ethnic *Kūšāyā* for its people, in the commentaries of Ephrem and the chronicles of the Syriac churches that later kept contact with the Christian kingdoms of Nubia.

The entry marks a line on the map of the Bible's daughter languages. Greek, Latin, Armenian, and Geʽez, reading the Septuagint, say Ethiopia; Syriac and the Aramaic of the Talmud, reading the Hebrew, say Kush. The Egyptian name went east with the Semitic Bible and the Greek description went west with the Greek one, and the Peshitta's three letters are the eastern frontier of that division. On this page they are the proof that the old name did not need Egypt to survive: it lived on in the churches of Mesopotamia on the strength of a translation alone.
