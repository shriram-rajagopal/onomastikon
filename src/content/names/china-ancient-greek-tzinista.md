The last Greek name of China before the ancient tradition closed, *Tzínista*, in the *Christian Topography* of Cosmas Indicopleustes, the Alexandrian merchant-turned-monk who had sailed the Indian Ocean routes around 550. Cosmas locates *Tzinista* as the country of silk at the world's eastern edge, "beyond which there is no other country, for the ocean encircles it on the east," and he is the first Western writer to grasp the two roads that reached it, the overland route through Persia and the sea route past Taprobane, modern Sri Lanka. The form is transparently the Iranian *Čīnistān*, "the land of Čīn," picked up from the Persian merchants who worked the ocean trade, the same name the atlas records in its Sogdian dress as *Chinastan*.

The entry completes the page's Greek triad and its lesson. Classical Greek had named China twice without knowing it, *Sêres* by the overland silk and *Sînai* by the sea, and Ptolemy mapped them as two countries; Cosmas, who had actually traded on the ocean, brought back a third name that quietly reunified them, one silk-land with two roads. Each of the three Greek names came in along a trade route, and each kept the shape of its carrier: *Sêres* smells of the caravan, *Sînai* of the monsoon ports, and *Tzínista* of the Persian middlemen who, by late antiquity, owned the conversation. Greece never did learn what China called itself; it learned, three times over, whom one had to ask.
