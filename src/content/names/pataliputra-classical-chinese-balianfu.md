*Bāliánfú yì*, "the town of Bāliánfú," is the Chinese name of Pataliputra as recorded by the monk Faxian, who reached Magadha around 405 CE on his overland pilgrimage from Chang'an to the Buddhist holy land. The first three characters transcribe a Middle Indo-Aryan pronunciation of the city's name, the syllables chosen for sound rather than sense; the fourth, *yì*, is the plain Chinese word for a town. Faxian found the city still impressive and the palace attributed to Aśoka standing, walls and gates carved, he wrote, as no human hands could have made; he stayed three years, copying texts and learning Sanskrit.

The transcription is this family's easternmost member, and it reverses the geometry of every other exonym on the page: the Greek and Latin names came from ambassadors of empires to India's west looking in, while the Chinese name came from a pilgrim for whom India was the West, the source-country of scripture. Where Megasthenes measured walls and towers, Faxian counted monasteries. The same city, transcribed at opposite ends of the ancient world's road network, entered Greek as a marvel of size and Chinese as a station of holiness.
