The one appearance of Ur in Greek, *Ouríē*, in a fragment of Eupolemus, a Jewish historian writing in Greek in the second century BCE, preserved at third hand: Alexander Polyhistor excerpted him, and Eusebius copied the excerpt. Eupolemus says Abraham was born "in the Babylonian city Kamarina, which some call the city *Ouríē*," adding the gloss that the name means "city of the Chaldeans"; the form is his hellenization of the *ʾÛr* of Genesis, the biblical *Ur of the Chaldees*, fitted with a Greek feminine ending. By his day the real city had been dead for centuries, its mound already sanded over, and neither he nor his readers could have pointed to it on any map.

Elsewhere on this page Ur is a place: Sumerian and Akkadian scribes date documents there, and even the Latin form belongs to working geography. *Ouríē* is the moment the city finished becoming literature. It survives in Greek not as a port or a province but as a birthplace in someone else's scripture, wrapped in a second name, *Kamarina*, that no cuneiform text knows, and glossed by a historian guessing at a language he could not read. The city of the moon-god's ziggurat entered the classical world only as Abraham's hometown, remembered by everyone and locatable by no one: the first city in this atlas to outlive its geography entirely.
