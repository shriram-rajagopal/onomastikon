*Ānguó* (安國), "the state of An," is the Tang name for Bukhara. The dynastic histories catalogue the Sogdian city-states as the "nine clans of Zhaowu," assigning each principality a single-character Chinese surname: Kang for Samarkand, An for Bukhara, Shi, Cao, Mi, and the rest for their neighbors, a whole foreign region rendered as a family register. Sogdians who settled in China took these surnames as their own, so a Bukharan merchant in Chang'an was surnamed An, exactly as his Samarkandi partner was surnamed Kang; the account of the Western Regions describes the An state's walls, its trade, and its embassies to the Tang court.

The system produced one of history's sharpest ironies of nomenclature, and the entry should not look away from it. The general An Lushan, whose rebellion of 755 broke the Tang dynasty's back, bore the Bukharan surname, his father's people being Sogdians of the An state; the city's Chinese name is therefore written into the catastrophe that ended China's central Asian ambitions and, with them, the Sogdian trading world itself. No other naming convention on this atlas swallowed a people so completely into another's bureaucracy: Bukhara entered China as a surname, and the surname made history that unmade Bukhara's world. Filing systems are never neutral; this one changed hands from the ledger to the battlefield.
