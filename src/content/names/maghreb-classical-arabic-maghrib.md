*al-Maghrib* (المغرب) is the noun of place from the root *gh-r-b*, "to set (of the sun)": the west, the sunset-land, paired in Arabic geography with its mirror *al-Mashriq*, the sunrise-land of the east. Applied to Africa west of Egypt from the conquests onward, it became the fixed name of the region in Arabic geography, subdivided by the geographers into the near, middle, and farthest west, with *al-Maghrib al-Aqṣā*, the farthest, roughly modern Morocco, which still bears the name as a state: *al-Mamlaka al-Maghribiyya*, the western kingdom. The name remains in full living use.

The entry is the atlas's clearest case of a name that is purely a bearing. Libya was a people's name and Africa a province's; *al-Maghrib* is neither, only a direction from a center, and the center was new, the Arabia and Iraq of the caliphate. To accept the name was to accept the orientation, a whole region defined for thirteen centuries by where it stood relative to someone else's sunrise. Its exact structural twin sits at the other end of this phase of the atlas: Khurasan, "the land of the sunrise," named from the same center facing the other way. Between them the two names sketch the caliphate's mental map in two words, a world with its back to the Atlantic and its face to the dawn, and both edges named by the light.
