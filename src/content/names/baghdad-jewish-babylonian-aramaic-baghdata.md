*Baḡdāṯā* (בגדתא) is the Jewish Babylonian Aramaic form of the name, attested in the Babylonian Talmud, where sages are identified by the toponym in passing legal discussions; the place it names was a modest settlement among the towns of Sasanian Babylonia, home to a Jewish community like dozens of others in the Talmud's landscape. The form is the same Iranian name with the Aramaic determined ending, and the Talmud's redaction was complete generations before al-Manṣūr's surveyors arrived in 762, making these citations the name's clearest pre-Islamic attestation.

The entry's whole value lies in what the text does not know. The Talmud records *Baḡdāṯā* exactly as it records any minor river town, without emphasis, because in its day there was nothing to emphasize; that innocence is what makes it evidence, proof untouched by hindsight that the name predates the caliphate. There is also a quiet continuity beneath it: when the Abbasids built their capital, the academies of Sura and Pumbedita eventually moved there, and the geonim of Baghdad led the Jewish world from a city their own Talmud had already named. Most pre-histories on this atlas are recovered from royal inscriptions; Baghdad's survives in a footnote of rabbinic law, the great city visible for a moment as the village it was.
