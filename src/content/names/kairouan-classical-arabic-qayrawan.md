*al-Qayrawān* (القيروان) has named the city since ʿUqba ibn Nāfiʿ pitched his permanent camp there around 670. The word is not Arabic: it is the Persian *kārwān*, "caravan," which in the usage of the conquest armies meant a military column and its encampment, and the geographers knew it; Yāqūt's entry explains the name as an arabized Persian word for a camp or a body of troops. The camp became the misr of Ifriqiya, then the metropolis of the whole Islamic west, and the common noun hardened into a proper name that the city has carried for thirteen centuries.

The etymology is the whole entry, because of the geography folded inside it. A Persian military term travelled with the caliphate's armies across the full width of their world to become the name of the first Islamic city of Africa, two thousand miles from the nearest Persian speaker; the empire's working vocabulary was already so mixed, forty years after the conquests began, that its westernmost foundation was named in the language of its easternmost province. The same word would later reach Europe again by the trade routes as "caravan." On this atlas Persian names usually flow east, to Dàshí and Baghdad's own etymology; al-Qayrawān is the proof they flowed west just as far, in soldiers' slang rather than merchants' ledgers. The holy city of the Maghreb is named in Persian, and the name records how it was born: not founded, encamped.
