*Pérsai* (Πέρσαι) is what the learned historians of Byzantium called the Seljuk Turks. Anna Komnene's *Alexiad*, the great account of her father's reign written around 1148, wars against the *Pérsai* of Anatolia throughout, and the usage runs through Komnenian historiography as a fixed convention; the same authors could use *Toûrkoi* in less elevated registers, but high style demanded the classical name, and the classical name for an empire attacking Rome from the east was Persian. The convention was archaism, not confusion: the historians knew perfectly well who the Seljuks were, and dressed them in Herodotus anyway.

The entry is the atlas's purest specimen of a name as a literary costume. Byzantine classicism required that present enemies wear ancient names, Scythians for steppe nomads, Persians for whoever held Iran and Anatolia's east, so the Seljuks inherited the name of the empire Alexander had destroyed fourteen centuries before them, purely because they occupied its stage. The habit has a mirror on this page's own frontier: the Seljuks called their Anatolian realm Rūm, Rome, while Byzantium called them Persia, each side naming the other with an empire of the dead. On the eleventh-century border the ancient world's great war was still being fought, in vocabulary; the Persians and Romans facing each other across it were Turks and Greeks who had each inherited the other's enemy.
