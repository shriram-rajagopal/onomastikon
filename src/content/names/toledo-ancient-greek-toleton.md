*Tṓlēton* (Τώλητον) is the Greek form of Toletum, carried in Ptolemy's tables of Iberia among the cities of the Carpetani, the peoples of the central plateau. It is a coordinate entry, a name and two numbers in the great Alexandrian gazetteer, its shape the Latin name adjusted to Greek habits with omega and eta stretching the vowels. Ptolemy's Iberia, like Strabo's, was compiled from Roman itineraries and measurements; no Greek is known to have had independent business in the Meseta.

Its value to the page is as a fixed observation between the name's two great careers. Ptolemy catches *Toletum* at its quietest, after Livy's frontier fort and before the Visigothic crown and the Arabic *Ṭulayṭula*, an ordinary inland town among dozens in a table; that such a moment is preserved at all is what the Alexandrian project was for. The atlas's Iberian pages keep meeting Ptolemy this way, at Córdoba and at distant Iathrippa and Makoraba on the Arabian pages, the same catalogue supplying the same service: a second-century snapshot against which every later transformation of a name can be measured. Here the snapshot shows nothing happening, which is exactly what makes it precious; everything this page records happened after it.
