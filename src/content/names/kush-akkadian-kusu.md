*Kūsu* is the Akkadian name of Kush, written KUR.ku-u-si with the land determinative, and it enters the record at the moment Assyria met the Kushites as rulers of Egypt. Esarhaddon's inscriptions after the conquest of Memphis in 671 BCE give Taharqa his title as king of Kush, and the Assyrian king's own titulary expands to "king of Egypt, Paturisi, and Kush," Lower Egypt, Upper Egypt, and the land beyond, the three terms in which Assyria carved up the Nile. The form recurs in Ashurbanipal's accounts of the Kushite retreat, and the Babylonian versions of the Achaemenid lists later carry the same name at the end of Darius's provinces. The spelling writes the *u* long and plainly, and the genitive *Kūsi* of the royal titles is the shape the word most often takes.

The entry is the oldest witness to how the name sounded. The hieroglyphs of *Kꜣš* write a consonant frame around a vowel the script could not show, and the cuneiform supplies it: the long *ū* that Hebrew *Kûš* confirms and that the atlas writes back into the Egyptian entry's reconstruction. The political fact is just as telling. Assyria did not learn the name of Kush by trading up the Nile; it learned it by fighting Kushite kings in the Delta, and the three-part title that put Kush beside Upper and Lower Egypt is the Assyrian scribe's record of what the Twenty-fifth Dynasty had been, an African kingdom that had made Egypt its northern province. Kush entered cuneiform as the name of the enemy who had been holding Memphis.
