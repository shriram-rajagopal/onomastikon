*Hrōm* is the Middle Persian name of the Roman empire, and from the fourth century onward that meant, in practice, the empire ruled from Constantinople. The word is a borrowing from Greek Ῥώμη, its initial *Hr-* preserving the Greek rough breathing that Latin *Roma* never had, and it is attested from the beginning of Sasanian epigraphy through the whole of the Pahlavi literature, where *Hrōm* names the western realm and *Hrōmāy* its people. For the four centuries in which the Sasanian and eastern Roman empires shared a frontier, fought, and made peace, *Hrōm* was Persia's standing word for the power across the border; the entry for Rome records the same form at its origin, and the page for Greece records its counterpart *Yunān*, the Greeks of antiquity and philosophy, from whom the contemporary empire was kept terminologically distinct.

The form documents a fact about the Byzantines that their neighbors' names keep proving: nobody who dealt with them called them Byzantine. The Sasanians, who fought this state longer and harder than any other power, never coined a name for a new Greek empire; to Persia the enemy at Constantinople was simply *Hrōm*, Rome carried on, which is what the Byzantines themselves maintained in calling their land *Rhōmanía*. The word also proved contagious westward of the border it named: Armenian took its *Hoṙom*, recorded on this page, from this Persian form rather than from Greek. Two empires that could agree on almost nothing agreed on this, that the state at Constantinople was Rome; the hostile witness of the Persian chancellery is among the strongest confirmations the Byzantine self-understanding ever received.
