The Byzantine name for the Romani, *Atsínganoi*, a name that belonged to someone else first: the *Athínganoi*, "the untouchables," were an Anatolian heretical sect of the eighth and ninth centuries with a reputation for divination and strict purity taboos, and when a new people of itinerant fortune-tellers appeared in the empire, the dead sect's label slid onto them. The earliest record is not even Greek but Georgian: the *Life of St George the Athonite* tells how Constantine IX brought "Adsincani, renowned sorcerers," to rid the imperial park of wild beasts around 1054, and Balsamon's twelfth-century canon commentary treats the *athínganoi* as a living presence to be kept from the faithful.

The entry is the headwater of the *tsigane* family, and its mechanism deserves the attention. Byzantium did not ask the newcomers who they were; it reached into its own past for the nearest available category, a heresy remembered for magic and untouchability, and named the strangers after its memory of other strangers. From that misapplied label descend the Latin *Cigani* of this page and, beyond the atlas's edge, Tsigane, Zigeuner, Cigány, and țigan, the word by which half of Europe would name the Romani for six centuries, much of that time as a slur. The most consequential name on this page began as a case of mistaken identity inside a single empire's filing system; Europe inherited the file, not the people.
