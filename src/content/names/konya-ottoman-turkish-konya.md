*Konya* (قونيه) is the Turkish form of the name, the Arabic-Persian spelling of Qūniya read with Turkish vowels, in use through the beylik and Ottoman centuries and unchanged today. After the Seljuk sultanate's end the city passed to the Karamanid beys, the Ottomans' most stubborn Anatolian rivals, and fell finally to Mehmed II in the atlas's closing decade; through all of it the city remained what the Seljuks had made it, the shrine-city of Mevlânâ, whose dervish order kept Konya a place of pilgrimage under every subsequent flag.

The Turkish entry closes the ikonion family's twenty-two-century relay, and the closing has a fitting shape: the name's last handover was its gentlest, a respelling of the Arabic respelling of the Greek, each transfer smaller than the last, as if the name were settling. What Turkish added was not sound but finality; there has been no later language to hand the name to, and *Konya* is simply where the chain stopped. Xenophon's unexplained Phrygian word, Paul's tough synagogue town, Cicero's mustering point, Rūmī's Rome; the modern city holds all of it in two syllables that no one has needed to change since the Middle Ages, the atlas's quietest possible ending for one of its longest-running names.
