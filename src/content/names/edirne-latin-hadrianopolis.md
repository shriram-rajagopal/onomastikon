*Hadrianopolis* is the Latin form of the name, the founder-emperor's own language receiving his Greek-built city-name back as a loan. Its fixed place in Latin letters is the thirty-first book of Ammianus Marcellinus, where the historian, a soldier himself, narrates the campaign of 378: the Gothic wagon-ring outside Hadrianopolis, the August heat, the emperor Valens vanishing in the rout, two-thirds of the eastern army destroyed, "no battle in our history except Cannae was such a massacre." Through Ammianus and the church historians the name entered the Latin west's permanent memory as the pattern of catastrophe, cited whenever Rome's fall wanted a beginning.

The Latin entry is thus a case study in how one text can own a name. Latin knew Hadrianopolis for centuries of ordinary provincial existence, but the language's whole surviving investment in the name is concentrated in a single afternoon of 378, so that in Latin the city is the battle, the way Cannae, its own chosen comparison, is a battle before it is a town. The parallel entries on this page show the name's Greek longevity and Turkish afterlife; Latin's share is the shortest and the heaviest, one book of one historian, carrying the moment the empire's soldiers first understood that the walls of the world could fail. Hadrian built the city and Ammianus built its Latin meaning, and only the second construction still stands.
