*Ishbīliya* (إشبيلية) is the Arabic name of Seville, formed at the conquest from the late Romance pronunciation of *Hispalis*, which had by then worn down to something like *Spalis*; Arabic broke the initial cluster with a prosthetic vowel, heard the *p* it lacks as *b*, and dressed the ending in the feminine *-iya*. Under this name the city was among the greatest of al-Andalus, capital of the ʿAbbādid taifa of the poet-king al-Muʿtamid and then of the Almohad empire, whose minaret, the Giralda, still carries the skyline. The name remains the Arabic word for Seville.

The entry completes the page's westernmost demonstration of a chain the atlas usually traces eastward: Castilian *Sevilla* continues *Ishbīliya*, not *Hispalis*, the initial vowel and the *-illa* both betraying the Arabic intermediary, so the modern name is a Latin name that survives only in its Arabic pronunciation. The pattern pairs the city with Córdoba, and the two make the rule visible: in the valley where Rome's province was richest, every major city's modern name passed through Arabic on its way to Spanish. Seville's patron saint writes Latin, its tower is a minaret, and its name, said plainly, is five centuries of Arabic pronounced with a Castilian accent.
