*Turuṣka* is the Sanskrit name of the Turks, the steppe ethnonym fitted with an Indic suffix and declined like any Sanskrit noun. It names the Turkic powers that pressed on India's northwest from the seventh century onward, the Turk Shahi rulers of Kabul and Gandhara among them, and it runs through Kalhaṇa's twelfth-century chronicle of Kashmir, where *Turuṣka* kings appear both as ancient invaders and as contemporary neighbors; the referent is the Turkic world at large rather than the Orkhon qaghanate alone, and the prose of the chroniclers extends it, in time, to every Muslim power from the northwest.

The entry marks the southern limit of the name's spread and the furthest semantic drift in the family. In Kashmir's chronicle the old word gradually stops being ethnic and becomes civilizational, so that *Turuṣka* ends as Sanskrit's ordinary term for the Turco-Muslim world as such, the name of a steppe people repurposed as the name of a religion's carriers. Every language on this page stretched the name *Türk* somewhere; Sanskrit stretched it furthest, from a qaghanate on the Orkhon to a category of civilization, and in doing so wrote the first chapter of the long Indian habit of naming ages after the peoples who arrive through the passes.
