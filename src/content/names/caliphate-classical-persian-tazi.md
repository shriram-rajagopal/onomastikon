*Tāzī* (تازی) is the Classical Persian continuation of Middle Persian *Tāzīg*, with the final consonant worn away: the ordinary word for an Arab, and adjectivally for things Arabian, in the Persian literature of the medieval courts. Its most charged appearances are in the *Shāhnāma*, where Ferdowsi, composing the epic of pre-Islamic Iran under Islamic rule, names the seventh-century conquerors with the old Sasanian word; the laments put in the mouths of the last Persian commanders before the fall speak of the army of the *Tāzīān*. The word also settled into the vocabulary of fine things Arabian, above all *asb-i tāzī*, the Arabian horse, whence the *tazi* hound and horse of the eastern lands.

The entry's interest is in who is speaking. Persian was by the tenth century the second language of the caliphate's own civilization, written by Muslims for Muslim courts; yet its word for the Arabs remained the one coined when Persia was Zoroastrian and the Arabs were a border tribe. Ferdowsi could use it with the grief of the conquered, his patrons with the neutrality of habit, a horse-dealer with admiration, all in the same generation. Where Chinese *Dàshí* and Armenian *Tačik* are names used across a border, *Tāzī* was used inside the house, by the empire's own Persian-speaking subjects about their rulers' nation. The conquered kept their own name for the conquerors, and the conquerors' own courts came to speak it.
