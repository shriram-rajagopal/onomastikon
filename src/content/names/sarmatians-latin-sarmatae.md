The Latin *Sarmatae*, with the country *Sarmatia*, taken from the Greek and made a fixture of Roman geography and Roman frontier politics alike. Tacitus opens the *Germania* by bounding his subject with the mutual fear of Germans and Sarmatians, and later emperors took *Sarmaticus* among their victory titles after Danube campaigns against the Iazyges and Roxolani. The most intimate Roman witness is Ovid, exiled to Tomis on the Black Sea, who watched Sarmatian wagons cross the frozen Danube and claimed, by the end, to have learned to speak with his barbarian neighbors; the name that was cartography in Pliny is, in the *Tristia*, the daily weather of a poet's banishment.

Rome did more than record the name; it moved the people. After the Marcomannic wars Marcus Aurelius shipped thousands of Sarmatian cavalry to serve on distant frontiers, Britain among them, and late Roman registers list *Sarmatae gentiles* settled across Gaul and Italy, so that the Latin name ends attached to veterans' colonies far from any steppe. The word thus tracks the whole arc of Rome's way with nomads: an ethnographic rumor at the edge of the *Germania*, then a triumphal epithet, then a payroll entry. The steppe's name entered Latin as a threat and retired as a garrison.
