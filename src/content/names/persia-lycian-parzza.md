The Lycian name of the Persians, rendering the Iranian *Pārsa* with the Lycian letter *z*, an affricate, standing in for the foreign *s*. The Xanthos pillar writes it twice in the space of a dozen lines and spells it differently each time: *Parzza* in the clause "the Persians at Kaunos and the troops of the Spartans fought against the Athenians," and *parza* further down, where Tissaphernes and the Persians appear together in the narrative. Both occurrences sit in the historical prose of the north face, the dynast's account of the wars he fought under Persian authority.

On this page the name of Persia travels with the empire, from the Akkadian of Cyrus's Babylon to the Aramaic of the imperial chanceries, and the Lycian form shows the view from inside a satrapy: a client people writing the overlord's name in their own alphabet while fighting the overlord's wars against Athens. Lycia had no need to borrow the word from afar; the Persians were the power that appointed its governors and took its levies. That the same stone spells the name two ways, *parzza* and *parza*, is its own small testimony: the scribe was writing a foreign word by ear, and the master's name had not yet hardened into an orthography.
