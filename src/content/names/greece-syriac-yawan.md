*Yāwān* (ܝܘܢ) is the Syriac name of Greece, inherited with the biblical text: the Peshitta sets Yāwān among the sons of Japheth in Genesis 10:2 and the sons of Zion against the sons of *Yāwān* in Zechariah 9:13. In living usage the country name is rarer than its derivatives. The gentilic *Yawnāyā*, plural *Yawnāyē*, is the ordinary Syriac word for the Greeks, so that Daniel 8:21 reads "king of the *Yawnāyē*" and Acts 6:1 uses the same word for the Hellenists of Jerusalem; the adverb *yawnāʾīṯ*, "in Greek," names the language, as in John 19:20, where the inscription over the cross is written *ʿebrāʾīṯ*, *yawnāʾīṯ*, and *rhōmāʾīṯ*, in Hebrew, in Greek, and in Latin.

The form is the Late Antique continuation of the Northwest Semitic branch of the Ionian family, cognate with Hebrew *Yāwān* and Imperial Aramaic *Ywn* rather than a fresh borrowing, and it preserves the old uncontracted shape of the name with both syllables intact. What Syriac adds to the family is a habit of time-reckoning: Syriac scribes dated by the Seleucid era, "the years of the *Yawnāyē*," and went on counting the years of the Greeks in their colophons for more than a millennium after the last Seleucid king was gone. Beside this stands the usual Late Antique division of labor, *Yawnāyē* for the Greeks as a people and a language, *Rhōmāyē* for the empire ruled from Constantinople, the same split that Middle Persian and Arabic draw between *Yunān* and *Hrōm*, *al-Yūnān* and *al-Rūm*. A church that quarreled with the Greeks of its own day over creeds and councils still numbered its years by them, and signed the count with their name.
