*Meluḫḫa* is a Bronze Age name, the Sumerian and Akkadian word for the farthest land of the Gulf trade, in all likelihood the Indus, and it has its own page in this atlas. In the Sargonid period the Assyrian scribes brought it back for a different far country. Sargon II's rock inscription at Tang-i Var (706 BCE) names Shebitku, the Kushite pharaoh, as the king of Meluḫḫa who extradited the rebel of Ashdod; Esarhaddon's inscriptions use the name seven times in the context of the Egyptian campaigns, paired with Egypt; and the same scribes revived *Magan*, the old copper-land of the Gulf, as an archaic name for Egypt itself. The reading of these passages as Kush, or as the Kushite-ruled Nile valley more broadly, is the common one, but the referent is debated and the standard edition leaves the name unglossed, which is why the entry carries the disputed label rather than passing the identification off as settled.

The form is the one name on this page that was a name before Kush existed and names Kush by pretending it is somewhere else. Assyrian learning kept the geography of the third millennium alive as a literary register, and when its kings reached the edge of the known world on the Nile they reached for the words their ancestors had used for the edge of the known world on the sea. The Indus name on the Nile is scribal antiquarianism, and it is also a measure: Kush was, to the Assyrians, as far as a place could be, and the only word they had for that far was two thousand years old.
