The Hebrew *Bāvel*, Babylon, as medieval Jewry's name for the world whose capital was Baghdad. When the ancient academies of Sura and Pumbedita moved into the new Abbasid metropolis in the ninth and tenth centuries, they carried their address with them: their heads remained the geonim of the yeshivot of *Bāvel*, the exilarch in Baghdad ruled the captivity of *Bāvel*, and the legal correspondence that bound the diaspora to Baghdad ran under Babylon's name for centuries after the city of Nebuchadnezzar lay in mounds a day's ride to the south. Benjamin of Tudela's twelfth-century itinerary still moves through this doubled landscape, the living caliphal city and, nearby, the ruins his tradition knew from scripture.

The entry is the mirror of the page's Middle Persian *Baγdād*, and the two make the neatest inversion in the atlas. The Pahlavi tradition recorded the new city under its own new name in a dying language; the Hebrew tradition refused the new name almost entirely and folded the city into the oldest address it had, so that the caliphs' City of Peace appears in the responsa as Babylon, of all names, the exile's name. For the scattered communities writing to the geonim, the point was precisely the continuity: questions had gone to *Bāvel* since the Talmud was made there, and *Bāvel* is where answers still came from, whatever the caliphs called the place. Rarely has a name so old been made to do such practical, current work: a postal address fifteen centuries deep.
