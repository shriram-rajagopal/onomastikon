*Kūš* in the Babylonian Talmud is the biblical name carried into the academies of Sura and Pumbedita, and its most memorable appearance is a dispute about the shape of the world. Esther 1:1 says that Ahasuerus reigned "from Hoddu to Kush," from India to Kush, and Megillah 11a records Rav and Shmuel disagreeing about what the verse means: one holds that Hoddu is at one end of the world and Kush at the other, so that the king ruled everything between; the other that Hoddu and Kush are adjacent, and that the verse means he ruled the hundred and twenty-seven provinces as easily as he ruled two neighboring ones. The name is spelled as in the Hebrew, without points in the manuscripts, and read with the same long *u*.

The rabbinic Kush has no king and no capital. By the Bavli's centuries the Meroitic kingdom was dying or dead, and the Talmud does not notice; its Kush is a cosmographic term, a boundary of the inhabited earth, and whether it touches India is a question about geography, not about Nubia. The entry pairs with the Talmud's *Yāwān* on the Greece page, a name frozen at the moment of the Maccabees; *Kūš* is frozen at the moment of Esther, a compass point in a Persian king's titulary. In Babylonia the name of the Nile kingdom had become a direction.
