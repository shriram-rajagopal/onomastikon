The Avestan name of the Hindu Kush, *Upāiri.saēna*, appearing in the Zamyād Yašt's catalogue of the world's eminent mountains, whose summits stand closest to the heavens, and again in the Hōm Yašt, both times paired with Mount Iškata in a dual compound, *iškata upāiri.saēna*. The name is conventionally interpreted as "higher than the eagle," the range so tall that even the highest-flying bird passes below its peaks, and it continued in Zoroastrian tradition as the Middle Persian *Abarsēn*, the great mountain wall of the Iranian world's eastern books.

This is the headwater of the page's *paropamisos* family: the Greek *Paropámisos* that Alexander's geographers carried west reflects the Old Persian shape of this same Iranian name, garbled in transmission until its eagle was no longer visible inside it. The page thus runs the full arc of the range's naming: an Avestan hymn measures the mountains against a bird's flight, Greek administration flattens the phrase into a label, and the eventual winner, *Hindu Kush*, names the range from the opposite direction entirely, for what lies beyond it rather than what flies beneath it. Of all the ways to say a mountain is high, the oldest name on this page chose the most vivid: too high for eagles.
