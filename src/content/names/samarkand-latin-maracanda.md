The Latin name of Samarkand, *Maracanda*, taken from the Greek of the Alexander historians: Curtius Rufus, writing the fullest surviving Latin account of the conquest, places *Maracanda*, the capital of Sogdiana, at the center of the campaign's darkest stretch, the guerrilla war against Spitamenes and the drunken feast at which Alexander killed his friend Cleitus. The name reached Latin as a book-word, carried by historiography rather than by any Roman who ever went there, and it never advanced past the Alexander narrative into the working geography of the empire.

That bounded life is the entry's point on a page whose other names all keep moving. The Sogdian *Smarkand* fed the Persian and Arabic forms and ultimately the modern name; the Chinese *Kāngguó* filed the city into a Tang administrative system; Latin alone took the name, used it for one story, and set it down. Rome knew Samarkand only as the place where Alexander's expedition began to curdle, a stage direction in a cautionary tale about conquest and drink; for Latin readers across the empire's whole history, that was the city's entire existence. Some names are roads and some are rooms; *Maracanda* is a room in one book, furnished once, never entered again.
