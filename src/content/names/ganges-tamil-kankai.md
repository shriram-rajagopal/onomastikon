The Tamil name of the Ganges, *Kaṅkai*, the Sanskrit *Gaṅgā* passed through Tamil's phonology: the language admits no voiced stop at the start of a word, so *g-* hardens to *k-*, and the final long *-ā* is reshaped to the Tamil feminine ending *-ai*. The river stands at its most dramatic in the Cilappatikāram, the earliest Tamil epic, whose final movement sends the Chera king Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ marching the length of India so that the stone for the slain heroine Kaṇṇaki's image can be cut in the Himalaya and bathed in the Ganges before it comes south; in later medieval Tamil the Chola kings made the same journey a boast, Rajendra I taking the title of the king who fetched the Ganges.

On this page the river's name mostly travels west, to Greek and Arabic and Latin ears that knew it as a rumor at the world's edge. The Tamil entry is the opposite case: a name that traveled south inside India, to a people for whom the river was no less distant, three thousand kilometers of someone else's country away, but who made it the fixed pole of sanctity all the same. The epic's kings do not trade with the Ganges or map it; they walk armies to it so that its water can consecrate what matters at home. For the Greeks the Ganges measured how far the world went; for the Tamils it measured how far a king would go.
