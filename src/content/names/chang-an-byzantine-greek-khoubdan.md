*Khoubdán* is the Byzantine Greek name of Chang'an, preserved in Theophylact Simocatta's excursus on Taugast, his name for north China, written in Constantinople in the earlier seventh century from reports that had crossed the whole of Asia. The excursus describes two great cities: Taugast itself, whose ruler is titled "son of God," and, a few miles away, Khoubdan, divided by two great rivers with banks of cypresses, which the account improbably credits to Alexander; the details are garbled in transit, but the name is recognizably the road's *Khumdān*, and the identification with Chang'an is standard.

The entry closes the longest transmission line on any page of this atlas: a city in the Wei valley, named in Iranian by the Sogdian merchants who worked it, the name relayed caravan by caravan across Central Asia and the steppe until a Greek historian on the Bosphorus wrote it down, six thousand kilometers and at least four languages from home. Greek, which named so much of the world in this atlas from autopsy or conquest, here records a name it received at maximum distance, the far terminus of the road heard faintly at the near one.
