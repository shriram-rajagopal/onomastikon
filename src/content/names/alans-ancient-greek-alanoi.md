The Greek *Alanoí*, first substantially attested in Josephus, who describes the great raid of the 70s CE through the Caspian Gates into Media and Armenia, kings fleeing before nomads who roped their prey from horseback with lassos. The name then enters the working vocabulary of empire: Ptolemy maps the *Alanoí* among the peoples of Sarmatia, and Arrian, governing Cappadocia for Hadrian, wrote the *Order of Battle against the Alans*, the one surviving Roman staff document composed against a named steppe enemy, when the raiders came south again in 135. Byzantine writers carried the name on for the Caucasian kingdom, but its classical attestations belong to these first alarmed and precise centuries.

Greek gives the family its earliest firm dates, and something rarer, the name embedded in the paperwork of response: not ethnography at leisure but a raid remembered by a survivor-historian and a battle plan drafted by a sitting governor. Between Josephus's lassos and Arrian's deployment orders, the *Alanoí* enter literature already as a military problem stated with professional respect. On a page where most names begin as rumors at the world's edge, this one begins as an operational term, which is its own kind of tribute from one cavalry tradition to another.
