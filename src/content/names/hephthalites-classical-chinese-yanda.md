*Yàndā* 嚈噠 is the Chinese name of the Hephthalites, carried by the northern and southern dynastic histories alike; the *Weishu* traces the embassies that crossed the Pamirs to the Northern Wei court, and the *Liangshu* preserves, in its gallery of tribute-bearing envoys, a portrait of a Hephthalite ambassador, the only surviving image of one drawn from life outside their own coins. The transcription renders the same dynastic name that Greek wrote *Ephthalîtai*, heard along the mountain roads rather than through Persian intelligence, and the histories add the details their sources cared about: a state without cities in one account, with walled towns in another, the contradiction of a people every neighbor found hard to classify. Chinese IPA is omitted per this atlas's policy for reconstructed ancient readings.

The entry closes the pincer of testimony around a people with no surviving books. Byzantium described the Hephthalites from the west through enemy report, China from the east through embassy protocol, and the two descriptions, written within a century of each other by courts that knew nothing of one another, agree on the name and disagree about nearly everything else. The atlas has few better demonstrations that a name is the most portable fact about a people: institutions, habits, even cities may blur in transmission, but the four syllables of the royal clan crossed both the Pamirs and the Persian frontier intact.
