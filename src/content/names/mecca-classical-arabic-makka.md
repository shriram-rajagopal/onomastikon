*Makka* (مكة) enters the durable written record in the Qur'an itself, which names the city once in this form, "in the valley of Makka," and once in another: "the first House established for mankind is the one at *Bakka*." The doublet is ancient and unresolved; the commentators offered every solution from a dialectal b/m alternation to a distinction between the sanctuary (Bakka) and the town around it (Makka), and no explanation has won. The name's etymology is equally open. Proposals have reached from Arabic roots to the Old South Arabian *mkrb*, "sanctuary," but the honest position, held by the standard references, is that the origin of *Makka* is unknown.

For the city at the center of a world religion, this is a remarkably undocumented name, and the entry's significance is precisely that opacity. Elsewhere on the atlas the great sanctuaries carry transparent names, Jerusalem's "foundation of peace," Babylon's "gate of the god"; Mecca's name explains nothing and descends from nothing traceable, as if the city had no history before the revelation that made it the axis of prayer. Even its earliest secure attestations are scripture rather than stone: no inscription, no archive, only the recited text, which then preserved both variants of the name forever because the text could not be altered. A name of unknown meaning, fixed eternally in two spellings by the one book that could fix anything; the city that orients a faith cannot itself be traced past the faith's first page.
