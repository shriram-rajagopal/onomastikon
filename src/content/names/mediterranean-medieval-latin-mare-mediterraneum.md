The medieval career of *Mare Mediterraneum*, the name the Latin entry above traces to late antiquity. Its fortune was made by Isidore of Seville, whose *Etymologiae* defined it for the Middle Ages: this sea "is also the Mediterranean, because it pours through the middle of the land all the way to the east, dividing Europe, Africa, and Asia." Isidore's book became the standard reference of medieval learning, and his sentence made the adjective into the sea's proper name; the mappae mundi drew the world as his three continents parted by this central water, and the name runs through medieval geography to the end of the period and beyond, into every modern European language.

The classical *Mare Nostrum* on this page died with the power that could say "our"; *Mediterraneum* asserted no ownership, only position, and position could not be conquered. But a neutral name still needed an authority to carry it, and Isidore was the funnel: one late-antique encyclopedia, copied in every monastic library, fixed which of the sea's Latin names the next thousand years would use. Empires lose their possessions, and *Nostrum* went with Rome's; the sea in the middle of the lands is in the middle still.
