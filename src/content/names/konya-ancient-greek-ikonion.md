*Ikónion* (Ἰκόνιον) is the Greek name of Konya, attested from Xenophon, whose Ten Thousand passed through it in 401 BCE as "the last city of Phrygia" on Cyrus's march east. The name is pre-Greek and unexplained; Greek antiquity, hearing *eikṓn*, "image," in it, supplied a legend that Perseus had set up the Gorgon's image there, one more folk etymology doing duty for a lost original. The city's most consequential ancient appearances are in the Acts of the Apostles, which brings Paul and Barnabas to Iconium's synagogue and its divided crowds; through Acts the name entered every Christian literature, and the Greek form remained in Byzantine use until the city's Turkish centuries and beyond.

The entry's long window is its point: a thousand years separate Xenophon's notice from the last Byzantine campaigns near the city, and the name did not move. It anchors a family whose whole story is phonetic drift without replacement, Latin *Iconium*, Arabic *Qūniya*, Turkish *Konya*, each a light re-hearing of this form; no conqueror of the plateau ever imposed a new name, which for a city passed among Phrygians, Persians, Greeks, Romans, Arabs, and Turks approaches a record. The unexplained word Xenophon wrote down marching east is, allowing for accent, the word on the road signs today.
