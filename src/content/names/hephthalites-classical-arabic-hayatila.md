The Arabic *al-Hayāṭila*, a broken plural built on the same dynastic name the Greeks wrote *Ephthalîtai*, and a name the Arabs acquired entirely secondhand: the Hephthalite empire was sixty years gone when the first Arab armies reached Khurasan. It lives in the historians' retrospect, above all in al-Ṭabarī's long Sasanian chapters, drawn from Persian court tradition, where the *Hayāṭila* kill Pērōz, shelter and restore the fugitive Kavād, and stand as the standing eastern complication of the old empire's politics; the geographers still knew districts of Ṭukhāristān as former Hephthalite country, and remnant *Hayāṭila* principalities figure in the conquest narratives of Balkh and Badghis.

The entry documents a name already historical at the moment of borrowing, Arabic's word not for a neighbor but for a chapter of the empire it had swallowed. In the family's transmission chain it is the proof of how durably the Persian court tradition curated its own humiliations: the story of the king killed by the *Hayāṭila* passed from Sasanian chronicle into Arabic annal and Persian epic, each retelling keeping the enemy's name alive centuries after the enemy. The Hephthalites survived longest not on their own coins but inside the historiography of their victims.
