*Hadrianoúpolis* (Ἁδριανούπολις) is the Greek name of Edirne, given when the emperor Hadrian refounded the Thracian town, earlier called Uskudama in the thin older record, around 125 CE. It is one of a string of Hadrianopolises the traveling emperor left across the provinces, but the Thracian one absorbed the name's whole fortune: commanding the military road from the Danube to Constantinople, it became the fortress the capital's fate repeatedly turned on, and Byzantine historians from Ammianus's Greek successors to Anna Komnene and beyond return to it constantly, under the shortened spoken form Adrianople that the written tradition also admits. The city stayed Greek-named to the Ottoman capture and the Greek form continued in use after it.

Its position in the family is the fountainhead of a name that spent thirteen centuries slowly shedding its emperor, and the entry's substance is what the name witnessed while eroding. Under this name the eastern field army and the emperor Valens burned in 378, the disaster Ammianus ranked with Cannae; under it the first Crusaders quarreled, the Latins of 1205 lost their emperor to the Bulgarians, and the Ottomans in 1369 acquired their European capital. Hadrian founded cities to spread his name; this one spread it chiefly across casualty lists. The most peaceful of emperors, the one who fixed the empire's walls, is remembered on the map by the battlefield where walls availed nothing.
