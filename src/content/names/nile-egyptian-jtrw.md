The Middle Egyptian word for the Nile, *jtrw*, written with the water-ripple determinative: the common noun "river," which in a country with one river was all the name the river ever needed. The word saturates the classical language, in the geography of the nomes, the measurement of the land (an *jtrw* was also the standard itinerary measure of about ten and a half kilometers, the river supplying even the unit of distance), and the literature that moves up and down it; where the page's *Ḥꜥpy* names the inundation as a god, *jtrw* names the water as a fact. The reconstructed vocalization *\*jatraw* is anchored at both ends of the word's later history, by the Hebrew *Yeʾōr* borrowed from its New Kingdom pronunciation and by the Coptic *Eioor* it finally became.

This entry is the headwater of the *jtrw* family, and until now the family stood without it, a Hebrew loan and a Coptic descendant holding the surface while the source ran underneath. Restored, the arc shows a single word passing through every stage of its own language, hieroglyphic *jtrw*, Demotic *yr*, Coptic *Eioor*, while a neighbor's scripture carried a snapshot of its middle age abroad as a proper name. Egypt never capitalized the word; everyone else did. The atlas's longest-serving common noun did three thousand years of work as a name without ever being promoted to one.
