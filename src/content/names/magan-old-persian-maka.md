The Old Persian *Maka*, a province of the Achaemenid Empire, listed at Behistun among the countries that came to Darius and again in the satrapy rolls of Persepolis and Naqsh-e Rustam, where the *Maciyā*, the men of Maka, also stand carved among the throne-bearing peoples of the empire. The Persepolis Fortification tablets, in the Elamite of the imperial chancellery, know the land as *Makkaš* and record a satrap and rations for travelers between it and the heartland. The province lay along the southern coasts, and its identification is the dispute this entry's label declares: many scholars place Maka on the Oman peninsula and read the name as old *Magan* resurfacing, while others set it in Gedrosia, the Makran coast of the Iranian side, whose modern name may itself continue *Maka*. The Babylonian version of the royal lists gives the land a wholly different name, *Qadê*, the term the Assyrians had used for an Omani kingdom with its seat at Izki, which shows that the Achaemenid chancellery did not connect Maka with the cuneiform tradition's *Makkan*.

The entry earns its place on this page as the most consequential maybe in the Gulf's onomastic history. If *Maka* is *Magan*, then a Bronze Age trade-name survived a thousand years of silence in local mouths, unheard by any scribe, to re-enter writing from below when a new empire needed a label for the old copper coast, and both shores of the strait have carried it since, Makran on one side and, more remotely, perhaps even Oman's medieval nisba on the other. If it is not, the resemblance of the names is a coastal coincidence, and Magan's thread truly ended with the Assyrian archaists. The evidence does not settle the question, and the honest reading of this entry is that a single syllable's worth of similarity is bearing a very great deal of historical weight.
