The Persian name, *Lūlī*, with its older doublet *Lūrī*, the word classical Persian used for the itinerant musician peoples of Iran, and the name around which Persian literature preserved an origin story: Ferdowsi tells how Bahrām Gūr, wanting music for his poor, asked the king of India for ten thousand *Lūrī* minstrels, settled them with oxen and seed-corn, and watched them eat the oxen and take to the roads forever, wanderers by royal appointment. By Ḥāfiẓ the *lūlī* is a stock figure of the ghazal, beautiful, disreputable, and free. The dispute the label records is the identification: tradition and much scholarship see in the legend a memory of the Indian migrations that produced the Romani and their Middle Eastern kin, but the story is legend, the word's referent in any given text is simply "wandering musician," and no Persian source knowingly names the Romani as such.

What the entry preserves is unlike anything else on the page. Byzantium and the West named the Romani by misclassification, from the outside, on arrival; Persia, which the migration crossed centuries earlier, kept instead an explanation, a story about who the wanderers were and why they wander, dressed as an episode of the national epic. The legend even gets the direction right, out of India westward, which is more than the "Egyptians" of the Latin record managed. On a page of names that are all mistakes of one kind or another, the Persian entry is the mistake that contains a map: wrong as history, it remembers the road.
