The Latin name of the Seljuks in the crusade chronicles, *Turci*, the plain ethnonym Turk carried into Latin through the Byzantine *Toúrkoi*. The *Gesta Francorum*, the anonymous eyewitness narrative of the First Crusade, uses it on nearly every page: the *Turci* who ambush the columns at Dorylaeum, defend Nicaea and Antioch, and rule the Anatolian interior the crusaders had to cross; Fulcher of Chartres and the rest of the chronicle tradition write the same. The Latin West knew essentially nothing of the dynasty; it named the whole Seljuk order by the ethnic name of its soldiers, and by the thirteenth century Latin usage had made the land itself *Turchia*.

The page's other names all came from closer up and lasted less. Byzantine Greek, archaizing as always, called the newcomers *Persai*, dressing the emergency in a classical costume that died with Byzantium; Arabic and Persian named the dynasty, *al-Saljūq*, *Saljūqiyān*, names that fell with the house that bore them. The crusaders, farthest away and worst informed, reached for the crudest label available, the bare name of a people, and it is the one name on this page still on the map: their *Turci* in their *Turchia* became the Turks in Turkey. Ignorance, wanting nuance, sometimes picks the name that history keeps.
