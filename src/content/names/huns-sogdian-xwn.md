*xwn* occurs in the second of the Sogdian Ancient Letters, a merchant's report abandoned in a Dunhuang watchtower around 313 CE, describing catastrophe in China: famine in the capitals, the emperor fled, and Luoyang sacked by the *xwn*. The events are known from the Chinese side, and the people who sacked Luoyang in 311 were the Southern Xiongnu; the Sogdian writer, that is, calls the Xiongnu by a name that looks exactly like "Hun," six decades before any Hun appears on Rome's horizon. The disputed label belongs not to the reading, which is secure, but to what the word proves, since it is the single strongest piece of evidence in the old question of whether the Huns of Europe descend from the Xiongnu of the Chinese frontier. The rendering uses the Old Sogdian letters this atlas employs for the language.

This is the linchpin entry of the whole page, three consonants doing more work than any monument in the family. If *xwn* is the Xiongnu's name in Iranian mouths, then the same name traveled the length of Eurasia in a century, and the peoples at its two ends were joined at least by a label and perhaps by blood; if it is coincidence, the entire Hun-Xiongnu edifice rests on air. The atlas records the Xiongnu on their own page, under their Chinese names, and leaves the bridge between the pages exactly as wide as this word: one merchant's noun, written in a moment of panic, on which fifteen centuries of argument have balanced.
