*Marw* (مرو) is the Arabic form of the name, taken directly from the Middle Persian at the conquest of the 650s; the geographers distinguish the metropolis as *Marw al-Shāhijān*, royal Marw, from its smaller neighbor Marw al-Rūdh up the river. Under the caliphate the city reached its height: seat of the governors of Khurasan, launching point of the Abbasid revolution, residence of the caliph al-Maʾmūn, home of observatories and of the libraries that Yāqūt himself used, writing his gazetteer's entry from memory of working in them just before the catastrophe. The Mongols destroyed the city in 1221 with a massacre the sources count in the hundreds of thousands; Yāqūt's entry, finished after, reads as an obituary.

The Arabic stage is the name's peak and cliff in one entry. For five centuries *Marw* meant one of the half-dozen greatest cities of Islam, and its adjective *Marwazī* trails through the biographical dictionaries behind hundreds of scholars; after 1221 the name went on denoting a diminishing town beside an enormous ruin, and the geographers' tense shifts from present to past. Few entries on this atlas contain their own before and after so exactly; the librarian who wrote the city's fullest description was also the last man to borrow its books. The name survived the city by centuries, which on this atlas is common; what is rare is having the witness on file.
