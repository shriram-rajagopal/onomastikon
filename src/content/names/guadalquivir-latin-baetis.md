*Baetis* is the Latin name of the great river of southern Iberia, standard from the late Republic onward; Pliny's survey follows its course past Corduba and Hispalis to the ocean. The name is pre-Roman and unexplained, at home among the unetymologized Iberian names of the region, and Rome paid it the highest compliment in its administrative vocabulary: the province was named Baetica, for the river, the whole richest quarter of Hispania filed under the water that drained it.

The entry marks the ancient half of a name-history with a clean break in the middle, rare on an atlas full of continuous chains. *Baetis* had everything a name needs to survive, a province named for it, six centuries of literature, poets from its own banks; and it did not survive, because the next language to hold the valley described the river instead of naming it, and the description stuck. Only the province's ghost remained: Baetica's territory, under its newer name al-Andalus, became Andalucía, so the river that lost its own name kept, at one remove, the naming of the land. Strabo preserves that the oldest Greeks had called the river Tartessos; Baetis was thus already the name that replaced a legend, before becoming the name a sentence replaced.
