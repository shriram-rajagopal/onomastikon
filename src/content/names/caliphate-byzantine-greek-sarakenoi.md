The Byzantine name for the Arabs and the Islamic power, *Sarakēnoí*, the Greek original behind the Latin West's *Sarraceni*. Like the Latin, it is older than Islam, a Ptolemaic name for Arabian nomads promoted to cover an empire; from the conquests of the 630s it is the standing term of Byzantine chronicle and polemic, running through Theophanes and his successors for the whole eight centuries in which the empire and the caliphates shared a frontier. John of Damascus, writing inside the caliphate itself as a Christian official of Umayyad Damascus, gave the medieval world its folk etymology: *Sarakēnoí* as if *Sárras kenoí*, "empty of Sarah," the descendants of Hagar the sent-away, whence the parallel names *Agarēnoí* and *Ismaēlîtai*.

The page's Persian and Latin entries stretch old tribal names over the new civilization from outside; this entry does the same from the border itself, and adds the theology. The "empty of Sarah" etymology is philologically worthless and historically priceless: it shows Byzantium, like the Latin West after it, able to place the caliphate only inside the biblical family tree, as the other line of Abraham's house. The empire that fought the caliphate longest also transmitted its name farthest, west into *Sarraceni* and onward into every medieval European tongue; Christendom's whole vocabulary for Islam descends from a Greek pun about a slighted matriarch. The neighbors were never strangers; that was the problem the name kept solving.
