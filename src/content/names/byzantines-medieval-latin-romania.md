The medieval Latin continuation of *Romania*, the late-antique name for the Roman world that the Latin entry above traces to Orosius and Jordanes. Through the Middle Ages the name stayed attached to the territory of the surviving eastern empire, in papal correspondence and above all in Italian usage, and it reached its strangest official moment in 1204: the treaty by which the Fourth Crusade's victors carved up the Byzantine state is the *Partitio terrarum imperii Romanie*, "the division of the lands of the empire of Romania," and the crusader state planted at Constantinople called itself the empire of *Romania*, the entity historians now call the Latin Empire.

The usage preserves a precise medieval contradiction. The Latin West would not concede that the Byzantines were Romans, and called them *Graeci*, their ruler at best *rex Graecorum*; yet the West never stopped calling their country *Romania*, because the land's name had been fixed before the quarrel began. People and country thus carried names from two different arguments, Greeks living in Roman-land, and in 1204 the men who extinguished the empire adopted its name for their own creation the moment it was theirs. The name outlived both empires on the map, and in Italy it still marks the coast the Byzantines held longest: the Romagna, Roman-land seen from Lombard Italy. Rome's name proved most durable exactly where Rome's power had become a memory, kept alive by neighbors, conquerors, and heirs who agreed on nothing else.
