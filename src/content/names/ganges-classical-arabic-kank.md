Al-Bīrūnī's Arabic transcription of Sanskrit *Gaṅgā*, written *nahr Kank*, "the river Kank," throughout his *Taḥqīq mā li-l-Hind* of c. 1030, the systematic study of India he composed at Ghazni from Sanskrit sources and Indian informants. Arabic has no letter for *g*, so the velar *kāf* stands in for both stops of the Sanskrit name. He records not only the river's course but the beliefs attached to it, reporting that in Indian tradition the Ganges once flowed through the land of paradise, a doctrine he presents with the same care he gives its astronomy.

The entry is distinctive on this page for its directness. The Greek and Latin forms above reached the Mediterranean through Persian intermediaries and returning campaign literature; the Chinese form traveled with pilgrims; *Kank* was taken down from Sanskrit at first hand by a single scholar who had learned the language for the purpose. It is also a record of method: al-Bīrūnī transcribes the sacred river of a religion not his own without polemic and without conversion of the name into something more familiar. One man with a working knowledge of Sanskrit moved the name straight from one classical language to another, no empire required.
