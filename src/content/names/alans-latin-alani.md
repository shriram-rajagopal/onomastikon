The Latin *Alani*, in the language from the first century, when Lucan could already use the name for the warlike riders beyond the Caucasus, and given its full portrait by Ammianus in the same excursus that introduced the Huns: tall, fair, terrifyingly mobile, worshippers of a sword fixed in the earth, a people holding the immense steppe from the Danube toward the Ganges in the geographer's exaggeration, and, he notes, in almost all things like the Huns but gentler in life and manners. After 406 the name enters the annals of the West's dismemberment, Alans crossing the frozen Rhine with Vandals and Sueves, settling in Gaul and Iberia, riding to Carthage under a king whose Vandal successors kept *rex Wandalorum et Alanorum* in their royal style for a century.

The entry's distinction on this page is that Latin alone records the name at both ends of its career, as the far rumor of Nero's generals and as a licensed people inside the falling empire's own provinces. No other steppe nation of this atlas got so deep into the Roman world while keeping its name: Gaulish place-names and the medieval fashion for the personal name Alain have both been traced, plausibly if not provably, to the settlements of the fifth century. The Sarmatians gave Rome a frontier; the Alans, in the end, gave it inhabitants.
