The medieval Latin name of northern China, *Cataya*, with the variants *Cataia* and *Cathaia*, from the ethnonym of the Khitan, the steppe people whose Liao dynasty had ruled northern China and whose name spread west across Asia in Turkic and Mongol mouths as *Kitai*. William of Rubruck brought the name into Latin in the report of his journey to the Mongol court of 1253 to 1255, and it runs through the whole later travel literature, Odoric of Pordenone's *Relatio* and the Latin versions of Marco Polo, always for the north of the country, beside *Mangi* or *Manzi* for the south. Rubruck, seeing the silks that reached the Mongol camps from *Cataya*, judged that these people must be the Seres of the ancients, the silk-people the classical geographers had named without ever meeting.

The name reached Europe already obsolete. The Khitan state had fallen in 1125, more than a century before Rubruck wrote, and the Chinese themselves never called their country by the Khitan's name; Europe learned China's name from the steppe peoples in between, who had fixed the label of a fallen dynasty onto everything beyond it. On this page *Cataya* is the second time the West named China at second hand and after the fact, for the Latin *Seres* before it had named the silk-people from the silk alone. Rubruck's guess joined the two: the friar on the steppe recognized, in the silks of *Cataya*, the *Seres* of the schoolbooks, and for a moment the medieval and the ancient names of China, both wrong in the same secondhand way, met and confirmed each other. The name still lives where the steppe passed it on, in Russian *Kitay*; Europe let it go, but *Cathay* kept its shimmer of distance, the name of China as heard across the whole width of Asia.
