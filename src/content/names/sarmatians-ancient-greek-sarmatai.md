*Sarmátai* is the standard later Greek name of the steppe peoples west of the Don, current from the Hellenistic period when the westward-moving tribes entered treaty relations with the Pontic kingdoms; Polybius records a Sarmatian king among the signatories of a treaty of 179 BCE, and by Strabo and Ptolemy the geographers had divided a *Sarmatía* of Europe from a *Sarmatía* of Asia at the Don, a partition of the whole northern world under this one name. The relation of the form to the older *Sauromátai* beside it, one name worn smooth or two names merged, is the standing problem the previous entry states.

What this entry adds is the name's career as a container. As tribe after tribe crossed into the Greek field of view, Iazyges, Roxolani, Aorsi, Alans, the geographers filed each as a kind of *Sarmátai*, until the word named less a people than a zone; Ptolemy's two Sarmatias hold dozens of peoples who never called themselves any such thing. It is the atlas's clearest Greek case of a name promoted from ethnography to cartography, and the promotion gave it a long afterlife: when Renaissance mapmakers needed a classical name for the lands of Poland and Russia, they reached past a millennium to Ptolemy's *Sarmatía*, and Polish nobles, in one of history's stranger acts of onomastic adoption, claimed the steppe nomads as ancestors on the strength of the map alone.
