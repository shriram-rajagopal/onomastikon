The Classical Armenian *Hon*, plural *Honkʿ*, the standing name for the Huns in the fifth-century historians and their successors. Armenia met Huns not across a distant frontier but through the Caucasus passes at its shoulder: Pʿawstos already sets Hun contingents in the wars of fourth-century kings, and the historians of the following centuries know the *Honkʿ* of the north, the Caucasian Hunnic peoples of the Derbent corridor, as raiders, mercenaries, and eventually as a mission field, for it was to these Huns that Armenian churchmen carried Christianity, and an Armenian bishop's seventh-century report from their country north of Derbent is among the closest surviving looks at any Hunnic society.

The Armenian form is the family's longest-serving working name. Rome's *Hunni* outlived its referent as rhetoric; the Armenian *Honkʿ* stayed attached to actual neighbors for centuries after Attila's empire dissolved, because the Caucasian Huns remained a fact of Armenian life long after the European ones became a memory. On a page dominated by names that hardened into symbols, this entry is the ethnonym as a tool kept in daily use, worn smooth by proximity: for the historians of Armenia the Huns were never the end of the world, merely the other side of the mountains.
