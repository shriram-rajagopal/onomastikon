The second river of Eden, *Gîḥôn*, "the Gusher," which Genesis sends winding "through the whole land of Kush," and which ancient readers therefore took for the Nile, the one great river of the Kushites' country. The identification is not a modern guess but an ancient chain: the Septuagint's translators put *Gēōn* into Jeremiah where the Hebrew names Egypt's river, Ben Sira set the Gihon among the great rivers of wisdom, and Josephus states it flatly, "Geon, which runs through Egypt, is the one the Greeks call the Nile." The dispute the label records is between these ancient readers and modern ones, for whom a river of Eden rising with the Tigris and Euphrates fits no Nile at all, and whose candidates run from Arabian watercourses to no earthly river whatsoever.

The entry is the exact mirror of the *Pîšôn* on the Ganges page, its companion stream in Genesis, and together with the Arabic pair elsewhere in the atlas, *Jayḥūn* for the Oxus and *Sayḥūn* for the Jaxartes, it closes the net that the four rivers of Eden cast over the real map. Every civilization that inherited the verse spent its geography trying to land paradise's rivers on actual water, and each landed them on the greatest rivers it knew. The Hebrew page of the Nile thus carries a name the river never heard on its own banks: not what Egypt called it, nor what its neighbors called it, but where scripture's readers decided paradise had drained to. The Nile's own entry above means simply "the river"; *Gîḥôn* means the river was once in Eden.
