The Hebrew *Ṣīn*, China, as the traveler Benjamin of Tudela set it down around 1170 in the *Sefer Masaʿot*, the record of his journey from Navarre to Baghdad and the Persian Gulf and home by way of Egypt. His account of the Indian Ocean ends past the pepper port of Khulam, the modern Quilon, with a report rather than a visit: to cross over to the land of *Ṣīn* is a voyage of forty days, and *Ṣīn* lies in the uttermost East, beyond a Sea of Nikpa where the storms of Orion hold ships fast and sailors escape sewn into ox-hides, carried to shore by griffins. The name he was given is the Arabic *al-Ṣīn* of the geographers and the sea-merchants, and the Hebrew spelling says so: the emphatic *ṣade* answers Arabic *ṣād* letter for letter, as it does everywhere in the Judeo-Arabic world he traveled. By the usual reckoning he is the first European writer to name China at all, a century before Marco Polo.

This page already holds a Hebrew China that almost certainly is not one, Isaiah's *Sînîm*, spelled with *samekh*, the disputed ghost that readers have wanted to be China since antiquity. *Ṣīn* is the real thing, and its letters show how it came: not through scripture but along the Arabic sea-road, carrying the emphatic consonant that the Arabic entry calls its signature, the trace of the Iranian route by which *Čīn* reached Arabic. Where *Sînîm* was found in the Bible by people looking for China, *Ṣīn* was found in China's direction by a man who got as far as the pepper coast and wrote down what the sailors told him. Hebrew's first true name for China is a loan three times over, *Qín* made Persian, made Arabic, made Hebrew, and the ghost and the name differ by a single consonant; the consonant is the whole road.
