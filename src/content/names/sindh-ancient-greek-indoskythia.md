The Greek name of the lower Indus country in the Roman period, *Indoskythía*, "Indian Scythia." The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, the merchant's handbook of the first century, calls the region at the Indus mouths simply *Skythía*, with its port of Barbarikon serving a royal city upriver; Ptolemy, a century later, fixes the compound *Indoskythía* on his map for the whole stretch of the river's lower course. The name records a political fact: the Saka, the Iranian steppe people the Greeks called Scythians, had come down through the passes in the last centuries BCE and made the lower Indus their kingdom, and the geographers renamed the province for its new masters.

The page's other names all descend from the river, *Sindhu* and its Persian and Arabic children; this one ignores the river entirely and names the land for a people who had arrived within living memory, the atlas's steppe pattern breaking through on its southernmost shore. It is also a name with a doubled horizon: *Scythia* proper lay north of the Black Sea, at the opposite corner of the Greek world, and the compound quietly asserts that the same steppe that touched the Danube had now touched the Indian Ocean. For the sailors of the *Periplus*, Scythia was no longer the frozen north of Herodotus; it was a monsoon port at the mouth of the Indus, the steppe met by sea.
