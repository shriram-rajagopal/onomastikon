*Madīnat al-Salām* (مدينة السلام), "the City of Peace," is the official name al-Manṣūr gave his round city at its foundation in 762. The choice was a manifesto: it echoes the Qur'an's *dār al-salām*, the "abode of peace" that is paradise, and cast the perfectly circular capital, with the caliph's palace and mosque at its center, as a kind of paradise realized in brick on the Tigris. The name was no dead letter; it held the official registers for centuries, and Abbasid dirhams and dinars name their mint as Madīnat al-Salām down through the dynasty's history, so that for five hundred years the silver in every purse contradicted the name in every mouth.

The entry preserves one half of the atlas's cleanest natural experiment in naming. From 762 the same walls bore two names, an official one backed by the caliph, the coinage, and scripture itself, and a village name backed by nothing but habit. The official name never displaced the other even in Baghdad's own streets, and when the histories came to be written, the historians wrote *Baghdād*. Constantinople ran the same experiment with the opposite result, the founder's name holding for a thousand years; the difference, perhaps, is that Constantine renamed a city while al-Manṣūr tried to rename ground. On the coins it was always the City of Peace; in every mouth it was Baghdad, and the mouths outlasted the mint.
