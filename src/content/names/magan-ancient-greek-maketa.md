The Greek *Máketa*, the great cape that Nearchus, bringing Alexander's fleet home from the Indus in 325 BCE, sighted jutting from the Arabian shore at the mouth of the Gulf, the promontory known today as Ras Musandam at the tip of the Oman peninsula. The name is preserved in Arrian's *Indica*, the account built on Nearchus's own log: the pilots point the headland out across the strait, name it Maketa, and note it as a source of the cinnamon trade to Assyria, and Nearchus refuses his officers' urging to steer for it, holding the fleet to the Persian coast. It is a sailor's landmark, not a country: one fixed point of Arabia as seen from a passing deck.

Its claim to this page is the name's shape. *Máketa* looks like *Maka* with a Greek ending, and *Maka* may be *Magan*; if both links hold, then the one time Greek eyes fell on the copper coast of the Bronze Age, the word they wrote down for it still carried the old name, and the chain *Magan* to *Maka* to *Máketa* runs unbroken from Naram-Sin's war to Alexander's fleet. Neither link is secure, which is why the label on this entry reads as it does, and the geography invites caution as much as romance, since a cape is not a province. But the possibility is the point: on the strength of two syllables, a Macedonian sailing log may be the last ancient text to name Magan while still pointing, however incidentally, at the actual place.
