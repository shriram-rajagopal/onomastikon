The second Geʿez name for Egypt, *Mǝsr*, standing beside the older *Gǝbṣ* of this page: where *Gǝbṣ* came with the Bible through Greek, *Mǝsr* is the Semitic family name, entering Ethiopian usage in the Islamic centuries when Egypt, as the seat of the Coptic patriarchate that consecrated Ethiopia's own bishops, was a constant presence in the church's affairs and documents. The two names divided the work much as the sources divided the country: *Gǝbṣ* is Egypt in scripture, *Mǝsr* is Egypt across the water, the living land one corresponded with.

*Mǝsr* makes Geʿez the thirteenth language of the *mṣr* family and its southernmost member, and it closes a loop the family began four thousand kilometers away and three thousand years earlier: a Semitic root for "frontier" coined in the Bronze Age Levant, carried down Arabia's far side and across the strait, arriving in the Ethiopian highlands with the name's shape still intact. Geʿez is the rare language that holds both of Egypt's great exonym traditions at once, the Greek line and the Semitic line, filed under different registers of one civilization's attention. Egypt is the atlas's most-named entity in part because every route to it worked; the Geʿez page is the only place where two of the great routes end side by side.
