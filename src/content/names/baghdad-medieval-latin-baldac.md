The medieval Latin name of Baghdad, *Baldac*, with the manuscript variants *Baldach* and *Baldacum*, taken from the Arabic *Baghdād* through the mouths of Italian merchants, whose vernacular made the city *Baldacco*. The form runs through the travel literature of the later thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, Odoric of Pordenone's *Relatio* and the Latin versions of Marco Polo among them, the writers locating in *Baldac* the caliph, the schools, and the silk workshops of the Abbasid capital that the Mongols had taken in 1258.

The name's real monument is not in any chronicle. The brocaded silk the city exported was called in medieval Latin *baldakinus*, "Baghdad cloth," and because such cloth was hung on poles and carried over kings and relics, the fabric's name slid onto the canopy itself: the *baldacchino* that stands over the high altar of St Peter's is, philologically, a bolt of Baghdad silk. Of all the names on this page, *Baldac* had the strangest afterlife, outliving its own reference: Europe mostly forgot that its word for the canopy of honor was the name of the caliphs' city, and the name of Baghdad has hovered, unrecognized, over the altars of Christendom ever since.
