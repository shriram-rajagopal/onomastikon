*Táxila* is the Greek name of the city, fixed by the historians of Alexander's expedition, which crossed the Indus and rested there in 326 BCE. Arrian calls it the greatest city between the Indus and the Hydaspes; Strabo remembers its good laws and the generosity of its king, whom the Greeks called *Taxíles* after his own capital, as though the city had lent the man its name. The form clips the four syllables of the Indian name to three and neuters it into a Greek plural, the treatment of a name learned by ear on the march.

Among the family's forms the Greek is the outlier in what it discards: the entire meaningful superstructure. Sanskrit and Pali speakers heard "stone" in the name and told stories about the first element; the Greeks heard nothing at all and transmitted a pure label, indifferent to legend. That indifference proved durable, for it is the Greek shape, passed through Latin, that modern archaeology adopted, and the site is excavated and visited today as Taxila, a soldiers' abbreviation now more current than the name it abbreviated.
