The Greek *Oûnnoi*, the eastern empire's name for the people on its Danube, and the form under which the Huns received their one intimate portrait. Priscus of Panium traveled with the embassy of 449 to Attila's own encampment and wrote down what he saw: the wooden palace, the protocol of the feast, the king's studied plainness among gold-decked lieutenants, and a Greek merchant gone native who lectured the historian on the empire's failings. A second-century *Choûnoi* in Ptolemy is sometimes claimed as the name's first flicker, but the identification is too uncertain to build on, and the working history of the Greek form begins with the Hunnic crisis itself.

Where the Latin entry beside this one preserves terror, the Greek preserves acquaintance. Priscus is the only classical author who met Huns as hosts rather than as rumors, and his fragments are the reason anything humane is known about them at all: diplomacy, not war, produced the record. The pairing is the page's lesson in how names take their color, the same people in the same decade rendered as monsters by a historian who saw them across a battlefield's distance and as canny negotiators by one who drank at their table. The name a people gets depends on the door through which its recorder arrived.
